2.4

Light and the quantized atom

Every color in a fireworks show was chosen by a chemist. Strontium compounds produce red, barium compounds green, sodium compounds yellow and copper compounds blue. The fuel and the explosion are much the same in every shell. Where, then, do the different colors come from, and why does each metal always produce the same one?Chemists pick the colors in fireworks. Strontium gives red. Barium gives green. Sodium gives yellow. Copper gives blue. The fuel is about the same in every shell. So why does each metal always give its own color?

How does the light from an element reveal what is happening inside its atoms?

§2.4.1

Light is more than you can see

The light from a lamp, the signal to your phone, the heat you feel from a campfire and the X-ray at a dentist are all forms of . It is energy that travels as a wave of electric and magnetic fields. It needs no air to travel, so it can cross the empty space between the Sun and Earth. In a , all types travel at the same speed, the : c = 2.998 × 108 m/s.Lamp light, phone signals, the heat from a fire and X-rays are all the same kind of energy. They are electromagnetic radiation, energy that moves as a wave. It can travel through empty space. In empty space, all of it moves at the speed of light: 2.998 × 108 m/s.

The types differ in (λ, the Greek letter lambda), the distance from one wave crest to the next. Arranged by wavelength, they form the . From longest to shortest wavelength, the regions are radio waves, microwaves, , visible light, , X-rays and gamma rays.These types differ in wavelength, the distance from one wave top to the next. Line them up by wavelength and you get the electromagnetic spectrum. Longest to shortest: radio, microwaves, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays.

§2.4.2

Wavelength and frequency

A wave's (f) is the number of crests that pass a point each second. Its unit is the : 1 Hz = 1 wave per second, written 1 s−1. Radio stations are identified by frequency: KUT in Austin broadcasts at 90.5 MHz, which is 9.05 × 107 waves each second.Frequency is how many wave tops pass a point each second. Its unit is the hertz (Hz), one wave per second. Radio stations use it as their name: KUT in Austin is at 90.5 MHz, or 9.05 × 107 waves per second.

Wavelength, frequency and speed are related by one equation: c = λf. Because c is the same for all light, λ and f have an : double the wavelength and the frequency halves. Long radio waves have low frequencies; short X-rays have very high ones.The equation is c = λf. The speed c never changes. Wavelength and frequency therefore have an inverse relationship. Double one, and the other halves.

c = λf   so   f = c ÷ λ   and   λ = c ÷ f

c is in meters per second, so λ must be in meters. Convert nanometers by multiplying by 10−9: 656.3 nm = 656.3 × 10−9 m = 6.563 × 10−7 m. Forgetting this step makes the answer a billion times too small.Always change nanometers to meters first. Multiply by 10−9. So 656.3 nm becomes 6.563 × 10−7 m.

Key idea

c = λf. Wavelength and frequency are inversely related, and λ must be in meters.

§2.4.3

Photons and energy

Light behaves like a wave, but it also delivers energy in separate packets called . In 1900 Max Planck proposed that energy is exchanged only in such packets, and in 1905 Albert Einstein proposed that light itself travels in these packets, which explained how light ejects electrons from metals. The energy of one photon depends only on its frequency:Light acts like a wave. It also carries energy in small packets called photons. Planck (1900) and Einstein (1905) showed this. One photon's energy depends only on its frequency:

E = hf   with   h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s

h is . E and f have a : double the frequency and the photon's energy doubles. Combined with c = λf, this means that a longer wavelength gives a lower frequency and less energy per photon. Red photons carry less energy than violet ones, and ultraviolet photons carry more still. That is why ultraviolet light can cause sunburn while even bright red light cannot.h is Planck's constant. E and f have a direct relationship: higher f, more energy. So a longer wavelength means less energy. Red photons have less energy than violet ones. Ultraviolet photons have even more. That is why UV light can burn your skin.

Brightness is a separate property. A brighter lamp emits more photons each second, but each photon keeps the same energy. Ten thousand red photons cannot substitute for one ultraviolet photon, because each photon acts alone.Brighter light means more photons, not stronger ones. Each photon still has the same energy. Many red photons cannot do what one UV photon does.

Think of it this way: a vending machine that takes only one coin at a time and sells a snack for one dollar. A dollar coin works; a pile of pennies does not, however many you have. Photons are the coins. The analogy has a limit: photon energies are not restricted to a few coin values.

Honors · Energy from wavelength in one step

Substitute f = c ÷ λ into E = hf to get E = hc ÷ λ. For hydrogen's red line:

E = (6.626 × 10−34 J·s)(2.998 × 108 m/s) ÷ 6.563 × 10−7 m = 3.03 × 10−19 J

The answer matches the two-step result. The one-step method avoids rounding the frequency partway through.

Key idea

E = hf. Higher frequency, shorter wavelength, more energy per photon. Brightness reflects the number of photons; it does not change their energy.

Flame tests and spectroscopes

In a , a chemist dips a clean wire loop into a metal salt and holds it in a burner flame. The flame takes on a color that depends on the metal. The heat supplies energy to the metal's ; as they release that energy, they emit light of particular wavelengths.In a flame test, you put a little metal salt on a wire and hold it in a flame. The flame changes color. Heat gives energy to the metal's electrons. They give it back as light of certain colors.

Three kinds of spectra

A flame color is a mixture of wavelengths. To observe them separately, chemists use a , an instrument with a slit and a prism or grating that spreads light into its wavelengths. Through a spectroscope, light shows one of three patterns.A spectroscope splits light into its separate colors. With it, light shows one of three patterns.

A contains every wavelength. A hot gas at low pressure gives an of bright lines. When white light passes through a cooler gas, the gas absorbs the same wavelengths it would emit, leaving dark gaps: an . The two spectra look opposite, but their lines are at the same wavelengths.A continuous spectrum has every color. A thin glowing gas makes bright lines: an emission spectrum. A cooler gas in front of white light absorbs the same colors and leaves dark lines: an absorption spectrum. The lines are in the same places.

§2.4.5

Lines reveal energy levels

In §2.1.5 you studied Bohr's idea: an electron in an atom can have only certain energies, called . The lowest is the . Heat or an electric current can raise an electron to a higher level, an . When the electron drops back, the atom emits one photon whose energy equals the difference between the two levels:In §2.1.5 you learned that electrons have fixed energy levels. The lowest is the ground state. Energy can push an electron up to an excited state. When it falls back, the atom gives off one photon. The photon's energy equals the size of the drop:

Ephoton = Eupper − Elower = hf

If any energy were allowed, glowing hydrogen would give every wavelength, a full rainbow. It gives only a few separate lines, four of them bright. Only certain drops are possible, so the atom's energy is . The is the evidence.If electrons could have any energy, hydrogen would make every color. It makes only a few lines, four of them bright. So only certain energies are allowed. Energy is quantized, and the lines are the evidence.

§2.4.6

Spectral fingerprints

Each element has its own set of energy levels, so each has its own pattern of lines, as unique as a fingerprint. Matching the pattern identifies the element, even at a great distance.Each element has its own energy levels. So each one has its own pattern of lines, like a fingerprint. Match the pattern and you know the element.

  • Fireworks. Strontium compounds produce red, barium green, copper blue and sodium yellow. Lithium and strontium flames both look red, but a spectroscope distinguishes them: lithium has a strong line at 670.8 nm.Fireworks. Strontium gives red, barium green, copper blue, sodium yellow. Lithium and strontium both look red. A spectroscope shows lithium's line at 670.8 nm.
  • Lamps. Older low-pressure sodium lamps glow almost entirely at 589.0 and 589.6 nm, a single yellow color. Fluorescent lamps contain mercury, whose lines at 404.7, 435.8, 546.1, 577.0 and 579.1 nm are visible through a spectroscope.Lamps. Old sodium lamps glow almost only at 589.0 and 589.6 nm, pure yellow. Fluorescent lamps contain mercury, with lines at 404.7, 435.8, 546.1, 577.0 and 579.1 nm.
  • Signs. A true neon sign glows red-orange from dozens of neon lines between about 540 and 700 nm. Signs of other colors use other gases or coatings.Signs. Real neon glows red-orange from many lines. Other sign colors use other gases or coatings.
  • Stars. The Sun's spectrum is a rainbow crossed by dark lines. In 1859 Gustav Kirchhoff matched two of them to sodium's 589.0 and 589.6 nm lines, and hydrogen's lines also appear. Starlight reveals what stars are made of.Stars. Sunlight has dark lines. In 1859 Kirchhoff showed two belong to sodium. Hydrogen's lines are there too. That is how we know what stars contain.

The method also revealed new elements. In 1860 Robert Bunsen and Kirchhoff observed two unknown blue lines (455.5 and 459.3 nm) in the spectrum of mineral water from Dürkheim, Germany, and named the new metal cesium, from the Latin for sky blue. In 1868 a yellow line at 587.6 nm in light from the Sun matched no known element; it was helium, found on Earth only in 1895.This method found new elements. In 1860, Bunsen and Kirchhoff saw two new blue lines in mineral water and named the metal cesium, Latin for sky blue. In 1868, a new yellow line in sunlight (587.6 nm) led to the discovery of helium.
