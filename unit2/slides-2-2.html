        Chadwick, 1932 · Textbook §2.2.1

          Discovery of the neutron

          The puzzle

          Known by 1920: the electron (1897) and the nucleus (1911, Lesson 2.1) and the proton (1920). A helium nucleus has charge +2 (2 protons), but about 4 times a proton's mass.

          Chadwick fired alpha particles at beryllium. A radiation came out that no field could bend.

          Conclusion: the nucleus also holds neutrons: no charge, mass about equal to a proton's. Like a billiard ball, only a particle of similar mass could knock protons out so fast.

          1. Puzzle · 2 min. Recall 2.1 in one sentence each: electron 1897, nucleus 1911. Ask: if helium has 2 protons, what makes up the other 2 units of mass? (Something with mass but no charge.)
          2. Experiment · 2 min. Polonium was the alpha source. The unknown radiation passed through fields unbent and through thick materials. On striking wax (rich in hydrogen, whose nuclei are single protons), it knocked protons out at high speed.
          3. Charge · 1 min. Unbent means neutral. Misconception: "fast electrons." Fields bend electrons at any speed. The rival idea was energetic gamma rays; Chadwick showed they could not give the protons that much energy.
          4. Conclude · 1 min. Chadwick's letter "Possible Existence of a Neutron" appeared in Nature in 1932. Nobel Prize in Physics, 1935. SEP 3(A): explanations from evidence.

            Locations and scale · Textbook §2.2.2

              The nucleus and the electron cloud

              Protons and neutrons in the nucleus; electrons in the cloud. Then switch to True scale.

              True scale

              The nucleus is only 1/10,000 to 1/100,000 of the atom's diameter, yet it holds more than 99.9% of the mass.

              Think of it this way: atom = football stadium, nucleus = a marble at the center. Limit: an atom has no walls; its edge is a fuzzy cloud.

              1. Build · 2 min. Start with helium-4 (2, 2, 2). Electrons are dots in a cloud, not on rings: 2.1 replaced fixed orbits. Watch for students drawing circles around the nucleus.
              2. True scale · 1 min. Click True scale: the nucleus vanishes. "Mass in nucleus" stays above 99.9%. Return to prediction 3.
              3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: large volume means large mass.
              4. Analogy · 1 min. Ask students to state the limit of the analogy themselves.

              Atomic number · Textbook §2.2.3

                Atomic number

                Start with carbon-12. Add a proton, then take it away. Add a neutron. Remove an electron.

                Atomic number Z = number of protons. It names the element.

                Mass number A = protons + neutrons. Neutrons change A, not the element.

                Electrons change the charge, not the element. Neutral atom: electrons = protons.

                1. Protons · 2 min. Add a proton: carbon becomes nitrogen. Remove it: carbon again. The periodic table is in order of Z.
                2. Neutrons · 1 min. Add a neutron: still carbon, now carbon-13. Misconception to name aloud: "changing neutrons changes the element." Return to prediction 1.
                3. Electrons · 1 min. Remove an electron: still carbon, now charged. Preview of slide 13.
                4. Check · 1 min. Expected: carbon-13.

                  Counting protons, neutrons, electrons

                  Watch · fluorine-19

                  Fluorine has atomic number 9. This atom has mass number 19.

                  Protons= Z = 9

                  NeutronsA − Z = 19 − 9 = 10

                  Electronsneutral, so = protons = 9

                  Z comes from the periodic table. A comes from the name: fluorine-19.

                  Your turn · in your notebook

                  Aluminum has atomic number 13. An aluminum atom has mass number 27. Find its protons, neutrons and electrons.

                  Tool: neutrons = A − Z.

                  Check

                  13 protons, 27 − 13 = 14 neutrons, 13 electrons.

                  1. Watch · 2 min. Model the three steps aloud. Key move: the mass number already includes the protons, so subtract.
                  2. Your turn · 2 min. Circulate. Common errors: using A as the neutron count, or adding A + Z (40).
                  3. Check · 1 min. Extension: 26 protons and A = 56 gives 30 neutrons; 26 protons is iron.

                  Isotopes · Textbook §2.2.4

                    Isotopes

                    carbon-12

                    6 protons · 6 neutrons · 6 electrons

                    stable, about 99% of carbon

                    carbon-13

                    6 protons · 7 neutrons · 6 electrons

                    stable, about 1% of carbon

                    carbon-14

                    6 protons · 8 neutrons · 6 electrons

                    radioactive, a tiny trace

                    Isotopes

                    Atoms of the same element (same protons) with different numbers of neutrons, so different mass numbers. Same electrons, so they react the same way.

                    How we know

                    1913: Thomson found neon atoms of two masses, neon-20 and neon-22. Soddy named them isotopes. Dalton's "all atoms of an element are identical" had to change.

                    1. Compare · 2 min. What is the same? (Protons, electrons, element.) What differs? (Neutrons, mass number.) The percentages are for context; how they combine into an average is Lesson 2.3.
                    2. Define · 1 min. Students write the definition. Misconception: isotopes are "radioactive versions." Two of the three carbon isotopes here are stable.
                    3. History · 1 min. Links back to Dalton's postulate 2 in Lesson 2.1: evidence revised the model again.

                    Isotope notation · Textbook §2.2.4

                      Reading isotope notation

                      Pick an isotope. Count its particles, then check.

                      top: mass number A = protons + neutrons

                      bottom: atomic number Z = protons

                      hyphen name: carbon-14 or C-14

                      1. Read · 1 min. Mnemonic: the larger number (A) sits on top. The bottom number repeats what the symbol already tells you, so it is often left off.
                      2. Decode · 3 min. Students call out each count before you check. Try sodium-23 and chlorine-37. The decoder names the common error, such as using A as the neutron count.
                      3. Check · 1 min. Tc-99m: the m changes no counts. 99 − 43 = 56.

                      Isotopes at work · Textbook §2.2.4

                        Uses of isotopes

                        Iodine-13153 p · 78 n

                        The thyroid collects iodine and cannot tell isotopes apart. I-131 gathers there, and its radiation destroys overactive or cancerous cells.

                        Technetium-99m43 p · 56 n

                        Used in about 80% of nuclear-medicine scans. A camera outside the body detects its gamma rays to image bones, the heart and other organs.

                        Carbon-146 p · 8 n

                        Every living thing takes in a little C-14 with ordinary carbon, because it reacts the same way. Scientists use it to date old wood and bone (a later unit).

                        Same chemistry, different nucleus. Isotopes of an element have the same protons and electrons, so the body handles them the same way. Only the nucleus differs: its neutrons, and whether it is stable or radioactive.

                        1. Iodine-131 · 2 min. Same 53 protons as iodine-127 in salt, so the body treats it as iodine: chemistry depends on electrons, not neutrons. Answers slide 2.
                        2. Technetium-99m · 1 min. The m means metastable: the nucleus holds extra energy. It is used in tens of millions of scans a year worldwide.
                        3. Carbon-14 · 1 min. Context only. Decay and half-life belong to the nuclear unit; do not teach them here.
                        4. Key idea · 1 min. Ask: why can a medicine "hide" among ordinary iodine atoms? (Same electrons, same chemistry.) End of period 1.

                        Ions · Textbook §2.2.5

                          How ions form

                          Sodium: remove one electron. Then choose chlorine and add one.

                          charge = protons − electrons

                          Lose electrons → positive ion, a cation (Na⁺).

                          Gain electrons → negative ion, an anion (Cl⁻).

                          Never protons. Adding a proton to sodium makes magnesium, not a sodium ion.

                          1. Remove · 2 min. Start of the second half-period. Sodium: 11 protons, 11 electrons, charge 0. Remove one: 11 − 10 = +1, Na⁺.
                          2. Add · 1 min. Chlorine: add one electron; 17 − 18 = −1, Cl⁻. Ask: why does gaining a particle make it negative? (The added electron carries negative charge.)
                          3. Protons stay · 1 min. Click "+" under Protons on purpose: the element changes. Misconception: ions form by gaining protons. Return to prediction 2.
                          4. Check · 1 min. Expected: 18.

                          Ions in your body · Textbook §2.2.5

                            Electrolytes

                            IonProtonsElectronsRole in the body
                            Na⁺ cation11nerve signals
                            K⁺ cation19nerve signals
                            Ca²⁺ cation20muscle contraction
                            Cl⁻ anion17water balance

                            electrons = protons − charge

                            Example: Mg²⁺ has 12 protons, so it has 12 − 2 = 10 electrons.

                            Your turn

                            Use the rule under the table to count each ion's electrons. Then check.

                            Sports drinks

                            Sweat carries Na⁺, K⁺ and Cl⁻ out of the body. Sports drinks replace sodium and potassium ions.

                            1. Read · 1 min. These four ions are electrolytes: ions dissolved in body fluids.
                            2. Count · 2 min. Students compute each electron count, then click Show. Ca²⁺: 20 − 2 = 18. Cl⁻: 17 − (−1) = 18.
                            3. Connect · 1 min. Why sodium loses one electron and calcium two is explained in Lesson 2.5. SEP 4(B): research and society.

                            Ions · Textbook §2.2.5 worked examples

                              Finding an ion's charge

                              Watch · magnesium

                              A magnesium atom (12 protons) loses 2 electrons.

                              Mg

                              12 p⁺ · 12 e⁻

                              Mg²⁺

                              12 p⁺ · 10 e⁻

                              Electrons12 − 2 = 10

                              Charge12 − 10 = +2

                              An oxygen atom (8 protons) gains 2 electrons. How many electrons, what charge, and what kind of ion?

                              O

                                            Answer the driving question

                                            What makes an atom the element it is, and how can atoms of one element differ? Write your answer in your notebook.

                                            Claim

                                            One sentence that answers the question.

                                            1. An atom's element is set by…
                                            2. Atoms of one element can differ in…

                                            Sample claim

                                            The number of protons determines the element, while atoms of one element can differ in their numbers of neutrons and electrons.

                                            Evidence

                                            Specific atoms and their counts.

                                            1. Iodine-127 and iodine-131 both have…
                                            2. Na and Na⁺ both have…, but…

                                            Sample evidence

                                            Iodine-127 and iodine-131 both have 53 protons but 74 and 78 neutrons. Na and Na⁺ both have 11 protons, but Na⁺ has 10 electrons instead of 11.

                                            Reasoning

                                            Why the counts lead to the claim.

                                            1. Because the protons did not change,…
                                            2. Different neutrons make…; different electrons make…

                                            Sample reasoning

                                            Because the protons did not change, each pair is still one element. Different neutrons make isotopes with different mass numbers; different electrons make ions with a charge.
