2.5

Arranging electrons

Sodium has 11 electrons and neon has 10. A difference of one electron separates a gas that reacts with nothing from a metal that must be stored under oil. The total number of electrons is not what matters most. What matters is how the electrons are arranged, and especially how many occupy the outermost level.Sodium has 11 electrons. Neon has 10. Neon does not react with anything. Sodium reacts with water and air. The difference is not the total number of electrons. It is how they are arranged, especially the outermost ones.

§2.5.1

Levels, sublevels and orbitals

Lesson 2.1 introduced the modern model of the atom: electrons occupy , regions where an electron is likely to be found. Orbitals are grouped by energy. Each main is numbered n = 1, 2, 3 and so on, and higher levels are farther from the nucleus on average and higher in energy.Electrons are found in orbitals. An orbital is a region where an electron is likely to be found. Orbitals are grouped into energy levels, numbered 1, 2, 3 and up. Higher levels have more energy and are farther from the nucleus.

Each energy level is split into named s, p, d and f. Level 1 has only an s sublevel. Level 2 has s and p, level 3 has s, p and d, and level 4 has all four. A sublevel is written with its level number first: 3p means the p sublevel of energy level 3.Each energy level has smaller groups called sublevels: s, p, d and f. Level 1 has only s. Level 2 has s and p. Level 3 has s, p and d. Level 4 has all four. "3p" means the p sublevel in level 3.

Key idea

Energy levels contain sublevels (s, p, d, f), and sublevels contain orbitals. Each orbital holds 2 electrons, so s holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10 and f holds 14.

An analogy: an atom is like a hotel. Energy levels are floors, sublevels are types of rooms on a floor, and each orbital is a room with exactly two beds. Floor 1 has a single room; floor 2 has one s room and three p rooms. The analogy has limits: electrons do not stay at fixed spots as hotel guests do, and the floors overlap in energy, so a room on floor 4 can be lower in energy than one on floor 3. The next section shows this overlap.An analogy: an atom is like a hotel. Energy levels are floors. Sublevels are room types. Each orbital is a room with two beds. The analogy has limits. Electrons do not stay in one spot. Also, some rooms on floor 4 are lower in energy than rooms on floor 3.

Extension: a whole level n holds 2n2 electrons (2, 8, 18, 32). Only the sublevel capacities are required.

§2.5.2

The filling order

An lists which sublevels an atom's electrons occupy. Electrons fill the lowest-energy sublevels first, the way water fills a container from the bottom. Each sublevel is written with a superscript for the number of electrons in it. Hydrogen is 1s1 and helium is 1s2; lithium's third electron must go into level 2, so lithium is 1s2 2s1.An electron configuration shows which sublevels hold an atom's electrons. Electrons fill the lowest-energy sublevels first. The small raised number is the number of electrons in that sublevel. Hydrogen is 1s1. Helium is 1s2. Lithium is 1s2 2s1.

The energy levels overlap. The 4s sublevel is lower in energy than 3d, so after 3p fills, the next two electrons go into 4s, and only then does 3d begin. The diagonal rule below gives the complete order: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p.Levels overlap in energy. 4s is lower in energy than 3d. So after 3p, electrons go into 4s first. Then 3d fills. Follow the arrows in the diagram to get the order: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p.

Key idea

Electrons fill sublevels from lowest to highest energy. The order is 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p: 4s fills before 3d.

§2.5.2

Reading the order from the table

You do not need to memorize the diagonal rule. The periodic table is arranged in the same order. Groups 1 and 2 form the s , groups 13 to 18 the p block, and groups 3 to 12 the d block. Reading across a period, left to right, gives the order in which sublevels fill. In period 4 that is 4s, then 3d, then 4p. The d block is always one level behind: in period 4 it is 3d.The periodic table follows the same order. Groups 1 and 2 are the s block. Groups 13 to 18 are the p block. Groups 3 to 12 are the d block. Read a period (row) from left to right to get the order. In period 4: 4s, then 3d, then 4p. The d block number is one less than the period number.

§2.5.3

Shortcuts and valence electrons

Full configurations can be long. A shortens them: write the symbol of the noble gas from the end of the previous period in square brackets, then only the electrons added after it. Sodium, 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s1, becomes [Ne] 3s1. The bracket stands for the 10 inner , arranged exactly as in a neon atom.A noble-gas configuration is a shorter form. Write the noble gas before the element in brackets. Then write only the electrons after it. Sodium becomes [Ne] 3s1. [Ne] stands for the 10 inner electrons, the core electrons.

The electrons in the highest occupied energy level are the . They are the outermost electrons, the ones involved when atoms react. Sulfur is [Ne] 3s2 3p4: its highest level is 3, which holds 2 + 4 = 6 electrons, so sulfur has 6 valence electrons.Valence electrons are the electrons in the highest energy level. They are the outermost electrons. They take part in reactions. Sulfur is [Ne] 3s2 3p4. Level 3 has 2 + 4 = 6 electrons. So sulfur has 6 valence electrons.

For (groups 1, 2 and 13 to 18) the periodic table gives the count directly. Groups 1 and 2 have 1 and 2 valence electrons. For groups 13 to 18, subtract 10: chlorine, in group 17, has 7. Every element in a has the same number, which is why the elements of a group behave as a chemical family.For representative elements, you can also use the group number. Group 1 has 1 valence electron. Group 2 has 2. For groups 13 to 18, subtract 10. Chlorine is in group 17, so it has 7. All elements in a group have the same number.

Helium is the exception to the group rule. It is placed in group 18, but it has only 2 electrons in total (1s2). Two electrons fill its first level, so helium behaves like the other noble gases. The other noble gases have 8 valence electrons, a full s and p set called an .Helium is the exception. It is in group 18 but has only 2 electrons. These 2 fill its only level. So helium behaves like the other noble gases. The other noble gases have 8 valence electrons, called an octet.

Key idea

Valence electrons are those in the highest energy level. For representative elements: group 1 has 1, group 2 has 2, groups 13 to 18 have the group number minus 10. Helium has 2.

§2.5.4

Lewis dot structures

A is a model that shows only an atom's valence electrons, as dots around its symbol. The symbol stands for the nucleus and all the core electrons. Chemists use it because valence electrons are the ones that take part in reactions, so the dots show at a glance the electrons available for reactions.A Lewis dot structure shows only the valence electrons. They are drawn as dots around the element's symbol. The symbol stands for the nucleus and the core electrons. The dots show the electrons that take part in reactions.

The drawing convention has three steps. (1) Find the number of valence electrons. (2) Place one dot on each of the four sides of the symbol before you pair any. (3) Pair the remaining dots, up to two per side, for at most 8. Which side you start on does not matter. Helium is drawn with its 2 dots as a pair, because its 2 electrons fill its only level, the way 8 fill the outer level of the other noble gases.To draw one: (1) Count the valence electrons. (2) Put one dot on each of the four sides first. (3) Then pair them up. A side can hold 2 dots, so the most is 8. You can start on any side. Helium is the exception: its 2 electrons fill its only level, so they are drawn as a pair.

§2.5.5

Metals that lose electrons

An atom whose outer level is full, like neon, is very stable. Atoms with only one or two valence electrons can reach that same arrangement by losing them, and atoms one or two short can reach it by gaining electrons. This single idea predicts the chemistry of whole families. You will often hear that an atom "wants" a full outer level. That is shorthand: atoms do not want anything, but a full outer level is a low-energy, stable arrangement.A full outer level is very stable. Atoms with 1 or 2 valence electrons can lose them to reach a full level. Atoms that are 1 or 2 short can gain electrons. People often say atoms "want" a full level. This is only shorthand. Atoms do not want anything. A full level is simply a stable arrangement.

(group 1) have 1 valence electron, which they lose easily to form 1+ ions such as Na+ and K+. They are the most reactive metals. Sodium and potassium react with water, releasing hydrogen gas and leaving a basic solution; because they also react with air, both are stored under oil. Potassium reacts more violently than sodium; the hydrogen can ignite, and potassium colors the flame lilac. Hydrogen is also placed in group 1, but it is a nonmetal gas, not an alkali metal.Alkali metals are in group 1. They have 1 valence electron. They lose it easily and form 1+ ions, like Na+. They are very reactive. Sodium and potassium react with water and give off hydrogen gas. They also react with air, so they are kept under oil. Hydrogen is in group 1 but is not an alkali metal.

(group 2) have 2 valence electrons and lose both, forming 2+ ions such as Mg2+ and Ca2+. They are reactive, but less so than the group 1 metal beside them: calcium reacts steadily with water, while potassium reacts violently. Magnesium burns with a brilliant white light, which is why it is used in flares and fireworks.Alkaline earth metals are in group 2. They have 2 valence electrons. They lose both and form 2+ ions, like Mg2+. They are reactive, but less reactive than group 1 metals. Magnesium burns with a bright white light. It is used in flares and fireworks.

Key idea

Group 1: 1 valence electron, 1+ ions, very reactive. Group 2: 2 valence electrons, 2+ ions, reactive but less than group 1.

§2.5.5

Gaining electrons, or none at all

(group 17) have 7 valence electrons, one short of an octet. They gain one electron to form 1− ions such as Cl−, which is the chloride ion in table salt, NaCl. Halogens are very reactive nonmetals, and fluorine is the most reactive nonmetal of all. As elements they exist as : at room temperature F2 and Cl2 are gases, Br2 is a red-brown liquid and I2 is a gray-black solid. Chlorine compounds disinfect swimming pools and drinking water.Halogens are in group 17. They have 7 valence electrons. They gain 1 electron and form 1− ions, like Cl− in table salt. They are very reactive. Their atoms come in pairs, as diatomic molecules like Cl2. Chlorine compounds kill germs in pools.

(group 18) already have a full outer level: 8 valence electrons, or 2 for helium. They form no ions in ordinary chemistry and are almost unreactive. This lack of reactivity makes them useful. Neon glows red-orange in signs without reacting with the tube, argon fills incandescent light bulbs so the hot filament does not burn, and helium fills balloons because it is less dense than air and cannot burn. Liquid helium, at about −269 °C, cools the magnets in MRI scanners. Xenon and krypton form some compounds, made only under special conditions.Noble gases are in group 18. Their outer level is full: 8 valence electrons, or 2 for helium. They almost never react. Neon glows in signs. Argon fills light bulbs. Helium fills balloons and, as a very cold liquid, cools MRI machines.

Key idea

Group 17: 7 valence electrons, gain 1 to form 1− ions, very reactive diatomic nonmetals. Group 18: full outer level, almost unreactive.

Notice how the prediction works: count the valence electrons, ask whether losing or gaining is the shorter route to a full level, and the charge follows. Why reactivity also changes down a group (potassium is more reactive than sodium, but fluorine is more reactive than chlorine) is a periodic trend explained in Unit 3.To predict: count the valence electrons. Is it easier to lose them or to gain more? That gives the charge. Unit 3 explains why reactivity changes down a group.

§2.5.6

Transition metals

The occupy groups 3 to 12, the d block. Across period 4, from scandium to zinc, each new electron goes into the 3d sublevel while 4s usually remains full. Iron, for example, is [Ar] 4s2 3d6. Its highest level, n = 4, holds only 2 electrons, so the group number 8 is not its number of valence electrons. The 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy, and different numbers of them can be lost.Transition metals are in groups 3 to 12, the d block. Their added electrons fill a d sublevel. Iron is [Ar] 4s2 3d6. Iron is in group 8, but it does not have 8 valence electrons. Its 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy, so it can lose different numbers of them.

This closeness in energy explains why most transition metals form ions with more than one charge: iron forms Fe2+ and Fe3+, and copper forms Cu+ and Cu2+. Transition metals are also generally less reactive than group 1 and 2 metals. Copper does not react with water, so it is used for water pipes; it conducts electricity well, so it is used for wiring. Many of their compounds are brightly colored, which makes them important pigments.Because of this, most transition metals can form more than one ion. Iron forms Fe2+ and Fe3+. Copper forms Cu+ and Cu2+. They are less reactive than group 1 and 2 metals. Copper does not react with water, so it is used for pipes. It carries electricity well, so it is used for wires. Many of their compounds are brightly colored.

Key idea

In transition metals, a d sublevel is being filled. Their group number does not give valence electrons, they often form ions with more than one charge, they are less reactive than groups 1 and 2, and many of their compounds are colored.

Because transition metals do not follow a simple valence pattern, they are not drawn with Lewis dot structures in this course. The name chromium gives a clue to the family: it comes from the Greek word for color, because chromium compounds have so many different colors.Lewis dot structures are not drawn for transition metals in this course. Honors · Where the filling order breaks down

The filling order predicts chromium (Z = 24) as [Ar] 4s2 3d4. Measurements show [Ar] 4s1 3d5, often written [Ar] 3d5 4s1. Copper (Z = 29) is predicted as [Ar] 4s2 3d9 but is measured as [Ar] 4s1 3d10, often written [Ar] 3d10 4s1.

A half-filled (d5) or full (d10) d sublevel is especially stable, and 4s and 3d are so close in energy that moving one electron to 3d lowers the atom's energy. The orbital board shows both exceptions when you reach Z = 24 and Z = 29.

Once the 3d sublevel holds electrons, it is slightly lower in energy than 4s, so a transition metal loses its 4s electrons first when it forms an ion. Iron, [Ar] 4s2 3d6, forms Fe2+, [Ar] 3d6.
