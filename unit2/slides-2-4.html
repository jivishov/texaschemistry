Light and the quantized atom

Every element emits its own colors. Measured carefully, that light reveals how its electrons are arranged in energy.

        The spectrum · Textbook §2.4.1

          The electromagnetic spectrum

          Slide from radio waves to X-rays. Watch f and E.

          Longest λ to shortest λ

          radio · microwave · infrared · visible · ultraviolet · X-ray · gamma

          Visible light: about 380 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red). 1 nm = 10−9 m.

          All of it travels at c = 2.998 × 108 m/s.

          1. Explore · 3 min. Click FM radio, Microwave oven, TV remote, Hydrogen red, UV lamp, Dental X-ray in order. Ask: what happens to f and E each time? (Both rise as λ shrinks.)
          2. Order · 1 min. Mnemonic optional. Stress that radio waves and X-rays are light too. Misconception to watch: "light means only what we can see."
          3. Check · 1 min. Sound is a vibration of air, not electromagnetic radiation. SEP 2(B).

          Wavelength and frequency · Textbook §2.4.2

            Wavelength and frequency

            Wavelength λ: crest to crest, in meters. Frequency f: crests per second, in hertz (Hz = s−1).

            c = λf

            c never changes, so λ and f are inversely related: double λ and f halves.

            f = c ÷ λ  ·  λ = c ÷ f

            Convert first

            656.3 nm × 10−9 = 6.563 × 10−7 m

            c is in m/s, so λ must be in meters.

            1. Define · 2 min. Both waves move at the same speed. In one second the short wave sends more crests past a point.
            2. Relate · 1 min. Ask: if λ triples, what happens to f? (It becomes one third.)
            3. Convert · 1 min. Most common error: substituting 656.3 as meters, which makes the answer a billion times too small.

            Wavelength and frequency · Textbook §2.4.2 worked examples

              From wavelength to frequency

              Watch · hydrogen's red line, 656.3 nm

              Convert6.563 × 10−7 m

              Rearrangef = c ÷ λ

              Solve2.998 × 108 ÷ 6.563 × 10−7 = 4.57 × 1014 Hz

              Your turn · in your notebook

              A sodium street lamp glows at 589.0 nm. Find its frequency.

              Check

              5.890 × 10−7 m, so f = 5.09 × 1014 Hz: higher than red, because 589.0 nm is shorter.

              1. Watch · 2 min. Model the three steps aloud. Calculator tip: use the EE or ×10x key, not "× 10 ^".
              2. Your turn · 2 min. Circulate. Check the conversion step first.
              3. Check · 1 min. Ask: shorter wavelength, so is f higher or lower? (Higher.)
              4. Radio · 1 min. 90.5 MHz = 9.05 × 107 Hz. λ = 3.31 m. Wrong answers come from f ÷ c or from forgetting "mega." SEP 2(C).

              Photons · Textbook §2.4.3

                Photons and energy

                E = hf

                h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s (Planck's constant)

                Light delivers energy in packets called photons. Double f and each photon's energy doubles: a direct relationship.

                Brightness and energy

                A brighter lamp sends more photons, each with the same energy. Many red photons cannot replace one UV photon.

                One photon of each

                1. Packets · 2 min. Planck (1900) and Einstein (1905). A photon is the smallest amount of light energy at a given frequency.
                2. Compare · 2 min. Red 3.03, violet 4.84, UV 7.82 (× 10−19 J). This answers prediction 1: violet carries more.
                3. Brightness · 1 min. Analogy: a vending machine that takes one coin at a time. A pile of pennies cannot buy a one-dollar snack. Limit: photon energies are not limited to a few coin values.
                4. Check · 1 min. Misconception to watch: longer wavelength means more energy.

                Photons · Textbook §2.4.3 worked examples

                  Calculating photon energy

                  Watch · the red photon, f = 4.57 × 1014 Hz

                  EquationE = hf

                  Substitute6.626 × 10−34 J·s × 4.57 × 1014 Hz

                  Solve3.03 × 10−19 J

                  Powers of ten: −34 + 14 = −20, then 30.3 × 10−20 = 3.03 × 10−19.

                  Your turn · in your notebook

                  Hydrogen's violet line (410.2 nm) has f = 7.31 × 1014 Hz. Find E.

                  Check

                  E = 4.84 × 10−19 J, more than red's 3.03 × 10−19 J.

                  Honors · one step

                  E = hc ÷ λ. A 254 nm UV lamp: (6.626 × 10−34)(2.998 × 108) ÷ 2.54 × 10−7 = 7.82 × 10−19 J

                  1. Watch · 2 min. Model it. Show the power-of-ten bookkeeping.
                  2. Your turn · 2 min. Students work in notebooks.
                  3. Check · 1 min. Shorter wavelength, higher frequency, more energy.
                  4. Honors · 2 min. Substituting f = c ÷ λ gives E = hc ÷ λ. One step avoids rounding f in the middle. Two-step answer: f = 1.18 × 1015 Hz, E = 7.82 × 10−19 J, the same.

                  Practice · Textbook §2.4.3

                    Practice: wavelength and energy

                    Type your source's λ or f and compare each step with yours.

                    In your notebook: f, E and region

                    A. TV remote, 940 nm

                    B. Hydrogen blue-green, 486.1 nm

                    C. Microwave oven, 2.45 × 109 Hz (find λ and E)

                    Period 1 exit question

                    Rank A, B and C from lowest to highest energy per photon. Explain using f.

                    1. Solve · 5 min. Answers: A f = 3.19 × 1014 Hz, E = 2.11 × 10−19 J, infrared. B f = 6.17 × 1014 Hz, E = 4.09 × 10−19 J, visible. C λ = 0.122 m, E = 1.62 × 10−24 J, microwave.
                    2. Compare · 3 min. Students type their own values. The calculator shows each step.
                    3. Exit · 2 min. C < A < B. The lowest f gives the lowest energy. End of period 1.

                    Investigation · Textbook §2.4.4

                      Flame tests

                      Hold each salt in the same flame. Then try the mystery salt.

                      What stays the same?

                      Same burner, same gas, every salt a chloride. Only the metal changes, and the color changes with it.

                      The color comes from the metal's electrons, not the fuel or the heat.

                      1. Test · 4 min. Students record a data table: salt, metal, color. Real lab colors: Li red, Na yellow-orange, K lilac, Ca orange-red, Sr red, Ba yellow-green, Cu green or blue-green. Safety for a live demo: goggles, wooden splints soaked in solution, no loose sleeves. SEP 1(B), 1(F).
                      2. Pattern · 1 min. This answers prediction 2. The heat only supplies the energy; the metal determines the color.
                      3. Unknown salt · 2 min. Li and Sr both look red. Switch to the spectroscope view: lithium shows one strong line at 670.8 nm, while strontium shows a group of red bands. Ca, Sr, Ba and Cu emit mostly through small molecules formed in the flame; the sim draws their main bands at approximate positions.
                      4. Check · 1 min.

                      Spectra · Textbook §2.4.4

                        Three kinds of spectra

                        Continuous · a glowing solid, such as a filament

                        Emission · glowing hydrogen gas: bright lines on black

                        Absorption · white light through cooler hydrogen: dark lines on a rainbow

                        1. Continuous · 1 min. A hot solid or dense gas emits every wavelength.
                        2. Emission · 1 min. Bright lines at 656.3, 486.1, 434.0 and 410.2 nm, the same four lines from §2.1.5.
                        3. Absorption · 1 min. Same positions, opposite look. Misconception to watch: "emission and absorption spectra look the same." Point at one line in each strip.
                        4. Check · 1 min.

                        Spectroscope · Textbook §2.4.4

                          Reading a spectroscope

                          Pick a lamp. Click a line to read its frequency and energy.

                          Measure: hydrogen's 486.1 nm line has f = 6.17 × 1014 Hz and E = 4.09 × 10−19 J.

                          Switch to Absorption: the dark lines lie at exactly the same wavelengths.

                          1. Lamps · 3 min. Show H (4 lines), Na (a yellow pair at 589.0 and 589.6 nm), Hg (5 lines) and Ne (many red-orange lines). SEP 2(B).
                          2. Measure · 1 min. Ask students to verify 486.1 nm with their calculator.
                          3. Switch · 1 min. A gas absorbs exactly the photons it can emit. Ask why; the next slide explains.

                          Energy levels · Textbook §2.4.5, links to §2.1.5

                            Energy levels and spectral lines

                            Recall Bohr (§2.1.5)

                            Electrons occupy fixed energy levels. Lowest: ground state. Higher: excited states.

                            Ephoton = Eupper − Elower

                            Ephoton = hf

                            Drop the electron to n = 2 from 3, 4, 5 and 6.

                            1. Recall · 1 min. Ask a student to explain the staircase from Lesson 2.1.
                            2. Drop · 3 min. 3→2: 3.03 × 10−19 J, red. 4→2: 4.09. 5→2: 4.58. 6→2: 4.84 (× 10−19 J). Then a drop to n = 1 (ultraviolet) and 4→3 (infrared). SEP 1(G).
                            3. Measure · 1 min. The calculated wavelengths (656, 486, 434, 410 nm) match the measured lines to within rounding.
                            4. Check · 1 min.

                            Quantized energy · Textbook §2.4.5

                              Evidence for quantized energy

                              If any energy were allowed

                              Every drop size would be possible, so glowing hydrogen would give every wavelength.

                              What hydrogen emits

                              Only a few separate lines (four bright ones), so only a few drops exist. The energy is quantized.

                              Think of it this way: you can stand on a step, not between steps. Unlike stairs, the levels get closer together higher up.

                              Honors

                              En = −2.18 × 10−18 J ÷ n2. E4 − E2 = 4.09 × 10−19 J → 486 nm.

                              1. Any energy · 1 min. This answers prediction 3.
                              2. Lines only · 1 min. Gaps in the spectrum are evidence of gaps between allowed energies.
                              3. Analogy · 1 min. Analogy limit: real levels crowd together near the top (see the ladder on slide 14).
                              4. Honors · 2 min. E4 = −1.36 × 10−19 J, E2 = −5.45 × 10−19 J. f = 6.17 × 1014 Hz, λ = 486 nm. Electron configurations, which describe how electrons fill levels, come in Lesson 2.5.

                              Fingerprints · Textbook §2.4.6

                                Identify the mystery lamp

                                Compare the mystery lamp with each element.

                                How to decide

                                Every mystery line needs a partner in the reference. One matching line is not enough: helium's 587.6 nm is close to sodium's 589.0 nm.

                                1. Compare · 3 min. Students vote before you click. Press Mystery again for a new lamp. SEP 2(B), 3(A).

                                3. Check · 1 min.

                                Fingerprints · Textbook §2.4.6

                                  Uses of line spectra

                                  Fireworks

                                  Strontium red, barium green, copper blue, sodium yellow. Lithium also looks red, but its 670.8 nm line identifies it.

                                  Lamps and signs

                                  Low-pressure sodium street lights: almost only 589.0 and 589.6 nm. Fluorescent lamps: mercury lines. True neon: red-orange.

                                  Stars

                                  Sunlight has dark absorption lines. In 1859 Kirchhoff matched two to sodium; hydrogen's lines also appear.

                                  New elements

                                  1860: two new blue lines (455.5, 459.3 nm) in mineral water: cesium, "sky blue." 1868: a new yellow line in sunlight, 587.6 nm: helium.

                                  1. Fireworks · 1 min. Simplified: the red, green and blue come mostly from molecules such as SrCl, BaCl and CuCl formed in the burst. Each metal still gives its own color.
                                  2. Lamps · 1 min. Many cities have replaced sodium lights with LEDs, which give a continuous-looking spectrum.
                                  3. Stars · 1 min. Absorption, not emission: the cooler outer gas of the Sun absorbs light from the hotter layers below.
                                  4. Discoveries · 1 min. Bunsen and Kirchhoff (cesium, 1860). Helium was seen in sunlight during the 1868 eclipse and found on Earth in 1895. Point to the Unit 2 case file.

                                  Putting it together

                                    Summary: how a line forms

                                    1 · Drop

                                    An electron drops from n = 4 to n = 2.

                                    ΔE = 4.09 × 10−19 J

                                    2 · Photon

                                    One photon carries that energy.

                                    f = E ÷ h = 6.17 × 1014 Hz

                                    3 · Line

                                    It appears as one line.

                                    λ = c ÷ f = 486 nm

                                    Not longer λ = more energy. Shorter λ, higher f, more energy.

                                    Not the fuel. The metal's electrons make the flame color.

                                    Not any energy. Lines mean fixed levels.

                                    1. Chain · 2 min. Run the chain forward, then backward: from a measured line, find the drop.
                                    2. Misconceptions · 1 min. Ask students which of these they believed before today.

                                              Wrap-up · Exit ticket

                                                Answer the driving question

                                                How does the light from an element reveal what is happening inside its atoms? Write your answer in your notebook.

                                                Word bank
                                                energy levelphotonemission spectrumwavelengthfrequencyquantizedE = hf

                                                Claim

                                                One sentence that answers the question.

                                                1. An element's light shows that its electrons…

                                                Sample claim

                                                An element's line spectrum shows that its electrons can have only certain energies, and the lines reveal the gaps between them.

                                                Evidence

                                                Data from a spectrum or a flame test.

                                                1. Hydrogen gives only…
                                                2. The red line at 656.3 nm has E =…

                                                Sample evidence

                                                Glowing hydrogen gives only separate lines, not a rainbow. The red line at 656.3 nm has f = 4.57 × 1014 Hz and E = 3.03 × 10−19 J.

                                                Reasoning

                                                Connect the data to energy levels.

                                                1. Each line's photon energy equals…
                                                2. Since only some lines appear,…

                                                Sample reasoning

                                                Each line is light of one photon energy, equal to the drop between two levels, here n = 3 to n = 2; each atom that makes this drop gives one photon, and many atoms together make the line. Only certain lines appear, so only certain drops are possible: the energy is quantized.

                                                Self-check: mark each part your answer meets, then compare with the sample.
