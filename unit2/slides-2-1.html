        Models and evidence · Textbook §2.1.1

          Scientific models and evidence

          A shape is sealed in the box. Probe it from the left and from the top.

          Choose the model that fits every probe.

          If two shapes fit your probes, you do not have enough evidence yet. Probe where the shapes would differ.

          Key idea

          A model is judged by evidence. When a reliable result does not fit, the model changes, as atomic models did.

          Scientific law

          Describes a pattern: what always happens. Mass is conserved in reactions.

          Scientific theory

          Explains why the pattern happens. Atomic theory explains why mass is conserved.

          1. Probe · 2 min. Invite students to call out where to aim. Click just left of or above the box to aim a probe.
          2. Decide · 1 min. Vote before choosing. With few probes, two shapes can fit: that is the point. Collect more evidence, then decide.
          3. Reflect · 1 min. Ask: how is this like studying atoms? (No one sees inside; models come from indirect probes.)
          4. Law or theory · 1 min. A theory never "becomes" a law. Laws describe; theories explain. SEP 1(H).

          Dalton · Textbook §2.1.2

            Three laws from mass data

            Analyze each sample. Compare the three water samples, then hydrogen peroxide.

            Three mass laws, about 1780 to 1803

            Conservation of mass (Lavoisier): total mass is unchanged by a reaction.

            Definite proportions (Proust): a compound has fixed proportions by mass.

            Multiple proportions (Dalton): masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other form small whole-number ratios.

            1. Analyze · 3 min. Click Analyze on each row and talk through one calculation: 2.80 ÷ 25.0 × 100 = 11.2%. All water is 11.2% hydrogen by mass.
            2. Compare · 1 min. Peroxide is a different compound with its own fixed makeup. Per gram of hydrogen it has twice the oxygen: 15.83 ÷ 7.93 = 2.0.
            3. Three laws · 2 min. Students record the laws. Ask: what kind of matter would produce whole-number ratios? (Countable pieces: atoms.)

            Dalton · Textbook §2.1.2

              Dalton's postulates (1808)

              1. All matter is made of tiny, indivisible particles called atoms.
              2. Atoms of the same element are identical; atoms of different elements differ. → definite proportions
              3. Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios. → definite and multiple proportions
              4. In reactions, atoms are rearranged, never created or destroyed. → conservation of mass

              What later changed

              Atoms contain smaller particles (slide 9), and atoms of one element can differ in mass (isotopes, Lesson 2.2). Postulate 4 still holds whenever you balance an equation.

              Build Dalton's atom: one choice per row.

              1. Postulates · 2 min. Students record the four postulates.
              2. Link · 2 min. Before revealing, ask which law each postulate explains. The tags appear on this step.
              3. Build · 2 min. Construct the model (C.6(A) requires constructing models). Dalton's atom: no charges, no internal parts, a solid uniform sphere.
              4. Limits · 1 min. Preview: the next experiment reveals particles inside atoms.

              Dalton · Textbook §2.1.2 worked examples

                Worked example: multiple proportions

                Watch · carbon's two oxides

                1.00 g of carbon combines with 1.33 g of oxygen in one oxide and 2.66 g in the other.

                FixBoth are per 1.00 g of carbon.

                Divide2.66 ÷ 1.33 = 2.0

                Conclude2 oxygen atoms for every 1: CO2 and CO.

                Your turn · in your notebook

                Nitrogen forms N2O and NO. 1.00 g of nitrogen combines with 0.571 g of oxygen in one and 1.14 g in the other. What is the ratio, and what does it mean?

                Check

                1.14 ÷ 0.571 = 2.0, a 2 : 1 ratio. NO has twice as much oxygen per nitrogen atom as N2O.

                1. Watch · 2 min. Model the three steps aloud. The key step: compare masses of one element combined with a fixed mass of the other.
                2. Your turn · 2 min. Students work in their notebooks. Circulate and check that they divide larger by smaller.
                3. Check · 1 min. Extension: SO2 and SO3 give 1.497 ÷ 0.998 = 1.5, which is 3 : 2. Ratios like 1.5 are still whole-number ratios.

                Thomson, 1897 · Textbook §2.1.3

                  Thomson's cathode-ray tube

                  A beam leaves the negative plate (the cathode) in a tube with almost no air.

                  Turn on the plates.

                  Turn on the magnet, then balance it with the plates.

                  The bending gives the charge-to-mass ratio: over 1,000 times a hydrogen ion's. The particles are extremely light.

                  Change the cathode metal and the gas.

                  Conclusion: atoms contain tiny negative, extremely light particles found in all matter: electrons.

                  1. Observe · 1 min. Point out the cathode (−), the anode (+) and the glowing screen. In 1897, no one agreed on what the beam was.
                  2. Charge · 1 min. Click Top plate +. The beam bends toward the positive plate, so the particles are negative.
                  3. Magnet · 2 min. Magnet on, then Top plate + to balance: the beam goes straight. Thomson used this balance to find the speed, then the charge-to-mass ratio.
                  4. Vary · 1 min. Change the metal and the gas. The ratio stays the same, so the same particle is part of all atoms.
                  5. Conclude · 1 min. Record the three properties. Address the misconception: Thomson discovered the electron's properties, not the nucleus.

                  Thomson, 1904 · Textbook §2.1.3

                    Thomson's model: positive charge spread out

                    Reason it out

                    Atoms are neutral. If they contain negative electrons, they must contain an equal amount of positive charge.

                    Thomson spread that positive charge through the whole atom, with electrons embedded in it, like raisins in a pudding.

                    A testable prediction

                    Spread-out charge pushes only weakly. Fast, heavy, positive particles fired at a thin metal foil should all pass straight through.

                    Build Thomson's atom: one choice per row.

                    1. Reason · 1 min. Ask why positive charge must exist (neutral atoms). Name the "plum pudding" model only after students describe it.
                    2. Build · 2 min. Common error: placing the positive charge in a center. That is Rutherford's idea; it comes next.
                    3. Prediction · 1 min. Students write this prediction in their notebook. The next slide tests it.

                    Geiger, Marsden and Rutherford, 1909 to 1911 · Textbook §2.1.4

                      Testing Thomson's model with gold foil

                      Fire 100 with Thomson's model.

                      Alpha particles are fast, positive and about 7,300 times an electron's mass. The gold foil is under 0.001 mm thick.

                      Switch to the real experiment. Fire 20,000 and watch the Bounced back counter.

                      Zoom into atoms. What turns a particle back?

                      1. Predict · 1 min. Students commit. The answer is graded at step 3, after the real run.
                      2. Thomson's model · 1 min. Fire 100 in "Thomson's prediction" mode: nothing turns more than a degree or two.
                      3. Real experiment · 2 min. Switch to Real experiment and fire 20,000. About 1 bounces back: Rutherford's 1911 paper reports about 1 in 20,000 for gold foil (Geiger and Marsden's 1909 platinum foil gave about 1 in 8,000). Read Rutherford's "15-inch shell" quote from §2.1.4.
                      4. Zoom in · 2 min. Switch to Zoom into atoms and fire. Only particles passing very close to a nucleus turn sharply. Stress that the nuclei are drawn far too large.

                      Rutherford, 1911 · Textbook §2.1.4

                        The nuclear atom

                        EvidenceConclusion
                        Most alpha particles passed straight through.The atom is mostly empty space.
                        About 1 in 20,000 bounced back.A tiny, dense, positive nucleus holds nearly all the mass.
                        Electrons are far too light to turn an alpha particle.Electrons move in the space around the nucleus.

                        How tiny?

                        If an atom were a football stadium, its nucleus would be a marble at the center, holding more than 99.9% of the mass.

                        Two puzzles the model could not solve

                        Why do electrons not spiral into the nucleus? Why does each element emit only certain colors of light?

                        1. Explain · 3 min. Each conclusion comes from one observation. Ask students to justify each line before revealing.
                        2. Scale · 1 min. The nucleus is about 1/10,000 to 1/100,000 of the atom's diameter. Return to prediction 1 (empty space).
                        3. Puzzles · 1 min. End of period 1. Exit question: "What did the gold foil show that Thomson's model could not explain?"

                        Bohr · Textbook §2.1.5

                          Hydrogen's line spectrum

                          Light from a glowing solid and from glowing hydrogen gas, spread by a prism. What is different?

                          Every element has its own lines, like a barcode.

                          Why would an atom emit only certain colors?

                          1. Observe · 1 min. A glowing solid produces a continuous rainbow. Hydrogen produces four visible lines: 656 (red), 486 (blue-green), 434 (blue-violet) and 410 nm (violet).
                          2. Compare · 1 min. Each element's pattern is unique, so spectra identify elements, even in stars. Point out that helium's yellow line lies very close to sodium's. That near-miss is the start of the Unit 2 case file.
                          3. Question · 1 min. Rutherford's model offers no answer. Collect ideas, then introduce Bohr.

                          Bohr, 1913 · Textbook §2.1.5

                            Bohr's energy levels

                            1. The electron can have only certain energies: energy levels, n = 1, 2, 3…
                            2. In a level, it does not lose energy.
                            3. Dropping to a lower level emits one photon whose energy equals the gap.

                            Limits

                            Works for hydrogen only. Electrons do not follow fixed circular paths. The fixed energies survive.

                            Excite the electron, then drop it to n = 2. Match each drop to a line.

                            1. Ideas · 2 min. Staircase analogy: energy comes in steps, not a ramp. That is what quantized means.
                            2. Test · 3 min. Run 3→2 (red, 656 nm), 4→2, 5→2, 6→2. Larger drop, more energy, shorter wavelength. Then try a drop to n = 1: ultraviolet, invisible.
                            3. Predict · 1 min. Students answer before you run 6→2.
                            4. Limits · 1 min. Photon energy calculations (E = hf) are covered in Lesson 2.4.

                            Heisenberg, 1927 · Textbook §2.1.6

                              The electron cloud model

                              Uncertainty principle: exact position and exact momentum cannot both be known. Nature sets this limit, not instruments.

                              Measure the electron's position repeatedly.

                              1. Principle · 3 min. Ask first: how would you find where an electron is? (Bounce light off it, which pushes it.) Then move the slider: pinning down position widens the momentum spread. Stress that better instruments cannot fix this.
                              2. Cloud · 3 min. Find 1, then 100, then 1,000. A cloud appears, not a path. Turn on Bohr's orbit: it marks the most likely distance, but the electron is often inside or outside it. Return to prediction 2.
                              3. Check · 1 min. Misconception check: uncertainty is not an instrument problem.

                                    Why did the gold-foil results force scientists to replace Thomson's model?

                                    1. Thomson's model predicted that…
                                    2. Instead, most alpha particles…, but about…
                                    3. A spread-out positive charge cannot…
                                    4. Thus Rutherford concluded that the atom has…
                                    Word bank
                                    alpha particlepositive chargespread outdeflectedbounced backnucleusdenseempty space

                                    Success criteria

                                    Sample answer

                                    Thomson's model spread the positive charge thinly through the atom, so it predicted that every alpha particle would pass through the foil with almost no deflection. Instead, most passed straight through, but about 1 in 20,000 bounced back. A spread-out charge is too weak to reverse a fast, heavy alpha particle, so Rutherford concluded that the atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.

                                    3. Write · 2 min. Sentence starters support ESL students; strong writers may skip them.
                                    4. Self-check · 30 s.
                                    5. Compare · 1 min. Students revise in a second color.

                                      energy levelphotonemitdifferencequantizedline spectrumwavelengthcolor

                                      Success criteria

                                      Sample answer

                                      In Bohr's model, the electron can only be in certain energy levels. When it drops from a higher level to a lower one, the atom emits one photon whose energy equals the difference between the levels. Because only certain energy differences exist, only certain colors appear. For example, the drop from n = 3 to n = 2 always gives the red line at 656 nm.

                                        2. This is not because of…; it is…

                                        4. A dense part of the cloud means…
                                        Word bank
                                        positionmomentumexactinstrumentpathprobabilityorbitalelectron cloud

                                        Success criteria

                                        Sample answer

                                        Heisenberg's uncertainty principle states that an electron's exact position and exact momentum cannot both be known. This is not because of poor instruments; it is a property of nature. Thus scientists cannot draw a path; instead they describe where the electron is likely to be, using orbitals. A dense part of the cloud means the electron is more likely to be found there.

                                          Wrap-up · Exit ticket

                                            Answer the driving question

                                            How do scientists build a model of something they cannot see? Write your answer in your notebook.

                                            Word bank
                                            modelevidenceexperimentpredictrevisenucleusenergy levelelectron cloud

                                            Claim

                                            One sentence that answers the question.

                                            1. Scientists build models of atoms by…
                                            2. A model is kept until…

                                            Sample claim

                                            Scientists build atomic models from indirect evidence, and they revise a model whenever reliable new evidence does not fit.

                                            Evidence

                                            A specific experiment and its result.

                                            1. For example, Thomson's model predicted…, but…
                                            2. Cathode-ray experiments showed…
                                            3. Hydrogen's line spectrum showed…

                                            Sample evidence

                                            Thomson's model predicted that alpha particles would pass straight through gold foil, but about 1 in 20,000 bounced back. Glowing hydrogen also emits only four visible colors.

                                            Reasoning

                                            Why the evidence forced a change.

                                            1. This result forced a new model because…
                                            2. Each new model kept… and added…

                                            Sample reasoning

                                            A model must explain every reliable result. Bounce-backs need a tiny, dense nucleus, and fixed colors need fixed energy levels, so the model changed each time while keeping what still worked.

                                            Self-check: mark each part your answer meets, then compare with the sample.

                                            1. Claim · 2 min. One sentence. Push for "indirect evidence" and "revise."
                                            2. Evidence · 3 min. Must name an experiment and its result.
                                            3. Reasoning · 3 min. Must explain why the result could not fit the old model.
                                            4. Self-check · 2 min. Students self-check, then you show the sample; each part appears in its own card. Score 0 to 2 per part.
