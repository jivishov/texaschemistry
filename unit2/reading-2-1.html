2.1

The development of atomic models

No one has ever looked inside an atom the way one looks inside a box. A row of one million atoms would be roughly as wide as a human hair is thick. Yet chemists can describe what is inside an atom, where its mass is located and how its electrons behave. Each of these claims rests on experimental evidence.Atoms are too small to see inside. A million atoms in a row are about as wide as one hair. Still, scientists know what is inside an atom. They learned it from experiments.

§2.1.1

How a model earns trust

A scientific is a simplified picture, object or set of rules that explains what we observe and predicts what we will observe next. A model of the atom need not look like a real atom. It must agree with the .A model is a simple picture or set of rules. It explains what we see and predicts what we will see next. A model of the atom does not need to look like an atom. It needs to match the evidence.

Atomic models were built from indirect evidence. Scientists fired particles at atoms, sent electricity through gases and measured the light that atoms emit. They never saw inside an atom. They reasoned from the results to a model, much as one can infer the shape of an object sealed in a box by probing it from outside.Scientists could not look inside atoms. So they did experiments: they shot particles at atoms, sent electricity through gases and studied the light atoms give off. Then they used the results to build a model, like finding the shape inside a sealed box by poking it.

A model is kept as long as it explains the evidence. When a reliable new result cannot be explained, scientists revise the model or replace it. This is not a failure; it is how science advances. This chapter traces four such changes.Scientists keep a model while it fits the evidence. If new evidence does not fit, they fix the model or replace it. This is normal. In this chapter, the model of the atom changes four times.

Two terms must be kept distinct. A describes a pattern, such as “mass is conserved in a chemical reaction.” A explains why patterns happen; atomic theory explains why mass is conserved. A theory does not become a law, however well it is supported. The two serve different purposes.A law describes what always happens. Example: mass stays the same in a reaction. A theory explains why it happens. Example: atomic theory. A theory never turns into a law. They have different jobs.

Key idea

A model is judged by evidence. When evidence appears that the model cannot explain, the model changes.

§2.1.2

Dalton: atoms from mass data

Around 1800, chemists were carefully measuring masses in their reactions. Three patterns appeared repeatedly.Around 1800, chemists carefully measured masses in reactions. They found three patterns.

  1. Conservation of mass Antoine Lavoisier, 1780sIn a chemical reaction, the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants. This is the .The mass after a reaction equals the mass before it.
  2. Definite proportions Joseph Proust, 1790sA always contains the same elements in the same proportions by mass. Pure water is 11.2% hydrogen by mass, whether it comes from a lake or a lab. This is the .A compound always has the same makeup by mass. Water is always 11.2% hydrogen by mass, from any source.
  3. Multiple proportions John Dalton, 1803When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a small whole-number ratio. This is the .For a fixed mass of one element, the masses of the other in two compounds compare as small whole numbers, like 1 : 2.

Dalton asked what kind of matter would produce these patterns. Whole-number ratios suggested that matter comes in countable pieces. He proposed that matter is made of tiny, separate particles that combine in fixed numbers. He called them .Dalton asked: what must matter be like to follow these patterns? Whole numbers suggest countable pieces. His answer: matter is made of tiny pieces called atoms.

Key idea

Mass data, not direct observation, were the first evidence for atoms.

Dalton's postulates (1808)

Dalton stated his theory as four . Together they explain all three mass laws.Dalton's theory has four main ideas. Together they explain the mass patterns.

  1. All matter is made of tiny, indivisible particles called atoms.
  2. Atoms of the same element are identical in mass and properties. Atoms of different elements differ. explains definite proportions
  3. Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds. explains definite and multiple proportions
  4. In a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged. They are not created, destroyed or changed into atoms of another element. explains conservation of mass

Two postulates did not survive later evidence. Atoms contain smaller particles (§2.1.3), and atoms of one element can differ in mass (isotopes, Lesson 2.2). Postulate 4 still holds for chemical reactions. You use it every time you balance an equation.Later, scientists found two problems. Atoms have smaller parts inside. Also, atoms of one element can have different masses. Postulate 4 is still true for chemical reactions.

Thomson's model (1904)

Atoms have no overall charge. If they contain negative electrons, they must also contain an equal amount of positive charge. In 1904 Thomson proposed that an atom is a sphere of evenly spread positive charge with electrons embedded in it. It is often called the plum pudding model, after a British dessert dotted with raisins.Atoms have no overall charge. So if they have negative electrons, they also need the same amount of positive charge. Thomson pictured a ball of spread-out positive charge with electrons inside it, like raisins in a pudding.

Notice two features. The positive charge is spread out, not concentrated in one place, and the mass is spread through the whole atom. The next experiment tested exactly these two features.Two features matter here. The positive charge is spread out, and the mass is spread out. The next experiment tested these two ideas.

Honors · How light is an electron?

In 1909 Robert Millikan measured the electron's charge: e = 1.60 × 10−19 C. Combine it with the charge-to-mass ratio, e/m = 1.76 × 1011 C/kg:

m = e ÷ (e/m) = 1.60 × 10−19 C ÷ 1.76 × 1011 C/kg ≈ 9.1 × 10−31 kg

A hydrogen atom has a mass of 1.67 × 10−27 kg, so an electron has about 1/1,800 the mass of the lightest atom.

§2.1.4

Rutherford: the nucleus

Starting in 1909, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, working in Ernest Rutherford's lab in Manchester, England, aimed beams of at thin metal foils, including gold foil less than a thousandth of a millimeter thick. Alpha particles are fast, positively charged and about 7,300 times more massive than an electron. A screen around the foil flashed wherever one struck it.Starting in 1909, Rutherford's team shot alpha particles at very thin gold foil. Alpha particles are fast and positive. Each has about 7,300 times the mass of an electron. A screen around the foil flashed where each one hit.

Thomson's model made a clear prediction. Its positive charge is spread thin, so nothing in the foil should be strong enough to turn a heavy, fast alpha particle around. Every particle should pass through, deflected by a degree or less.Thomson's model predicted that every alpha particle would go almost straight through. Spread-out charge is too weak to turn them around.

What they observed

  • Most alpha particles passed straight through.
  • Some were at large angles.
  • A tiny fraction were turned through about 90° or more: roughly 1 in 20,000 for thin gold foil.

Geiger and Marsden's first result (1909, platinum foil) was about 1 in 8,000. Rutherford's 1911 paper reports about 1 in 20,000 for gold. The key point is how tiny the fraction is.

Rutherford later said it was “almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.”

The nuclear model (1911)

In 1911 Rutherford explained the results with a new model of the atom.In 1911, Rutherford made a new model to explain the results.

  • Most of the atom is empty space, so most alpha particles pass straight through.The atom is mostly empty space. That is why most particles went straight through.
  • Nearly all the mass and all the positive charge are packed into a tiny center, the . Only a near head-on approach to this concentrated charge can turn an alpha particle back.Almost all the mass and all the positive charge are in a tiny center, the nucleus. Only a near hit on the nucleus can push a particle back.
  • Electrons move through the space around the nucleus.Electrons move around the nucleus.

The nuclear model left two puzzles. The physics of the time predicted that an electron moving around a nucleus should emit energy and spiral into it within a tiny fraction of a second, yet atoms are stable. The model also could not explain why each element emits only certain colors of light.The model had two problems. Older physics said electrons should fall into the nucleus, but atoms do not collapse. The model also could not explain why each element gives off only certain colors of light.

Rutherford named the positive particles in the nucleus in 1920. Neutrons, discovered in 1932, are introduced in Lesson 2.2.

Bohr: energy levels

When electricity passes through hydrogen gas, the gas glows pink-purple. A prism does not spread this light into a full rainbow. Instead, it separates the light into thin lines; four are bright: red, blue-green, blue-violet and violet. Each element produces its own pattern of lines, its .Hydrogen gas glows when electricity passes through it. A prism splits this light into a few colored lines, not a rainbow. Four are bright. Each element has its own set of lines, its line spectrum.

In 1913 Niels Bohr explained hydrogen's lines with three ideas.In 1913, Niels Bohr explained the lines with three ideas.

  1. The electron can move only in certain orbits, each with a fixed energy. These are , numbered n = 1, 2, 3 and so on.
  2. While it stays in one level, the electron does not lose energy.
  3. When the electron drops from a higher level to a lower one, the atom emits one whose energy equals the difference between the two levels.

Only certain levels exist, so only certain energy differences exist, and only certain colors appear. The electron's energy is : it can have only certain fixed values, like the steps of a staircase rather than a ramp.Energy is quantized: only some values are allowed, like steps on a staircase. So only some energy drops can happen, and only some colors appear.

Bohr's model matched hydrogen's lines almost exactly. It failed for atoms with more than one electron, and later evidence showed that electrons do not travel in fixed circular paths. The idea of fixed energy levels survived.Bohr's model worked for hydrogen. It did not work for atoms with more than one electron. Electrons also do not move in circles. But the idea of energy levels is still used.

Key idea

A line spectrum is evidence that an electron's energy is quantized.

§2.1.6

Heisenberg: the electron cloud

To locate an object, an observer must interact with it, for example by bouncing light off it. For a baseball, the push from that light is far too small to notice. For an electron, it is not.To find where something is, you must bounce something off it, like light. Light barely moves a baseball. But it does move an electron.

In 1927 Werner Heisenberg showed that the limit runs deeper than any instrument. His states that it is impossible to know both the exact position and the exact (mass × velocity) of a particle at the same time. The more precisely one is known, the less precisely the other can be known. This is a property of nature, not a flaw in equipment.In 1927, Heisenberg showed it is not just a problem with tools. His uncertainty principle says we can never know both exactly where an electron is and exactly how it is moving. The better we know one, the worse we know the other. Better tools cannot fix this.

If an electron's position and momentum can never both be known exactly, it cannot be pictured as following a definite orbit. The quantum mechanical model, developed between 1925 and 1927 by Heisenberg, Erwin Schrödinger and others, replaces orbits with : regions where an electron is likely to be found. Together they form the , dense where the electron is likely to be and thin where it is unlikely.So an electron cannot have an exact path. The modern model uses orbitals instead: places where the electron is likely to be. Together they make the electron cloud.

Bohr's energy levels survive in the quantum model. Electrons still have quantized energies, which is why the quantum model still explains line spectra. What changed is the description of where the electron is.Energy levels are still part of the modern model. What changed is how we picture where the electron is.

Key idea

Electrons do not follow definite paths. The electron cloud shows where an electron is likely to be found.

Review

Chapter review

  • 2.1.1 Mass laws led Dalton to propose solid, indivisible atoms that combine in whole-number ratios.
  • 2.1.2 Cathode rays showed that atoms contain electrons: negatively charged, very light and present in all matter. Thomson's model spread the positive charge through the atom.
  • 2.1.3 Gold-foil bounce-backs showed a tiny, dense, positive nucleus in an atom that is mostly empty space.
  • 2.1.4 Hydrogen's line spectrum showed that electrons have fixed energy levels. Bohr's model explained it but failed for atoms with more than one electron.
  • 2.1.5 The uncertainty principle rules out exact orbits. Electrons are described by a cloud of probability.
  • 2.1.6 Models change when evidence demands it, and each new model keeps what still works.
