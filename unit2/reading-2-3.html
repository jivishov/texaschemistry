2.3

Average atomic mass

Look at chlorine on the periodic table and you will find the number 35.45. A chlorine atom has 17 protons and either 18 or 20 neutrons. No atom can have 18.45 neutrons, so the decimal cannot come from counting the particles in one atom. Instead, it comes from the way atoms of an element are mixed together in nature.The periodic table says chlorine has an atomic mass of 35.45. But a chlorine atom has 17 protons and 18 or 20 neutrons. An atom cannot have part of a neutron. So the decimal must come from somewhere else. It comes from how chlorine atoms are mixed in nature.

Driving question

If every atom is built from whole particles, why is the atomic mass on the periodic table a decimal?

§2.3.1

Weighing atoms

Recap from Lesson 2.2: are atoms of the same with different numbers of , so they have different masses.

In grams, the mass of one atom is extremely small; a chlorine atom is about 6 × 10−23 g. Chemists therefore use a unit suited to atoms, the (u). It is defined from one isotope: a carbon-12 atom has a mass of exactly 12 u, so 1 u is one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom, about 1.66 × 10−24 g. Every other atomic mass is measured relative to carbon-12.In grams, the mass of one atom is a tiny number. So chemists use a special unit, the atomic mass unit (u). A carbon-12 atom has a mass of exactly 12 u. That is the standard. The masses of all other atoms are compared with carbon-12.

On this scale, a proton is 1.007276 u and a neutron is 1.008665 u, both close to 1 u. An electron is only 0.000549 u, so electrons add very little to an atom's mass.A proton and a neutron are each about 1 u. A proton has about 1,836 times the mass of an electron. So electrons add almost nothing.

Isotopic masses are measured with a . It turns atoms into ions, speeds them up and sends them through a magnetic field. Strictly, the bend depends on an ion's mass divided by its charge. Here every ion carries the same +1 charge, so the field bends lighter ions more than heavier ones, and each isotope lands in its own place, and a detector counts the ions that arrive there. Francis Aston built one of the first such instruments in 1919 and used it to show that many elements, including neon and chlorine, are mixtures of isotopes. He won the 1922 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.A mass spectrometer measures isotopic masses. It gives atoms a charge, makes them move fast and bends them with a magnet. Light ions bend more than heavy ones. So each isotope hits a different spot, and a detector counts them. Francis Aston built one of the first in 1919. He showed that many elements are mixtures of isotopes.

The result is a : a bar for each isotope, placed at its on the horizontal axis. Its height is the isotope's , the percentage of the element's atoms that are that isotope. For chlorine, the bar at 34.968853 u reaches 75.76% and the bar at 36.965903 u reaches 24.24%. The percent abundances of all the isotopes add up to 100%.The graph is called a mass spectrum. Each bar is one isotope. The position of the bar shows its isotopic mass. The height shows its percent abundance: the percent of atoms that are that isotope. For chlorine, 75.76% of atoms are chlorine-35 and 24.24% are chlorine-37. Together they make 100%.

Key idea

A mass spectrometer sorts an element's atoms, as ions of equal charge, by mass. It measures each isotope's mass and the share of atoms that have it.

Mass number is not isotopic mass

An isotope's and its isotopic mass are close, but they are different quantities. The mass number is a count of protons plus neutrons, so it is always a whole number. The isotopic mass is a measured mass in u, read from a mass spectrum.Mass number and isotopic mass are close, but they are not the same. The mass number is a count of protons and neutrons. It is always a whole number. The isotopic mass is a measured mass in u.

Why the small difference? Protons and neutrons each have a mass slightly greater than 1 u, which would push the total up. However, when a nucleus forms, a small amount of mass is released as energy, which pulls the total down. Only carbon-12 has a mass that is exactly a whole number, and only because the unit is defined from it. In calculations, always use isotopic masses, not mass numbers.Why are they slightly different? Protons and neutrons are each a little more than 1 u. Also, when a nucleus forms, a little mass is released as energy. Only carbon-12 is exact, because the unit is based on it. In calculations, use the isotopic masses.

Key idea

Mass number counts particles. Isotopic mass measures mass. Use isotopic masses to calculate.

Mass spectrometers at work

Mass spectrometers like Aston's now have many uses. Forensic labs identify drugs and poisons from the masses of their molecules. Newborn screening labs test babies' blood for rare disorders with mass spectrometers. NASA's Curiosity rover carries one to study the air and rocks of Mars. Airport trace detectors mostly use a related method that sorts ions by how fast they drift through air.Mass spectrometers have many uses today. Crime labs use them to identify drugs and poisons. Health labs use them to test the blood of newborn babies. The Curiosity rover uses one on Mars. Airport explosive detectors mostly use a similar method that sorts ions as they drift through air.

§2.3.2

The weighted average

The periodic table gives one mass for each element, its . Because most elements are mixtures of isotopes, that number is an average. It is not a plain average, however: chlorine-35 atoms outnumber chlorine-37 atoms about three to one, so chlorine-35 must count more.The periodic table shows one mass for each element. It is called the average atomic mass. It is an average of the isotopic masses. But it is not a plain average. Chlorine has about three chlorine-35 atoms for every chlorine-37 atom, so chlorine-35 must count more.

Picture a jar of 100 chlorine atoms: 76 are chlorine-35 and 24 are chlorine-37 (the real 75.76% and 24.24%, rounded to whole atoms). The average mass of one atom in the jar is the total mass divided by 100. That is the same as multiplying each isotope's mass by its fraction of the atoms and adding the results. This is a .Imagine a jar of 100 chlorine atoms: 76 chlorine-35 and 24 chlorine-37. To find the average, add up all their masses and divide by 100. Or, the short way: multiply each mass by its fraction of the atoms, then add. This is a weighted average.

average = (mass1 × fraction1) + (mass2 × fraction2) + …

Each fraction is the percent abundance divided by 100, so 75.76% becomes 0.7576. The fractions of all the isotopes must add up to exactly 1, because together they describe every atom of the element. Check this before you multiply.Change each percent to a fraction: divide by 100. So 75.76% becomes 0.7576. All the fractions must add up to 1. Check this first.

Think of it this way: a course grade where tests count for 70% and homework for 30%. A 90 on tests and a 60 on homework give 0.70 × 90 + 0.30 × 60 = 81, not the plain mean of 75. The limit: a teacher chooses grade weights, but nature sets isotope weights by how many atoms of each isotope exist.Think of it this way: in some classes, tests count 70% and homework 30%. A 90 on tests and a 60 on homework give 81, not 75. The difference: a teacher picks grade weights. For isotopes, nature sets them: the weight is the share of atoms.

Average atomic mass weights each isotope's mass by its fraction of the atoms. The fractions add up to 1.

§2.3.3

Why the periodic table shows a decimal

A weighted average is pulled toward the values that occur most often. Chlorine-35 makes up about three quarters of chlorine atoms, so chlorine's average, 35.45 u, sits much closer to 34.97 u than to 36.97 u. The plain mean of the two masses, 35.97 u, would be correct only if both isotopes were equally common.The most common isotope pulls the average toward it. Three quarters of chlorine atoms are chlorine-35. So the average, 35.45 u, is close to chlorine-35. The plain mean, 35.97 u, would only be right if both isotopes were equally common.

This answers the opening question. The periodic table value 35.45 u is not the mass of any chlorine atom. Every chlorine atom is either chlorine-35 (34.968853 u) or chlorine-37 (36.965903 u). The decimal describes the mixture, in the same way that a class average of 83.5 can come from scores that are all whole numbers.So now we can answer the question. No chlorine atom has a mass of 35.45 u. Each atom is chlorine-35 or chlorine-37. The number 35.45 describes the whole mixture. A class can have an average score of 83.5 even if every score is a whole number.

Two other factors also make the value a decimal: isotopic masses are not whole numbers, and the percent abundances are not simple fractions. Even an element with one main isotope does not have a whole-number average. Natural helium is about 99.9999% helium-4 (4.002603 u), so its average is 4.0026 u.Two more reasons for the decimal: isotopic masses are not whole numbers, and the percents are not round numbers. Even helium, which is almost all helium-4, has an average of 4.0026 u.

Key idea

Average atomic mass is a decimal pulled toward the most abundant isotope. It describes a mixture of isotopes, so no single atom in the mixture has that mass. An element with only one natural isotope, such as fluorine, is different: all its atoms have the listed mass.

Isotope ratios as fingerprints

Periodic-table values are based on the percent abundances in ordinary samples from Earth. Real samples vary slightly, and an can reveal where a sample came from. Carbon is about 98.9% carbon-12 and 1.1% carbon-13, but plants, foods and the body each have slightly different shares of carbon-13.The periodic table uses the percents in ordinary samples from Earth. Real samples are a little different from each other. A sample's isotope ratio can show where it came from. Carbon is mostly carbon-12, with about 1.1% carbon-13. Different plants and foods have slightly different amounts of carbon-13.

  • Sports doping tests. Synthetic testosterone is made from plant chemicals, often from soy, which have less carbon-13 than the steroids an athlete's body makes. Anti-doping labs measure the carbon isotope ratio of steroids in urine. A low carbon-13 share indicates that the testosterone came from outside the body.Sports doping tests. Factory-made testosterone comes from plant chemicals. It has less carbon-13 than the body's own. Labs measure carbon-13 in urine to detect doping.
  • Honey and food origin. Sugar from corn and sugarcane has more carbon-13 than the sugars bees collect from most flowers. Food labs use this to detect honey diluted with cheap syrup, and other isotope ratios help trace where foods such as wine were produced.Honey and food. Corn and sugarcane sugar have more carbon-13 than most honey. Labs use this to find honey mixed with syrup. Other isotope ratios can show where a food was made.

Honors · Find the abundance from the average

If you know the average and the two isotopic masses, you can work backward. Let x be the fraction of the lighter isotope, so the heavier isotope's fraction is 1 − x. Copper averages 63.546 u, from copper-63 (62.929598 u) and copper-65 (64.927790 u):

62.929598 x + 64.927790 (1 − x) = 63.546

x = (64.927790 − 63.546) ÷ (64.927790 − 62.929598) = 1.381790 ÷ 1.998192 = 0.6915

Copper is therefore 69.15% copper-63 and 30.85% copper-65, the measured values. Use the average with all its decimal places: rounding it to 63.55 gives 68.95%.

%

(70.92470258 − 69.723) ÷ (70.92470258 − 68.9255735) = 1.20170 ÷ 1.99913 = 0.6011, so about 60.11% gallium-69. The measured value is 60.108%.
