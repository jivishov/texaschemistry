Driving question · Textbook 2.5 opener

    Sodium and neon

    Sodium, 11 electrons: a pea-sized piece fizzes and skates on water.

    Neon, 10 electrons: it reacts with nothing, so it glows safely in a sign for decades.

        Orbitals · Textbook §2.5.1

          Levels, sublevels and orbitals

          Nested structure

          Each energy level (n = 1, 2, 3…) contains sublevels s, p, d, f. Each sublevel contains orbitals. Level 1: s only. Level 2: s, p. Level 3: s, p, d.

          Every orbital holds 2 electrons

          SublevelOrbitalsElectrons
          s12
          p36
          d510
          f714

          Analogy

          An atom is like a hotel. Levels are floors, sublevels are room types, and each orbital is a room with two beds.

          Limits: electrons do not stay in fixed spots, and the floors overlap in energy: a room on floor 4 can be lower than one on floor 3.

          1. Structure · 2 min. Connect to Lesson 2.1: orbitals are regions where an electron is likely to be found, not paths.
          2. Capacity · 2 min. Derive each row: orbitals × 2. Extension: a whole level holds 2n2 (2, 8, 18, 32).
          3. Analogy · 1 min. Ask students for one way the hotel analogy fails. The overlap leads into the next slide.
          4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: "5" confuses orbitals with electrons.

          Configurations · Textbook §2.5.2

            The filling order

            Follow each arrow from top to bottom, starting at the top.

            4s is lower in energy than 3d, so 4s fills first.

            The periodic table follows the same order. Read each period left to right.

            1. Diagonal rule · 2 min. Students copy the rows and arrows into their notebooks. Say the order aloud together: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p.
            2. Blocks · 2 min. The d block is one level behind: in period 4 it is 3d. Helium is an s-block element placed in group 18.
            3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: "3d, to finish level 3." Scandium (21) is the first 3d element.

                  Valence electrons · Textbook §2.5.3

                    Valence electrons

                    Definition

                    Valence electrons are the electrons in the highest occupied energy level. Sulfur, [Ne] 3s2 3p4: level 3 has 2 + 4 = 6.

                    Shortcut for representative elements

                    Group12131415161718
                    Valence e−12345678

                    Groups 13 to 18: group number minus 10. Example: chlorine, group 17, has 17 − 10 = 7. Same group, same number.

                    The exceptions

                    Helium is 1s2: 2 valence electrons, and its first level is full. Transition metals (groups 3 to 12): the group number does not give valence electrons.

                    1. Define · 1 min. Valence electrons are the ones involved when atoms react.
                    2. Group rule · 2 min. Have students check sulfur: group 16, 16 − 10 = 6, matching the configuration.
                    3. Helium · 1 min. Return to prediction 2: helium has 2, not 8, yet it is just as unreactive.
                    4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: counting the 3d electrons as valence (17).

                        Families · Textbook §2.5.5 · SEP 3(A)

                          Groups 1 and 2

                          Alkali metals · group 1

                          1 valence electron, lost easily: 1+ ions, very reactive. Na and K react with water, releasing hydrogen.

                          Alkaline earth metals · group 2

                          2 valence electrons: 2+ ions, reactive but less than group 1. Magnesium burns white in flares and fireworks.

                          1. Predict · 3 min. Start with sodium (selected). Then potassium and magnesium. Students predict in notebooks before you check.
                          2. Group 1 · 1 min. Losing 1 electron leaves Na+ with neon's arrangement. Hydrogen is in group 1 but is not an alkali metal. Sodium and potassium are stored under oil.
                          3. Group 2 · 1 min. Calcium reacts steadily with water; potassium, beside it, violently. Why reactivity changes: Unit 3.
                          4. Check · 1 min. Misconceptions: metals are unreactive, or form negative ions.

                          Families · Textbook §2.5.5

                            Groups 17 and 18

                            Halogens · group 17

                            7 valence electrons, one short of 8: they gain 1 to form 1− ions (Cl− in table salt). Very reactive nonmetals made of diatomic molecules: F2, Cl2, Br2, I2. Chlorine compounds disinfect pools.

                            Noble gases · group 18

                            Full outer level (8; helium 2): no ions, almost unreactive. Neon glows in signs, argon fills light bulbs, helium fills balloons and, as a liquid at about −269 °C, cools MRI magnets.

                            Careful with words

                            "Chlorine wants one more electron" is shorthand. Atoms do not want anything: a full outer level is simply a stable, low-energy arrangement.

                            1. Halogens · 2 min. Return to prediction 3: gaining 1 is far easier than losing 7. Show chlorine, bromine and iodine in the predictor if time allows.
                            2. Noble gases · 2 min. Argon replaces air in bulbs because oxygen would burn the hot filament. Xenon and krypton form a few compounds under special conditions.
                            3. Shorthand · 30 s. Label "wants" as a simplification whenever students use it.
                            4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: families are rows. They are columns.

                            Families · Textbook §2.5.6

                              Transition metals

                              The d block

                              Groups 3 to 12 fill a d sublevel. Iron (group 8) is : only 2 electrons in level 4, so group 8 ≠ 8 valence electrons.

                              Family properties

                              • Several ion charges: Fe2+ and Fe3+, Cu+ and Cu2+
                              • Less reactive than groups 1 and 2: copper wiring and pipes
                              • Colored compounds: pigments
                              TiO2 whiteiron oxide redcobalt bluecopper green

                              Honors · two exceptions

                              Cr is [Ar] 3d5 4s1, not 4s2 3d4. Cu is [Ar] 3d10 4s1, not 4s2 3d9. Half-full and full d sublevels are especially stable.

                              1. d block · 2 min. The 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy, so different numbers can be lost. Lewis structures are not drawn for transition metals in this course.
                              2. Properties · 2 min. Chromium is named from the Greek word for color. Zinc is an exception: only Zn2+.
                              3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: group number = valence electrons for every element.
                              4. Honors · 1 min. Optional. Show Z = 24 and 29 on the orbital board.

                                      Checkpoint · Question 3 · Objective 2.5.5

                                        Sodium reacts violently with water, but neon reacts with nothing. Use valence electrons to explain.

                                        Think silently. Plan what you will say.

                                        0:30

                                        Explain your idea to your partner, then switch.

                                        1:00

                                        Write your answer in your notebook. Use these sentence starters:

                                        1. Neon has… valence electrons, so its outer level is…
                                        2. Sodium has… valence electron, which it…
                                        3. When sodium loses it, it forms… with the same arrangement as…
                                        4. So sodium is… and neon is…
                                        Word bank
                                        valence electronfull outer levelstablelose1+ ionalkali metalnoble gas

                                        Success criteria

                                        Sample answer

                                        Neon has 8 valence electrons, so its outer level is full and very stable: as a noble gas it forms no ions and almost never reacts. Sodium, an alkali metal, has 1 valence electron outside a neon core, which it loses easily. When sodium loses it, it forms Na+ with the same arrangement as neon. So sodium is very reactive and neon is almost unreactive.

                                        1. Think · 30 s.
                                        2. Talk · 1 min.
                                        3. Write · 2 min. Misconception to watch: "sodium becomes neon." It keeps its 11 protons; only the electron arrangement matches.

                                          Wrap-up · Exit ticket · SEP 3(A)

                                            Answer the driving question

                                            How does the arrangement of an atom's electrons predict how the element behaves? Write your answer in your notebook.

                                            Word bank
                                            valence electronconfigurationgroupfull outer levellosegainionreactive

                                            Claim

                                            One sentence that answers the question.

                                            1. An element's behavior depends on…
                                            2. Elements in the same group…

                                            Sample claim

                                            The number of valence electrons predicts how an element behaves, so elements in the same group behave alike.

                                            Evidence

                                            Two elements, their valence electrons and their behavior.

                                            1. For example, sodium has… and…
                                            2. Chlorine has… and…
                                            3. Neon has… and…

                                            Sample evidence

                                            Sodium ([Ne] 3s1) has 1 valence electron and reacts violently with water, forming Na+. Neon ([He] 2s2 2p6) has 8 and is unreactive.

                                            Reasoning

                                            Why valence electrons decide the behavior.

                                            1. A full outer level is…
                                            2. So atoms with 1 or 2… and atoms with 7…

                                            Sample reasoning

                                            A full outer level is stable. Sodium reaches it by losing its 1 valence electron, chlorine by gaining 1, and neon already has it, so it does not react.

                                            Self-check: mark each part your answer meets, then compare with the sample.

                                            1. Claim · 2 min. One sentence. Push for "valence electrons" and "group."
                                            2. Evidence · 3 min. Must name specific elements with their valence counts.
                                            3. Reasoning · 3 min. Must explain why a full outer level matters. "Wants" counts only if labeled as shorthand.
                                            4. Self-check · 2 min. Students self-check, then you show the sample. Score 0 to 2 per part.
