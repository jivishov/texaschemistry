Bonds within, attractions between

Chlorine

Chlorine is made of Cl2 molecules.

Predict

Which pull is stronger: inside a molecule, or between two?

Bond within

A covalent bond joins the two atoms. That is a bond within.

Attraction between

Weak attractions hold neighboring molecules close, much weaker than the bond.

Salt

Salt has no molecules. Ionic bonds run through the whole crystal.

Copper

Copper has no molecules. Metallic bonds run through the whole metal.

Rule

Bonds hold atoms or ions together in a substance. Much weaker attractions act between molecules.

Check

In liquid chlorine, what holds neighboring Cl2 molecules near each other?

  1. Aweak attractions between the molecules
  2. Bcovalent bonds between the molecules
  3. Cionic bonds

A. The Cl–Cl bond is inside each molecule. Between molecules there are only weak attractions.

Atom colors show the element, not the charge.

The TEKS call bonds "intramolecular forces", even though ionic and metallic crystals contain no molecules; the reading says "bonds within a substance" for that reason. Attractions between molecules are the "intermolecular forces" of slide 5. Atom colors are CPK (Cl green); the salt ions keep the unit code (Na⁺ red, Cl⁻ blue), hence the caption.

Dispersion forces

Electrons

Electrons in a molecule always move.

Predict

For an instant, where could more electrons sit?

For an instant

One end is δ− for an instant. The other end is δ+.

Attraction

The brief charges pull a neighbor. New ones keep forming: dispersion forces.

Evidence

Boiling point against electrons per molecule.

Rule

Dispersion forces act between all molecules. More electrons make stronger dispersion forces. Stronger forces mean a higher boiling point.

Check

Chlorine boils at −34.0 °C. Bromine boils at 58.8 °C. Why does bromine boil higher?

  1. ABr2 has more electrons, so stronger dispersion forces.
  2. BBr2 is polar.
  3. CBoiling must break the Br–Br bond.

A. Br2 has 70 electrons and Cl2 has 34. Both are nonpolar.

Fritz London explained these forces in 1930, so they are also called London dispersion forces. Halogens at 25 °C: F₂ and Cl₂ are gases, Br₂ is a liquid, I₂ is a solid; the plot shows why. The δ signs here last only an instant, unlike the lasting δ signs of a polar bond (slide 8).

Dipole-dipole forces

F2

F2 is nonpolar. It has dispersion forces only.

HCl

HCl is polar: δ+ on H, δ− on Cl (Lesson 4.4).

Predict

How will two HCl molecules line up?

Dipole-dipole forces

The δ+ end of one pulls the δ− end of the next: dipole-dipole forces.

Compare

Both have 18 electrons. HCl boils about 100 °C higher.

Rule

Polar molecules pull each other. These pulls are dipole-dipole forces. They keep dispersion forces too.

Check

Which substance has dipole-dipole forces between its molecules?

  1. Ahydrogen chloride, HCl
  2. Bcarbon dioxide, CO2
  3. Cmethane, CH4

A. HCl is polar, so dipole-dipole forces add to its dispersion forces.

F₂ and HCl both have 18 electrons, so their dispersion forces are about equal. F₂ boils at −188.1 °C and HCl at −85.1 °C, about 100 °C higher: the extra pull is the dipole-dipole force. Polarity comes from Lesson 4.4 (shape and bond polarity).

Hydrogen bonding

Same electrons

CH4, NH3 and H2O all have 10 electrons.

Predict

Which two boil far above methane?

Hydrogen bond

An H on O is pulled to a lone pair on the next O. A hydrogen bond.

How strong?

Not a covalent bond. About 5 to 10% as strong.

Methane

H on C: no hydrogen bonds. Dispersion forces only.

Water

Each water molecule can form up to four.

Rule

Hydrogen bonding needs an H atom bonded to N, O or F. The next molecule needs a lone pair on N, O or F.

Check

Which substance has hydrogen bonding between its molecules?

  1. Awater, H2O
  2. Bmethane, CH4
  3. Chydrogen chloride, HCl

A. Each H is bonded to O, and O has two lone pairs.

A hydrogen bond is an especially strong dipole-dipole attraction, not a covalent bond: only about 5 to 10% as strong. The 10-electron trio (CH₄ −161.5 °C, NH₃ −33.3 °C, H₂O 100.0 °C) has equal dispersion forces, so the difference is the hydrogen bonding. The reading's "all eight" view of the plot is not shown.

Molecules stay whole

Solid methane

Solid methane at −182.5 °C. Count the C–H bonds: all intact.

Predict

What comes apart when it melts?

Melting

The attractions let go. Every molecule stays whole.

Boiling

At −161.5 °C the molecules spread far apart.

Water

Water boils at 100.0 °C. Steam is still H2O.

Energy for 18 g of water

Boiling 18 g of water: 40.7 kJ. Breaking all its O–H bonds: about 928 kJ.

Rule

Melting and boiling separate molecules. They do not break the covalent bonds inside them.

Check

What happens to the water molecules when water boils?

  1. AThe attractions let go, and the molecules move far apart.
  2. BTheir O–H bonds break.
  3. CThey split into hydrogen and oxygen.

A. The molecules separate. Each keeps its two O–H bonds.

The reading's heating model: six molecules from solid to gas; the readout "Covalent bonds: 24 of 24 intact" (water: 12 of 12) never changes, while "Attractions drawn" falls to 0 in the gas. Energy (OpenStax): boiling 18 g of water at 100 °C takes 40.7 kJ; breaking every O–H bond in it would take about 928 kJ, more than 20 times as much. Shown as the reading's comparison only; no bond-energy calculation.

Why ice floats

Ice

Each molecule keeps its two O–H bonds.

Dashed lines

Each dashed line is a hydrogen bond.

Neighbors

Each oxygen has four neighbors, 276 pm away.

Predict

When ice melts, does the water take more space or less?

Open rings

Down this axis the molecules form open six-sided rings.

Ice floats

Ice is less dense, so it floats. Water grows about 9% when it freezes.

A hard freeze

This can burst water pipes in a hard freeze.

Rule

Hydrogen bonds hold ice in open rings. So ice is less dense than liquid water, and it floats.

Check

100.0 cm3 of water freezes at 0 °C. About what volume of ice forms?

  1. Aabout 109 cm3
  2. B100.0 cm3
  3. Cabout 92 cm3

A. Ice is less dense. The same mass takes about 9% more space.

Hexagonal ice (data notes: ideal oxygen framework, O···O 276 pm, hydrogen directions simplified). Densities at 0 °C (CRC): ice 0.9167 g/cm³, liquid water 0.99984 g/cm³; a floating block shows 8.3% above the surface; 100.0 cm³ of water becomes 109.1 cm³ of ice, the same 99.98 g. Melting collapses the open rings, so liquid water is denser. The Texas freeze case file uses this slide's model for the burst pipes of February 2021.

Does it conduct?

A current is a flow of charge. Something charged must move.

Solid salt. Lights or stays dark?

The ions are locked in the crystal. They cannot move.

Molten salt, 801 °C. Lights or stays dark?

Now the ions move. They carry the current.

Salt water: ions move among water molecules. An electrolyte.

Copper: delocalized electrons move.

Sugar water: whole molecules, no charge. Dark.

Distilled water: very few ions. Dark.

A substance conducts when it has charged particles that can move.

Ions in a liquid or a solution.

Delocalized electrons in a metal.

Check: Why does molten NaCl conduct, while solid NaCl does not?

  1. AIn the liquid, the ions are free to move.
  2. BMelting turns the atoms into ions.
  3. CMelting frees electrons from sodium.

A. The ions were there all along. Melting lets them move.

Atom colors show the element, not the charge.

The reading's conductivity table: solid NaCl (Na⁺ and Cl⁻ locked: dark), molten NaCl (lights), salt water (lights), sugar water (no charged particles: dark), copper (delocalized electrons: lights), distilled water (very few ions: dark). A substance that forms ions in water, like NaCl, is an electrolyte. The magnified circle draws the ions to scale (Shannon); water molecules in CPK colors share the circle with the ions, hence the caption. Nothing is demonstrated: molten salt is above 801 °C.

Some ionic and some molecular substances dissolve in water. Some do not. The type of bond alone does not predict it.

Malleable or brittle

Copper and salt: both are hard.

Which one breaks under a sharp blow?

The hammer strikes the top layers of both.

Copper: the layers slide, the electrons move with them.

Salt: shifted layers put like charges side by side.

Copper bends: malleable. Salt splits: brittle.

Molecular solids, like iodine, are soft. Only weak attractions hold them.

Ion and atom sizesNa+ 102 pmCl− 181 pmCu 128 pm

A metal bends because its electrons move with the shifting cations.

An ionic crystal splits because shifted layers put like charges side by side.

Check: Why does a salt crystal split when it is struck hard?

  1. AShifted layers put like charges together, and they repel.
  2. BIonic bonds are weak.
  3. CThe electrons flow out of the crystal.

A. The repulsion pushes the layers apart along a flat face.

Atom colors show the element, not the charge.

The reading's hammer model (§4.5.5): both blocks to one scale, Na⁺ 102 pm, Cl⁻ 181 pm at 282 pm, Cu 128 pm. A metal bends because its electrons move with the shifting cations; an ionic crystal splits because shifted layers put like charges side by side. Molecular solids (iodine, dry ice) are usually soft: only weak attractions hold them. Nothing is demonstrated.

Covalent network solids

Atom colors show the element, not the charge.

Dry ice

Dry ice is solid CO2: separate molecules.

Predict

CO2 and SiO2 have similar formulas. Do they behave the same?

−78.5 °C

At −78.5 °C the molecules fly off, whole. No liquid.

Quartz

Quartz, SiO2: covalent bonds join every atom. No molecules.

Heat it

To melt it, covalent bonds must break. Near 1700 °C.

Diamond

Diamond: a network of carbon atoms. The hardest natural substance.

Rule

In a covalent network solid, covalent bonds join every atom to its neighbors. To melt it, covalent bonds must break. So it melts only when very hot.

Check

Which substance is a covalent network solid?

  1. Aquartz, SiO2
  2. Bdry ice, CO2
  3. Ciodine, I2

A. Covalent bonds join every atom in quartz. It melts only near 1700 °C.

Dry ice sublimes at −78.5 °C: no liquid at 1 atm (never give CO₂ a boiling point). Quartz, SiO₂, melts near 1700 °C (sources 1710 to 1722 °C); its fragment is idealized with straight Si–O–Si. Most covalent network solids are very hard; diamond is the hardest natural substance. Teacher only, the exception: graphite conducts along its layers (one delocalized electron per carbon), and its layers are held by dispersion forces only, so it is soft. Atom colors are CPK: C dark gray, O red, Si tan.
