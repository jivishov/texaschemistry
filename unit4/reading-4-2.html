4.2

Ionic compounds: formulas and names

Copper combines with oxygen in two different ways, and the two compounds look nothing alike. Copper(I) oxide is a red powder, and most of the antifouling paints that keep barnacles off the hulls of boats contain it. Copper(II) oxide is a black powder that potters use to color glazes and ceramics. Both compounds contain only copper ions and oxide ions, yet their formulas, Cu₂O and CuO, are different. The difference comes from the electric charge on the copper ion, and each name records that charge. This chapter explains how the charges of ions determine both the formula and the name of an ionic compound.Copper joins with oxygen in two different ways. The two compounds look very different. Copper(I) oxide is a red powder used in boat paint. The paint keeps barnacles off the boat. Copper(II) oxide is a black powder used to color pottery. Both contain copper ions and oxide ions. Yet their formulas are different: Cu₂O and CuO. The charge on the copper ion makes the difference. This chapter shows how charges decide the formula and the name.

§4.2.1

Charges from the group number

Lesson 4.1 showed that a metal atom loses valence electrons when it forms an ion, while a nonmetal atom gains them. For a , the group number predicts how many electrons change hands. Metal atoms in groups 1, 2 and 13 lose their one, two or three valence electrons. They become with charges of 1+, 2+ and 3+. The ions of sodium, magnesium and aluminum all have the electron arrangement of neon, the noble gas just before them.Lesson 4.1 showed that metal atoms lose electrons and nonmetal atoms gain them. The group number predicts how many. A group 1 atom loses one electron, so its ion is 1+. A group 2 atom loses two electrons, so its ion is 2+. An aluminum atom in group 13 loses three electrons, so its ion is 3+.

A nonmetal atom gains electrons until it reaches eight valence electrons. Atoms in groups 15, 16 and 17 already have five, six and seven. They gain three, two and one electron and form with charges of 3−, 2− and 1−. An ion that contains a single atom is called a . A monatomic anion is named with the root of the element's name followed by the ending -ide, so chlorine becomes chloride and oxygen becomes oxide.A nonmetal atom gains electrons until it has eight outer electrons. A group 15 atom gains three electrons, so its ion is 3−. A group 16 atom gains two, so its ion is 2−. A group 17 atom gains one, so its ion is 1−. An ion made from one atom is a monatomic ion. Its name ends in -ide, so chlorine becomes chloride.

Ionization energies explain why magnesium stops after losing two electrons. Removing its first and second electrons requires 738 and 1451 kJ/mol, but removing a third would require 7733 kJ/mol, because that electron would come from the filled energy level underneath. Consequently, magnesium forms Mg²⁺ in its compounds and never Mg³⁺. Groups 14 and 18 are left out of this pattern: carbon and silicon rarely form simple ions, and the noble gases rarely form compounds.Energy explains why magnesium stops at two electrons. Removing a third electron would take about five times more energy than the second. So magnesium always forms Mg²⁺ and never Mg³⁺. Groups 14 and 18 are left out. Carbon and silicon rarely form simple ions. Noble gases rarely form compounds.

Key idea

Metals in groups 1, 2 and 13 form ions of 1+, 2+ and 3+. Groups 15, 16 and 17 form ions of 3−, 2− and 1−. A monatomic anion's name ends in -ide.

§4.2.1

Ions of transition metals

A does not follow the group-number rule. Many transition metals form more than one common ion, because their atoms can lose different numbers of electrons under different conditions. Iron forms both Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, and copper forms both Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺. The two copper oxides described in the opener are different compounds for exactly this reason.Transition metals do not follow the group rule. Many of them can form more than one ion. Iron forms Fe²⁺ and also Fe³⁺. Copper forms Cu⁺ and also Cu²⁺. That is the reason for the two different copper oxides.

Other transition metals form only one common ion. Silver forms Ag⁺ and zinc forms Zn²⁺, but neither charge can be predicted from the group number, so memorize them together with the ions of iron and copper. Section 4.2.3 explains how the name of a compound identifies which ion of iron or copper it contains.Some transition metals form only one common ion. Silver forms Ag⁺, and zinc forms Zn²⁺. The group number does not predict these charges, so memorize them. Section 4.2.3 shows how a name tells which iron or copper ion is present.

Rust and the iron ore hematite both contain Fe³⁺ ions. Iron also forms Fe²⁺, the ion found in the iron(II) sulfate that is sold as a supplement for plants. Because a single element can carry either charge, it can produce several different compounds with the same partner element.Rust and the iron ore hematite contain Fe³⁺ ions. Iron also forms Fe²⁺ ions. Some plant fertilizer contains an Fe²⁺ compound. So one element can make several different compounds.

Key idea

Iron (Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺) and copper (Cu⁺, Cu²⁺) each form two common ions. Silver (Ag⁺) and zinc (Zn²⁺) each form one.

§4.2.2

Balancing the charges

An is made of cations and anions, and its total charge is always zero. Therefore the positive charge of its cations must exactly equal the negative charge of its anions. In calcium chloride, each Ca²⁺ ion carries a charge of 2+, while each Cl⁻ ion carries only 1−. The compound therefore needs two chloride ions for every calcium ion.An ionic compound contains cations and anions. Its total charge is always zero. So the positive charges must equal the negative charges. In calcium chloride, each calcium ion is 2+. Each chloride ion is only 1−. So there are two chloride ions for every calcium ion.

The tile model makes this balance visible. Each ion is represented by a tile whose length equals the size of its charge, with cations arranged in the top row and anions in the row below. When the two rows have the same length, the positive and negative charges balance. For calcium chloride, one Ca²⁺ tile exactly matches two Cl⁻ tiles.The tile model makes the balance visible. Each ion is a tile, and its length is its charge. Cations go in the top row, and anions go below. When the rows are equal in length, the charges balance. One calcium tile matches two chloride tiles.

The smallest collection of ions with a total charge of zero is called one . A real crystal contains an enormous number of formula units, and every part of the crystal has the same ratio of ions. The formula of an ionic compound represents that ratio, not a separate particle.The smallest group of ions with zero total charge is one formula unit. A crystal contains a huge number of formula units. Every part of the crystal has the same ratio of ions. The formula shows that ratio.

Key idea

The total charge of an ionic compound is zero. Find the smallest numbers of cations and anions whose charges add to zero.

§4.2.2

Writing the formula

A combines element symbols with subscripts. The cation is written first and the anion second. Each tells how many of the ion just before it are present, and a subscript of 1 is never written. Two Al³⁺ ions and three O²⁻ ions therefore give the formula Al₂O₃.A chemical formula uses element symbols and small numbers. Write the cation first and the anion second. Each small number counts the ion just before it. A count of 1 is never written. Two Al³⁺ and three O²⁻ give Al₂O₃.

A formula always shows the lowest whole-number ratio of ions. Two Mg²⁺ ions and two O²⁻ ions have a total charge of zero, but the ratio 2 : 2 reduces to 1 : 1. For that reason, the formula of magnesium oxide is MgO rather than Mg₂O₂.A formula always uses the smallest whole numbers. Two Mg²⁺ and two O²⁻ do balance each other. But the ratio 2 : 2 reduces to 1 : 1. So magnesium oxide is MgO, not Mg₂O₂.

A shortcut called the crisscross method uses the size of each ion's charge as the subscript of the other ion. For Al³⁺ and O²⁻, the crisscross produces Al₂O₃ directly. For Mg²⁺ and O²⁻, however, it produces Mg₂O₂, which still has to be reduced to MgO. Use the shortcut only to check a formula that you have already balanced.One useful shortcut is called the crisscross. Each ion's charge becomes the other ion's small number. For Al³⁺ and O²⁻, it gives Al₂O₃ directly. For Mg²⁺ and O²⁻, it gives Mg₂O₂. That answer must still be reduced to MgO. Use the shortcut only to check your answer.

Key idea

Write the cation first and the anion second. The subscripts are the numbers of ions in the lowest whole-number ratio: MgO, not Mg₂O₂.

§4.2.3

Naming binary ionic compounds

A contains exactly two elements. To name a binary ionic compound, name the cation first and the anion second. A main-group cation keeps the name of its element, and the anion takes the ending -ide, so NaCl is sodium chloride and K₂S is potassium sulfide. These conventions come from , the international organization that sets the naming rules chemists follow worldwide.A binary compound contains two elements. Name the cation first, using the element's name. Then name the anion, which ends in -ide. NaCl is sodium chloride, and K₂S is potassium sulfide. These rules come from IUPAC. This group sets the naming rules for chemists everywhere.

Common names for ionic compounds usually leave out prefixes such as di- and tri-. The charges of the ions already determine the ratio, so CaCl₂ is simply calcium chloride. IUPAC also accepts a name that counts atoms with prefixes, calcium dichloride, but the shorter name is the one in common use. Lesson 4.3 does need prefixes, because two nonmetals can combine in several different ratios.Ionic names usually skip prefixes like di- and tri-. The charges already decide the ratio. So CaCl₂ is simply calcium chloride. IUPAC also accepts the name calcium dichloride. But the shorter name is the usual one. Lesson 4.3 uses prefixes for compounds of two nonmetals.

Names follow composition. Aluminum chloride, AlCl₃, has an electronegativity difference of only 1.55, so Lesson 4.1's guide calls its bonds polar covalent. It is still named like an ionic compound, because it is a metal combined with a nonmetal.Names follow what a compound is made of. In AlCl₃, the electronegativity difference is only 1.55. So its bonds are polar covalent. It is still named like an ionic compound, because it is a metal joined to a nonmetal.

Key idea

Name the cation, then the anion. A monatomic anion ends in -ide. The common name uses no prefixes, because the charges fix the ratio.

§4.2.3

The Stock system

The name iron chloride could describe either FeCl₂ or FeCl₃, because iron forms two common ions. The removes this ambiguity by stating the charge of the metal ion as a Roman numeral in parentheses, placed directly after the metal's name with no space. FeCl₂ is therefore iron(II) chloride, and FeCl₃ is iron(III) chloride. The German chemist Alfred Stock proposed this kind of name in 1919.The name iron chloride could mean FeCl₂ or FeCl₃. Iron forms two ions, so the name must say which one. The Stock system gives the charge as a Roman numeral. FeCl₂ is iron(II) chloride, and FeCl₃ is iron(III) chloride. The chemist Alfred Stock proposed these names in 1919.

ironthe metal(III)its ion is 3+chloridethe anion, Cl⁻→FeCl₃three Cl⁻ per Fe³⁺

The numeral always represents the charge of one metal ion. It is never a count of atoms, and it never appears as a subscript. To find the numeral from a formula, work backward from the anion. In CuO, a single O²⁻ carries 2−, so the copper ion must be 2+ and the name is copper(II) oxide. In Cu₂O, two copper ions together balance 2−, so each one is 1+ and the name is copper(I) oxide.The numeral is the charge of one metal ion. It never counts the atoms in a formula. To find it, begin with the anion. In CuO, the oxide ion is 2−, so the copper ion is 2+. The name is copper(II) oxide. In Cu₂O, two copper ions share 2+. So each copper ion is 1+, and the name is copper(I) oxide.

Only a metal that forms more than one common ion takes a numeral. Metals in groups 1 and 2 never need one, and neither do aluminum, silver and zinc, which each form a single common ion. Accordingly, AgCl is silver chloride and ZnO is zinc oxide, with no numeral in either name.Only metals with more than one common ion take a numeral. Metals in groups 1 and 2 do not need one. Aluminum, silver and zinc do not need one either. AgCl is silver chloride, and ZnO is zinc oxide.

Key idea

In the Stock system, the Roman numeral is the charge of the metal ion. Find it by working back from the anion. Only metals with more than one common ion take one.

Honors · Older names, and tin and lead

Before the Stock system, chemists named the two ions of a metal with endings on its Latin name: -ous for the lower charge and -ic for the higher. Iron(II) was ferrous and iron(III) ferric, and copper(I) was cuprous and copper(II) cupric. Tin and lead also form two common ions each, Sn²⁺ and Sn⁴⁺, and Pb²⁺ and Pb⁴⁺: stannous and stannic, plumbous and plumbic. The old names survive on labels. The stannous fluoride, SnF₂, in some toothpastes is tin(II) fluoride. IUPAC names use numerals, because an ending tells only which charge is lower, not what the charge is.

§4.2.4

Polyatomic ions

Some ions are made of several atoms rather than one. A is a group of atoms held together by covalent bonds that carries an overall electric charge. The group behaves as a single ion, and it keeps all of its atoms together in every compound it forms.Some ions contain several atoms. A polyatomic ion is a group of atoms joined by covalent bonds. The whole group carries one charge. It behaves like a single ion. Its atoms stay together in every compound.

This course uses six polyatomic ions. Ammonium, NH₄⁺, is the only positive one, and four of the six names end in -ate, a sign that those ions contain oxygen. Pay close attention to the endings: nitrate, NO₃⁻, is a completely different ion from nitride, N³⁻, and sulfate, SO₄²⁻, is different from sulfide, S²⁻.This course uses six polyatomic ions. Ammonium, NH₄⁺, is the only positive one. Four names end in -ate, and those ions contain oxygen. Read the endings carefully. Nitrate, NO₃⁻, is not nitride, N³⁻. Sulfate, SO₄²⁻, is not sulfide, S²⁻.

§4.2.4

Formulas with polyatomic ions

Compounds of polyatomic ions follow the same rules as compounds of monatomic ions. The cation is written first, the total charge is zero, and the name combines the names of the two ions. NaOH is sodium hydroxide, NH₄Cl is ammonium chloride, and CaCO₃, the principal mineral in limestone, is calcium carbonate.Polyatomic ions follow the same rules. Write the cation first, and make the total charge zero. Then combine the two ion names. NaOH is sodium hydroxide. NH₄Cl is ammonium chloride. CaCO₃ is calcium carbonate, the main mineral in limestone.

When a formula needs more than one of a polyatomic ion, enclose the ion in parentheses and write the subscript outside them. Calcium hydroxide contains two OH⁻ ions for every Ca²⁺ ion, so its formula is Ca(OH)₂. Without the parentheses, CaOH₂ would describe one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms. Ammonium sulfate, a common fertilizer, contains two NH₄⁺ ions for each SO₄²⁻ ion: (NH₄)₂SO₄.Sometimes a formula needs two or more of a polyatomic ion. Put that ion inside parentheses. Write the count outside them. Calcium hydroxide has two OH⁻ ions, so it is Ca(OH)₂. The formula CaOH₂ would be wrong. Ammonium sulfate, a fertilizer, is (NH₄)₂SO₄.

Never change the subscripts inside a polyatomic ion, because they define which ion it is. SO₃²⁻, with one fewer oxygen atom, is a different ion called sulfite. Parentheses are unnecessary when the formula contains only one of the ion, as in NaNO₃ or CaSO₄.Never change the small numbers inside a polyatomic ion. SO₃²⁻ is a different ion from SO₄²⁻. Use no parentheses when there is only one of the ion, as in NaNO₃.

Key idea

Treat a polyatomic ion as one unit. Put it in parentheses when the formula needs more than one of it, and never change the subscripts inside it.

§4.2.5

From name to formula

A name provides all the information needed to write a formula. First, write each ion with its charge, using the group number, the Roman numeral or the table of polyatomic ions. Second, find the smallest numbers of ions whose charges add to zero. Third, write the formula with the cation first, and place parentheses around any polyatomic ion that appears more than once.A name gives you everything you need. First, write each ion with its charge. Use the group number, the numeral or the ion table. Second, find the fewest ions that make the total zero. Third, write the formula with the cation first. Add parentheses when they are needed.

Converting a formula into a name follows the same steps in reverse. Identify the anion and its charge. If the metal forms more than one common ion, calculate its charge from the requirement that the total must equal zero. Finally, name the cation, including any numeral, and then the anion.To name a formula, follow the steps in reverse. First, identify the anion and its charge. If the metal has two common ions, use the anion to find its charge. Then name the cation and the anion.

The two directions provide a check on each other. After writing a formula, name it again and compare the result with the original name. After naming a formula, write the formula again from the name. If the two versions disagree, one of the steps contains an error.The two directions check each other. After you write a formula, name it again. After you name a formula, write it again from the name. If the two do not match, look for the mistake.

Key idea

Name to formula: ions, balance, write. Formula to name: find the anion's charge, work back to the metal's charge if it needs a numeral, then name both ions.
