4.3

Covalent compounds: names and Lewis structures

Dry air is 78% nitrogen gas, N₂, yet most plants cannot use this nitrogen directly to grow. The two atoms in each N₂ molecule share three pairs of electrons, which makes the bond between them very difficult to break. Factories break it with a catalyst at high temperature and pressure to manufacture ammonia, NH₃. About 88% of the ammonia produced in the United States becomes fertilizer for crops. The formulas N₂ and NH₃ identify which atoms each molecule contains. A (electron dot structure) also shows the pairs of electrons that hold those atoms together. This chapter explains how to name molecular compounds and how to draw their Lewis structures.Dry air is 78% nitrogen gas, N₂. Most plants cannot use this nitrogen on their own. The two nitrogen atoms share three pairs of electrons. That bond between them is very hard to break. Factories break it to make ammonia, NH₃, for fertilizer. A formula tells which atoms are in a molecule. A Lewis structure (electron dot structure) also shows the shared pairs. This chapter names molecules and draws these structures.

§4.3.1

Prefixes count the atoms

A is made of molecules, in which atoms of nonmetals are joined by . Two nonmetals can often combine in more than one ratio. Carbon and oxygen form both CO and CO₂, and nitrogen and oxygen form several different compounds. A name such as nitrogen oxide cannot distinguish among these compounds, so a molecular name must count the atoms of each element.A molecular compound is made of separate molecules. Its nonmetal atoms are joined by covalent bonds. Two nonmetals can often join in different ratios. Carbon and oxygen form both CO and CO₂. So a molecular name must count the atoms.

To name a of two nonmetals, write the full name of the first element. Then write the root of the second element's name followed by the ending -ide, as in the name of an anion. A placed in front of each part gives the number of atoms of that element. Accordingly, CO₂ is carbon dioxide, and N₂O₄ is dinitrogen tetraoxide.First, write the whole name of the first element. Next, give the second element the ending -ide. Then put a prefix in front of each part. Each prefix counts the atoms of that element. So CO₂ is called carbon dioxide. N₂O₄ is called dinitrogen tetraoxide.

Two additional rules keep the names of molecular compounds short. Mono- is omitted from the first element, so CO₂ is carbon dioxide rather than monocarbon dioxide. The second element always receives a prefix, even mono-, so CO is carbon monoxide. keeps every letter when a prefix meets a vowel, except that mono and oxide combine as monoxide. Key idea

Name the first element, then the second with -ide. A prefix counts the atoms of each, but mono- is left off the first element.

§4.3.1

Molecular formulas and accepted names

A molecular formula counts the atoms in one molecule. Unlike an ionic formula, it is never reduced to the lowest whole-number ratio. NO₂ and N₂O₄ contain nitrogen and oxygen atoms in the same ratio, 1 : 2, yet they are different substances. Nitrogen dioxide is a reddish-brown gas, while dinitrogen tetraoxide is colorless.A molecular formula counts the atoms in one molecule. It is never reduced, as an ionic formula is. NO₂ and N₂O₄ have the same 1 : 2 ratio, yet they are different substances. NO₂ is a reddish-brown gas, and N₂O₄ has no color.

Each N₂O₄ molecule consists of two NO₂ units connected by a bond between their nitrogen atoms. In a sealed tube, the two gases are always present together. Cooling the tube joins more NO₂ units into N₂O₄ molecules, so the brown color gradually fades. Writing NO₂ for both substances would hide this important difference.One N₂O₄ molecule is two NO₂ units joined together. A sealed tube always holds some of each gas. Cooling the tube joins more NO₂ units into N₂O₄. Then the brown color in the tube fades.

Some molecular compounds were familiar long before the modern naming rules existed, and they keep their accepted names. H₂O is water, NH₃ is ammonia, and CH₄ is methane, the principal component of natural gas. Chemists use these accepted names instead of prefix names.Some compounds were known before the naming rules. They still keep their older names, called accepted names. H₂O is water, and NH₃ is ammonia. CH₄ is methane, the main gas in natural gas. Use these names instead of prefix names.

Key idea

A molecular formula counts the atoms in one molecule, so it is never reduced. Water, ammonia and methane keep their accepted names.

Drawing a molecule begins with counting all of its valence electrons. Add the valence electrons of every atom, and count each atom separately, even when an element appears more than once. Water, H₂O, therefore has 1 + 1 + 6 = 8 valence electrons, and carbon dioxide, CO₂, has 4 + 6 + 6 = 16.To draw a molecule, first count its valence electrons. Add up the electrons of every single atom. Count each atom, even when an element repeats. Water has 1 + 1 + 6 = 8 valence electrons. Carbon dioxide has 4 + 6 + 6 = 16 of them.

This total remains fixed while you draw, because forming bonds neither creates nor destroys electrons. A correct Lewis structure therefore displays exactly that number, no more and no fewer. §4.3.2

Bonding pairs and lone pairs

A Lewis structure arranges the valence electrons in pairs. A is shared by two atoms and is drawn as a line between their symbols. A belongs to a single atom and is drawn as two dots beside its symbol. Each line and each pair of dots represents 2 electrons.A Lewis structure shows the electrons in pairs. A bonding pair is shared by two atoms. It is drawn as a line between them. A lone pair belongs to just one atom. It is drawn as two dots beside the atom. Each line or pair of dots means 2 electrons.

Water displays both kinds of pair, because its oxygen atom shares one bonding pair with each hydrogen atom and also keeps two lone pairs. Ammonia has three bonding pairs and one lone pair, whereas methane has four bonding pairs and no lone pairs at all.Water has both kinds of pairs. Its oxygen shares one pair with each hydrogen. The oxygen also keeps two lone pairs. Ammonia has three bonding pairs and one lone pair. Methane has four bonding pairs and no lone pairs.

To count the electrons surrounding one atom, add its bonding pairs and lone pairs at 2 electrons each. A shared pair counts for both of the atoms that share it. Around the oxygen atom in water, two bonding pairs and two lone pairs make 8 electrons, and each hydrogen atom has 2.To count electrons around one atom, add up its pairs. Each pair counts as 2 electrons. A shared pair counts for both of its atoms. Water's oxygen has four pairs, which make 8 electrons. Each hydrogen atom in water has 2.

Key idea

A line is a bonding pair, shared by two atoms. Two dots are a lone pair, held by one atom. Each stands for 2 electrons.

§4.3.3

Drawing with single bonds

A Lewis structure can be drawn in four steps. First, count the valence electrons. Second, bond: connect each outer atom to the with a , which is one bonding pair. Third, fill: place the remaining electrons as lone pairs, completing the outer atoms first. Fourth, share: if an atom still has fewer than 8 electrons, convert a neighboring lone pair into another bond.You can draw a Lewis structure in four steps. First, count all of the valence electrons. Second, join the outer atoms to the central atom. Each of these single bonds uses 2 electrons. Third, place the rest as lone pairs, outer atoms first. Fourth, if an atom is still short, share another pair.

For ammonia, the count is 5 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 8 valence electrons. Three N–H bonds use 6 of them, which leaves 2. Hydrogen atoms never hold lone pairs, so the final pair goes on nitrogen. Nitrogen then has 8 electrons around it and each hydrogen has 2, so the sharing step is unnecessary.Ammonia has 5 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 8 valence electrons. Its three bonds use 6, and that leaves 2. Hydrogen atoms never hold any lone pairs. So the last pair goes on the nitrogen atom. Now nitrogen has 8, and each hydrogen has 2. So ammonia does not need the share step.

The central atom is the atom bonded to the greatest number of other atoms. It is usually the element written only once in the formula, such as N in NH₃ or C in CH₄. Hydrogen is never central, because a single shared pair completes it and it therefore forms only one bond.The central atom is bonded to the most atoms. It is often the element written once in the formula. For example, N is the central atom in NH₃. Hydrogen is never the central atom in a molecule. One shared pair fills it, so it forms one bond.

Key idea

Count, bond, fill, share. Single bonds join the outer atoms to the central atom, and the electrons left become lone pairs, outer atoms first.

§4.3.3

Octets and duets

In most stable molecules, every atom other than hydrogen has 8 valence electrons around it, counting both its bonding pairs and its lone pairs. This pattern is called the . A set of 8 is an , the same arrangement found in every noble gas except helium.In most molecules, each atom has 8 electrons around it. Count both its bonding pairs and its lone pairs. This pattern is called the octet rule. A set of 8 electrons is called an octet. Most noble gases have an octet as well.

Hydrogen is the important exception, because its first energy level holds only 2 electrons. A hydrogen atom in a molecule has a , one shared pair, matching helium. For this reason, hydrogen forms exactly one bond and never carries lone pairs.Hydrogen is the one exception to this rule. Its first energy level holds only 2 electrons. So hydrogen has a duet, the same 2 electrons that helium has. That is why hydrogen forms just one bond. It never has any lone pairs of its own.

A finished structure must pass two checks. The electrons shown in the drawing must equal the valence electrons counted in the first step. In addition, every atom must have 8 electrons around it, or 2 for hydrogen. Section 4.3.4 explains what to do when the electrons run out before every atom reaches 8.A finished drawing has to pass two checks. It must show every valence electron, and no extra ones. Each atom must have 8, or 2 for hydrogen. Section 4.3.4 shows how to fix an atom that is short.

Key idea

Octet rule: in most molecules, each atom has 8 valence electrons around it. Hydrogen has a duet of 2, so it forms one bond and has no lone pairs.

§4.3.4

Double bonds

Sometimes the electrons run out before every atom has an octet. In carbon dioxide, two C–O single bonds use 4 of the 16 valence electrons. The remaining 12 give each oxygen atom three lone pairs. Each oxygen then has 8 electrons around it, but the carbon atom has only 4.Sometimes an atom is still short at the end. CO₂ has 16 valence electrons in all. Its two single bonds use 4 of them. The other 12 go on the two oxygen atoms. Now each oxygen atom has 8 electrons. But the carbon atom has only 4.

The solution moves electrons without adding any. One lone pair on each oxygen atom becomes a second shared pair between that oxygen and the carbon. Each connection is now a , consisting of two bonding pairs drawn as two lines. Carbon then has 8 electrons around it, and each oxygen still has 8.The fix moves electrons but does not add any. Each oxygen shares one of its lone pairs with carbon. Each link then becomes a double bond. A double bond is two shared pairs, drawn as two lines. Now every atom in CO₂ has 8 electrons.

The total remains 16 throughout, since the finished CO₂ structure has four bonding pairs and four lone pairs, which together make 8 pairs, or 16 electrons.The total stays at 16 the whole time. CO₂ ends with four bonding pairs and four lone pairs. That makes 8 pairs, which is 16 electrons.

Key idea

When an atom is short of an octet, a neighbor's lone pair becomes another shared pair. A double bond is two shared pairs.

§4.3.4

Triple bonds

Some molecules require two additional shared pairs, and nitrogen gas, N₂, with 10 valence electrons, is one of them. A single bond uses 2 of them, and the remaining 8 can give only one of the two nitrogen atoms an octet. Sharing two more pairs creates a , three bonding pairs drawn as three lines. Each nitrogen atom then keeps one lone pair and has 8 electrons around it.Some molecules need two extra shared pairs. N₂ has 10 valence electrons in all. One single bond uses 2 of them. The 8 left can fill only one nitrogen atom. So the atoms share two more pairs. That makes a triple bond, three shared pairs. Each nitrogen keeps one lone pair and has 8.

This triple bond is extremely difficult to break, which is one reason the nitrogen in air is so unreactive. Factories that manufacture ammonia break it with an iron catalyst at 400 to 500 °C and at 150 to 250 times normal air pressure.This triple bond is very hard to break. That is one reason nitrogen in air rarely reacts. Ammonia factories break it with heat, pressure and a catalyst.

The number of lines between two atoms identifies the bond. One line is a single bond, two lines are a double bond, and three lines are a triple bond. Every line counts as 2 electrons for each of the two atoms it connects.Count the lines between two atoms to name the bond. One line between two atoms is a single bond. Two lines make a double bond, and three make a triple bond. Each line counts as 2 electrons for both atoms.

Key idea

A triple bond is three shared pairs. In N₂, each nitrogen atom also keeps one lone pair, so each has 8 electrons around it.

§4.3.5

What a Lewis structure shows

A Lewis structure is a model that records which atoms are bonded, how many pairs each bond contains and where the lone pairs are located. It does not show the angles between bonds or the sizes of the atoms. Real electrons are not fixed dots, either; they spread out around the atoms.A Lewis structure is a model of a molecule. It shows which atoms are bonded to each other. It shows the pairs in each bond and the lone pairs. It does not show bond angles or atom sizes. Real electrons are not fixed dots on a page.

A 3D model adds what the drawing leaves out. In ammonia, the three hydrogen atoms sit below the nitrogen atom like the legs of a short stool. The H–N–H angles measure 106.7°, and the lone pair on nitrogen occupies the space above. Lesson 4.4 explains how these shapes arise.A 3D model shows what the drawing leaves out. In ammonia, three hydrogen atoms sit below the nitrogen. They look like the legs of a short stool. The angle between two bonds is 106.7°. The lone pair takes up the space above nitrogen.

The drawings in this chapter come from the work of Gilbert N. Lewis. Lesson 4.1 described his 1916 proposal that two atoms can share a pair of electrons. In the same paper, Lewis wrote molecules with dots for valence electrons and pictured a full set of 8 as the corners of a cube. Irving Langmuir extended this work in 1919, and the octet rule grew out of both.These drawings come from the chemist Gilbert N. Lewis. In 1916, he proposed that atoms share electron pairs. He drew valence electrons as dots around symbols. He pictured a full set of 8 as a cube's corners. Irving Langmuir built on his work in 1919.

Key idea

A Lewis structure shows the bonds and lone pairs, not the shape or the size of a molecule. A 3D model adds the angles.

Honors · Exceptions to the octet rule

The octet rule is a pattern, and a few stable molecules break it. In boron trifluoride, BF₃, boron has only 6 electrons around it. In sulfur hexafluoride, SF₆, sulfur shares six pairs, 12 electrons. Nitrogen dioxide, NO₂, has 17 valence electrons. An odd number cannot all be paired, so one electron in NO₂ is unpaired. Two NO₂ molecules can join through those unpaired electrons, which is how N₂O₄ forms.
