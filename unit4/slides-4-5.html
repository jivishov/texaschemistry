        Forces · Textbook §4.5.1 · SEP 2(A)

          Bonds and attractions

          Within

          Covalent, ionic and metallic bonds hold atoms or ions together within a substance.

          Between

          Separate molecules attract each other through much weaker intermolecular forces.

          1. Within · 2 min. The three kinds of bond come from Lesson 4.1. The TEKS call all three intramolecular forces. Sodium chloride and copper have no molecules, so the lesson calls them bonds within a substance.
          2. Between · 2 min. Point to chlorine: one covalent bond joins the two atoms of each Cl2 molecule, and dashed lines mark the attractions between molecules. Sodium chloride and copper have bonds throughout, and no separate molecules.
          3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: neighboring molecules are held together by covalent bonds.

            Forces · Textbook §4.5.1

              Dispersion forces

              Uneven electrons

              Electrons are always moving. For an instant, one end of a molecule is slightly negative, and the other slightly positive.

              More electrons

              These brief charges attract neighbors. More electrons give stronger dispersion forces.

              History

              Fritz London explained these forces in 1930. They act between all molecules, polar or not.

              1. Uneven electrons · 2 min. The brief charges shift the electrons of a neighboring molecule and attract it. Each attraction lasts only an instant, but new ones form all the time.
              2. More electrons · 1 min. The halogens from Unit 3 are the evidence. All four are nonpolar, so dispersion forces are their only attractions. The models are drawn to one scale from van der Waals radii (Alvarez, 2013).
              3. History · 1 min. London's paper appeared in 1930, which is why the forces are often called London dispersion forces.

              Forces · Textbook §4.5.1 · SEP 2(B)

                Halogen boiling points

                Plot

                Click each point, from F2 to I2.

                Trend

                The boiling point rises from −188.1 °C to 184.4 °C as the electrons grow from 18 to 106.

                1. Plot · 2 min. Each point names its substance, electrons, boiling point and strongest attraction. Fluorine and chlorine lie below the line at 25 °C, so both are gases at room temperature.
                2. Trend · 1 min. Bromine is a liquid and iodine a solid at room temperature. The rising trend is the first evidence for dispersion forces.
                3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: bromine boils higher because boiling breaks its Br–Br bond.

                Forces · Textbook §4.5.2

                  Dipole-dipole forces

                  Polar molecules

                  The δ+ end of one polar molecule attracts the δ− end of the next. These are dipole-dipole forces.

                  Same electrons

                  F2 and HCl each have 18 electrons. Polar HCl boils about 100 °C higher.

                  1. Polar molecules · 2 min. From Lesson 4.4: a polar molecule has a δ− end and a δ+ end. Polar molecules have dispersion forces as well, so dipole-dipole forces add to them. The δ labels are drawn from electronegativity, not a computed charge map.
                  2. Same electrons · 1 min. F2 boils at −188.1 °C and HCl at −85.1 °C (PubChem). With equal electrons, the dipoles help explain the difference.
                  3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: every molecule that contains H forms hydrogen bonds.

                  Forces · Textbook §4.5.2

                    Hydrogen bonding

                    Two conditions

                    Hydrogen bonding needs an H atom bonded to N, O or F, and a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule.

                    Not a covalent bond

                    A hydrogen bond is an attraction between molecules, only about 5 to 10% as strong as a covalent bond.

                    1. Two conditions · 2 min. N, O and F are so electronegative that an H atom bonded to them carries a large δ+ charge. The small H atom can also come very close to the lone pair of its neighbor. Methane's H atoms are bonded to carbon, so it forms none.
                    2. Not a covalent bond · 1 min. The dashed hydrogen bond is longer and far weaker than the covalent O–H bond. This settles prediction 2. Strength from OpenStax Chemistry 2e, section 10.1.
                    3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: HCl forms hydrogen bonds because it contains hydrogen.

                    Forces · Textbook §4.5.2 · SEP 2(B)

                      Ranking boiling points

                      Ten electrons

                      Methane, ammonia and water each have 10 electrons. Hydrogen bonding raises ammonia and water far above methane.

                      All eight

                      Choose All eight. Find the substance that lies farthest above the halogen trend.

                      Honors · Rank

                      Rank molecules of similar size by their strongest attraction. When sizes differ a lot, check the data: Br2 boils above polar HCl.

                      1. Ten electrons · 2 min. Methane boils at −161.5 °C, ammonia at −33.3 °C and water at 100.0 °C (NIST Chemistry WebBook). Water can form up to four hydrogen bonds per molecule, so it boils highest.
                      2. All eight · 2 min. Water, with only 10 electrons, boils far above the halogen trend.
                      3. Honors · 2 min. Bromine, with dispersion forces only, boils at 58.8 °C, far above HCl at −85.1 °C, because it has 70 electrons to HCl's 18. No type of attraction is always the strongest. Ranking by the strongest attraction works for molecules with similar numbers of electrons.

                        Melting · Textbook §4.5.3 · SEP 1(G)

                          Boiling water

                          Heat water

                          Ice melts at 0.0 °C, and water boils at 100.0 °C, far above methane.

                          Molecules stay whole

                          Steam is still H2O: each molecule keeps both O–H bonds.

                          1. Heat water · 2 min. Step from Solid to Gas, then switch to Methane and compare. Hydrogen bonds hold water molecules more strongly than dispersion forces hold methane molecules.
                          2. Molecules stay whole · 1 min. Click one molecule and follow it into the gas: it still has two O–H bonds. This settles prediction 1.
                          3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: melting or boiling splits water into hydrogen and oxygen.

                          Melting · Textbook §4.5.3 · SEP 2(B)

                            Two energies compared

                            Compare

                            Breaking every O–H bond would take more than 20 times the energy of boiling the water.

                            Explain

                            Use the two energies to explain why boiling does not break the O–H bonds.

                            1. Two energies · 2 min. Boiling 18 g of water at 100 °C takes 40.7 kJ. Breaking all of its O–H bonds would take about 928 kJ, from the average O–H bond energy of 464 kJ/mol (OpenStax Chemistry 2e).
                            2. Compare · 1 min. 928 divided by 40.7 is about 23.
                            3. Explain · 2 min. Only the attractions between molecules must be overcome, so melting and boiling points rise as those attractions grow stronger.

                              Four neighbors

                              In ice, each water molecule forms hydrogen bonds to four neighbors.

                              Open rings

                              The hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open pattern of six-sided rings.

                              Try

                              Step to Open rings, then turn the crystal to look down its tunnels.

                              1. Four neighbors · 2 min. Click the highlighted oxygen atom: its four neighbors sit 276 pm away in a tetrahedral arrangement. Two hydrogen bonds come from its own H atoms, and two reach its lone pairs.
                              2. Open rings · 1 min. Molecules at the edge of the fragment have lost some neighbors, as any piece cut from a crystal must.

                              Melting · Textbook §4.5.3

                                Why ice floats

                                Densities

                                Ice is 0.9167 g/cm³ and liquid water 0.99984 g/cm³ at 0 °C, so ice floats.

                                Expansion

                                Water expands by about 9% as it freezes, enough to split rocks.

                                1. Densities · 2 min. In most substances the solid is denser and sinks. Ice floats with about 8.3% of its volume above the surface. Densities from the CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics.
                                2. Expansion · 1 min. When ice melts, some hydrogen bonds are overcome, the rings collapse and the molecules pack closer. The Unit 4 case file uses this to explain burst pipes in the February 2021 Texas freeze.
                                3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: freezing always makes a substance shrink.

                                  Predict

                                  Test

                                  A current needs charged particles that can move: ions in a liquid or solution, or delocalized electrons in a metal.

                                    Properties · Textbook §4.5.5 · SEP 3(A)

                                      Malleable or brittle

                                      Copper

                                      Electrons shift with the cations, so copper bends. It is malleable.

                                      Salt

                                      A shift puts like charges side by side. They repel, so the crystal splits. It is brittle.

                                      1. Strike · 2 min. Step through Hammer blow and Layers shift. Both solids are hard; the difference shows only when the layers move.
                                      2. Copper · 1 min. Drag the top layers of copper one spacing and let go. Every cation is still surrounded by electrons, so the metallic bonding holds.
                                      3. Salt · 1 min. Drag the top layers of NaCl. As a layer slides, like charges line up and repel, and the crystal splits along a flat face.
                                      4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: salt shatters because ionic bonds are weak.

                                      Properties · Textbook §4.5.5

                                        Covalent network solids

                                        Network

                                        In a covalent network solid, covalent bonds join every atom. Melting it breaks covalent bonds.

                                        An exception: graphite

                                        Graphite conducts along its layers, which slide past one another.

                                        1. Network · 2 min. CO2 and SiO2 have similar formulas. Dry ice is separate CO2 molecules and sublimes at −78.5 °C, an example of sublimation. Quartz is a network and melts near 1700 °C. Diamond is the hardest natural substance.
                                        2. Graphite · 1 min. Each carbon atom bonds to three neighbors, and its fourth valence electron is delocalized across its layer. Only dispersion forces hold the layers together, so graphite is soft.
                                        3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: every solid with a high melting point is ionic.

                                                  A sheet of copper bends under a hammer, while a crystal of sodium chloride splits. Explain both results.

                                                  0:30

                                                  Explain your idea to your partner, then switch.

                                                  1:00

                                                  1. In copper, the cations sit in…
                                                  2. When layers of copper shift…

                                                  malleablebrittledelocalized electronscationlike charges

                                                  Success criteria

                                                  Sample answer

                                                  In copper, the cations sit in layers within a sea of delocalized electrons. When a hammer shifts one layer, the electrons move with the cations, so the metallic bonding holds and the copper bends. In sodium chloride, a shift puts ions beside ions of the same charge. These like charges repel, so the crystal splits. Copper is malleable, and sodium chloride is brittle.

                                                  1. Think · 30 s.
                                                  2. Talk · 1 min.
                                                  3. Write · 2 min. Watch for "ionic bonds are weak", which is not the reason.
                                                  4. Self-check · 30 s.
                                                  5. Compare · 1 min. Salt is hard because its ionic bonds are strong; it is brittle because shifted layers repel.

                                                      Claim

                                                      One sentence that answers the question.

                                                      1. A substance's properties depend on…

                                                      Sample claim

                                                      A substance's properties depend on which forces must be overcome, and on whether its charged particles can move.

                                                      Evidence

                                                      Use ice, salt and copper.

                                                      1. Ice melts at…, while salt…
                                                      2. Copper conducts and bends, while salt…

                                                      Sample evidence

                                                      Ice melts at 0.0 °C, while salt melts at 801 °C. Solid salt does not conduct, but molten salt does. Copper conducts as a solid and bends under a hammer, while salt splits.

                                                      Reasoning

                                                      Explain each property from the forces.

                                                      1. Melting ice loosens…
                                                      2. A current needs…

                                                      Sample reasoning

                                                      Melting ice loosens hydrogen bonds between molecules and leaves the O–H bonds intact, while melting salt frees ions from their fixed places. A current needs charged particles that can move. Copper's electrons move with its shifting layers, but shifted ions in salt repel.
