Chemistry Case Files · Unit 4

Bonding, Nomenclature and Geometry

Five lessons, ten class periods and one case-file period. All assessed content follows C.7(A) to C.7(D).

The package

Each lesson has a reading, a projector slide deck and a missions page. All pages are standalone builds. Student explanations and drawings belong in notebooks. The unit has no worksheets. The portal reference copy is unchanged.

Open the unit index. Build with py -3.13 kit/build.py unit4 from the texaschemistry folder.

Standards

Local TEKS.md, 19 TAC §112.43, Chemistry, adopted 2020 and effective April 28, 2021. Knowledge and skills statement (7): The student knows how atoms form ionic, covalent, and metallic bonds.

C.7(A) construct an argument to support how periodic trends such as electronegativity can predict bonding between elements;

C.7(B) name and write the chemical formulas for ionic and covalent compounds using International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) nomenclature rules;

C.7(C) classify and draw electron dot structures for molecules with linear, bent, trigonal planar, trigonal pyramidal, and tetrahedral molecular geometries as explained by Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) theory; and

C.7(D) analyze the properties of ionic, covalent, and metallic substances in terms of intramolecular and intermolecular forces.

SEPs: 1(G) models; 2(A) model advantages and limitations; 2(B) data patterns; 3(A) explanations; 3(C) scientific argumentation; 4(A) critique. Plan at least 40% of instruction for model use, data analysis, partner explanations and notebook arguments.

4.1 Predicting bond type

Driving question: How can the periodic table predict the kind of bond two elements form?

Objectives

  • 4.1.1 Explain how a metal atom and a nonmetal atom form ions, and describe an ionic bond as the attraction between those ions in a crystal.
  • 4.1.2 Describe a covalent bond as a shared pair of electrons, and use electronegativity to tell a polar covalent bond from a nonpolar one.
  • 4.1.3 Describe metallic bonding with the electron-sea model, and state what the model leaves out.
  • 4.1.4 Use the electronegativity difference and the positions of two elements on the periodic table to predict the type of bond between them.
  • 4.1.5 Write a claim, evidence and reasoning argument that predicts a bond type, and judge how strong the evidence is.

Models

Three models carry the lesson. In the ion-formation model, a sodium atom passes its electron to a chlorine atom and the two ions then settle into a turnable NaCl crystal, so the transfer and the bond are separate steps. The bond explorer takes any two elements from the periodic table and places their electronegativity difference on a continuum marked at 0.4 and 1.8. The electron-sea model shows sodium, magnesium or aluminum as positive cores in a sea of delocalized electrons; the case file adds copper.

Instruction and assessment

Ionic bonding is the attraction among ions in a lattice; the electron transfer is a step in forming the ions. Covalent bonds share electron pairs, and unequal sharing makes a bond polar. Metallic bonding is attraction between metal cores and delocalized electrons. Predictions combine the ΔEN, calculated from the shared Pauling values, with the positions of the elements, and the 0.4 and 1.8 boundaries are taught as a guide with exceptions.

Reading checks: 15. Slide checks: 8, then a three-question checkpoint and a CER exit ticket. The deck has 22 slides: period 1 uses slides 1 to 10, period 2 slides 11 to 22. Missions: Predict the bond (six everyday substances, from sodium fluoride to copper wire); Find a partner (custom: six puzzles in which students choose an element for a given bond type in the bond explorer); Build the argument (six claim, evidence and reasoning cases).

Misconceptions and corrections

  • Transfer is the ionic bond. Ion formation is followed by lattice attraction.
  • Every substance has molecules. Use NaCl formula units and metallic extended structures.
  • A cutoff proves bonding. Use composition and periodic position with the continuum.

Honors boundary

Two Honors boxes in the reading. One follows the energy: moving the electron costs 147 kJ/mol, and the lattice energy of about 787 kJ/mol, found through a Born–Haber cycle, is what makes the crystal stable. The other gives Pauling's estimate of percent ionic character, which shows why no boundary is sharp. Neither is assessed on the core path.

4.2 Ionic compounds: formulas and names

Driving question: How do the charges of ions decide the formula and the name of an ionic compound?

Objectives

  • 4.2.1 Predict the charge of the common ion of an element in group 1, 2, 13, 15, 16 or 17, and state the one common ion of silver and of zinc.
  • 4.2.2 Write the formula of an ionic compound by balancing the total charge of its ions, in the lowest whole-number ratio.
  • 4.2.3 Name binary ionic compounds, using the Stock system for iron and copper, whose ions can have more than one charge.
  • 4.2.4 Write the formulas and names of compounds that contain the six core polyatomic ions, using parentheses when more than one of an ion is needed.
  • 4.2.5 Translate between the name and the formula of an ionic compound, and use the formula to find the charge of a metal ion.

Models

The ion-tile model builds a formula by adding cation and anion tiles until the charges balance, then names the compound. Turnable 3D models show the six core polyatomic ions with their overall charges.

Instruction and assessment

Students predict common ion charges from the group, with silver and zinc as fixed-charge transition metals. A formula gives the lowest whole-number ratio with a total charge of zero; the crisscross shortcut is taught with a check that the ratio is reduced. An -ide anion follows the cation, and a Stock numeral gives the charge of iron or copper. A polyatomic ion stays intact, in parentheses when it repeats.

Reading checks: 15. Slide checks: 8, then a three-question checkpoint and a CER exit ticket. The deck has 23 slides: period 1 uses slides 1 to 11, period 2 slides 12 to 23. Missions: Name the compound (six compounds); Build the formula (custom: six formulas built from ion tiles, with a check button); Find the error (six formulas or names to correct).

Misconceptions and corrections

  • Crisscross always gives a final formula. Reduce ratios and verify charge.
  • Roman numerals count atoms. Infer the metal ion charge from the anion charge.
  • Parentheses change an ion. Repeat the whole intact polyatomic group.

Honors boundary

One Honors box gives the older -ous and -ic names (ferrous, ferric, cuprous, cupric) and the two common ions of tin and lead, with stannous fluoride as tin(II) fluoride. It is not assessed.

4.3 Covalent names and Lewis structures

Driving question: How do a molecule's name and Lewis structure show its atoms and the electrons that hold them together?

Objectives

  • 4.3.1 Name binary molecular compounds with the prefixes mono- to deca-, write their formulas from their names, and use the accepted names water, ammonia and methane.
  • 4.3.2 Count the valence electrons in a molecule, and tell bonding pairs from lone pairs in a Lewis structure.
  • 4.3.3 Draw Lewis structures with single bonds, in which each atom has an octet and each hydrogen atom has two electrons.
  • 4.3.4 Draw Lewis structures with double or triple bonds when single bonds leave an atom short of an octet.
  • 4.3.5 Check a Lewis structure or a name, find the error, and correct it.

Models

The name lab builds a molecular name from prefix buttons and checks it against the formula. The Lewis builder lets students place bonding pairs and lone pairs on a skeleton and counts electrons as they go. Each finished structure sits beside a turnable 3D model of the same molecule.

Instruction and assessment

Prefixes mono- to deca- count atoms, the first element takes no mono-, and accepted names such as water and ammonia are named as exceptions. Students count every valence electron, separate bonding pairs from lone pairs, and check octets and hydrogen duets. Double and triple bonds move existing lone pairs into the bond without changing the electron total, in CO2, N2, HCN and CH2O.

Reading checks: 15. Slide checks: 8, then a three-question checkpoint and a CER exit ticket. The deck has 23 slides: period 1 uses slides 1 to 10, period 2 slides 11 to 23. Missions: Name the molecule (six names and formulas); Draw the structure (custom: six Lewis structures built in the Lewis builder); Check the drawing (six drawings checked by electron count and octets).

Misconceptions and corrections

  • A line is one electron. Count two per bonding pair.
  • Hydrogen needs an octet. Check a two-electron duet.
  • Multiple bonds add electrons. Redistribute existing lone pairs.

Honors boundary

One Honors box shows exceptions to the octet rule: BF3 with six electrons on boron, SF6 with twelve on sulfur, and NO2 with an odd count, which explains how N2O4 forms. It is not assessed.

4.4 Molecular shapes (VSEPR)

Driving question: How does a Lewis structure predict the shape of a molecule in three dimensions, and why does that shape matter?

Objectives

  • 4.4.1 Count the electron domains around a central atom from its Lewis structure: each lone pair and each single, double or triple bond counts as one.
  • 4.4.2 Predict the electron-domain arrangement from the number of domains (linear, trigonal planar or tetrahedral), as the arrangement with the least repulsion.
  • 4.4.3 Classify a molecule as linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal or bent from its Lewis structure, and tell its molecular geometry from its electron-domain arrangement.
  • 4.4.4 Draw the five molecular geometries with wedges and dashes, and compare the ideal bond angles with the measured ones.
  • 4.4.5 Decide whether a molecule is polar from its bond polarities and its shape, and state what the VSEPR model can and cannot predict.

Models

In the domain model, students choose two, three or four electron domains and zero, one or two lone pairs; the domains spread apart, and the model names the arrangement and the molecular shape that the bonded atoms make. Turnable 3D models of real molecules, in ball-and-stick and space-filling views, show measured angles, lone pairs and δ labels. The custom mission uses the same 3D models so students can match a wedge-and-dash drawing to a molecule they can turn.

Instruction and assessment

Each lone pair and each single, double or triple bond counts as one electron domain. Two, three and four domains give linear, trigonal planar and tetrahedral arrangements; lone pairs then leave trigonal pyramidal or bent shapes. Measured angles from NIST (CH4 109.5°, NH3 106.7°, H2O 104.5°) are kept apart from ideal ones. Polarity combines bond polarity with shape, and C–H bonds (ΔEN 0.35) count as nonpolar.

Reading checks: 15. Slide checks: 9, then a three-question checkpoint and a CER exit ticket. The deck has 24 slides: period 1 uses slides 1 to 11, period 2 slides 12 to 24. Missions: Classify the shape (six molecules from CS2 to PCl3); Draw with wedges (custom: draw each bond as a line, wedge or dashed wedge to match a turnable model); Polar or nonpolar (six molecules with live 3D models).

Misconceptions and corrections

  • A double bond is two domains. Count one direction per bond.
  • Domain arrangement equals molecular shape. Exclude lone pairs from atom-position names.
  • Polar bonds guarantee polar molecules. Check cancellation using geometry.

Honors boundary

One Honors box applies polarity to all five shapes (HCN and CH2O polar, BF3 nonpolar) and introduces SO2, bent with three domains, whose equal measured bonds lead to resonance. It is not assessed.

4.5 Bonds, forces and properties

Driving question: How do the forces within and between particles explain the melting point, conductivity and hardness of a substance?

Objectives

  • 4.5.1 Distinguish the bonds within a substance from the attractions between molecules, and use the halogen boiling points as evidence for dispersion forces.
  • 4.5.2 Identify dipole-dipole forces and hydrogen bonding, and compare the boiling points of molecular substances by their strongest attraction.
  • 4.5.3 Explain melting and boiling as molecules separating with their covalent bonds intact, and explain why ice is less dense than liquid water.
  • 4.5.4 Explain the electrical conductivity of solids, liquids and solutions from charged particles that can move, and describe which substances dissolve in water.
  • 4.5.5 Compare the hardness, brittleness and malleability of ionic, metallic, molecular and covalent network solids.

Models

Five models carry the lesson. The heating model warms methane or water from solid to gas while the molecules stay whole and the attractions between them break. The boiling-point plot compares CH4, NH3 and H2O with the halogens and HCl, plotted against electrons per molecule. The turnable ice lattice shows hydrogen bonds and open six-sided rings. The conductivity tester has a working switch and bulb for six samples, with a zoomed view of the particles. The hammer blow bends copper but lines up like charges in sodium chloride until it cracks.

Instruction and assessment

Bonds within molecules are kept apart from attractions between them: dispersion forces in all molecules, dipole-dipole forces in polar ones, and hydrogen bonding with N–H, O–H or F–H. Melting and boiling separate molecules and leave their covalent bonds intact, and hydrogen bonding explains the less dense ice. Conduction needs charged particles that can move. Hardness, brittleness and malleability come from the particles in each kind of solid, with quartz and diamond as covalent networks.

Reading checks: 15. Slide checks: 9, then a three-question checkpoint and a CER exit ticket. The deck has 28 slides: period 1 uses slides 1 to 12, period 2 slides 13 to 28. Missions: Name the attraction (six substances); Explain the property (six properties of real materials); Rank the boiling points (custom: five sets ordered from lowest to highest boiling point).

Misconceptions and corrections

  • Boiling breaks water molecules. Track intact O–H bonds through every phase.
  • Charge alone guarantees conduction. Require mobile charge carriers.
  • All covalent solids melt low. Compare molecular water with network quartz and diamond.

Honors boundary

One Honors box ranks molecular substances by their strongest attraction and shows where the ranking fails: bromine, with 70 electrons and dispersion forces only, boils far above polar HCl. It is not assessed.

Case file: Preparing for a Texas freeze

Students act as science advisers to a fictional Texas school district. After Winter Storm Uri in February 2021, the district's facilities team asks what it should do before and during the next hard freeze, and why.

Briefing

The memo explains the task and lists the six files with their TEKS. A fact box gives the real context. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission reported on November 16, 2021 that more than 4.5 million people in Texas lost power, some for as long as four days. The Texas Department of Transportation sprays brine on roads before a storm. A 1996 University of Illinois study froze water pipes on purpose to learn where they split. Carbon monoxide poisonings surged when people ran generators, grills and car engines indoors, and the CDC advises never to do so. The page gives no death toll. The district, its storeroom and its plumber are fictional, and the page says so.

Each file pairs real evidence with a model students can work. Students then name the Unit 4 idea the evidence uses and decide about a claim. Files 1, 2, 3 and 5 also have a calculation or a count.

File 1 · De-icers in the storeroom · C.7(A) C.7(B)

A table of three chloride de-icers with Pauling electronegativities and their ions, and the Lesson 4.1 bond model comparing Na–Cl with Ca–Cl. Students can choose other partners for chlorine. Task: for magnesium chloride, 3.16 − 1.31 = 1.85, just above the 1.8 line, which shows the guide marks a gradual change rather than a switch. Then write CaCl2 for a smudged label from the ion charges.

File 2 · One water molecule · C.7(C)

The Lewis structure (electron dot structure) of water and the turnable Lesson 4.4 model, with lone pairs, the 104.5° angle and δ labels drawn from electronegativity. Task: count four electron domains on oxygen; explain that water is polar because its polar O–H bonds sit in a bent shape.

File 3 · Why pipes burst · C.7(D)

The Lesson 4.5 ice lattice and a new frozen-pipe model. Students freeze a pipe with the faucet shut or dripping. Ice forms a ring and then a plug, pressure rises in the liquid trapped against the closed faucet, and the pipe splits there, away from the ice. A dripping faucet lets the trapped water out. Task: (0.99984 ÷ 0.9167 − 1) × 100 = 9.07%; name hydrogen bonds as what holds the open rings of ice; correct a custodian who says the ice pushed the wall out. Source: Gordon (1996), University of Illinois Building Research Council.

File 4 · Copper pipe or plastic tubing · C.7(D)

The Lesson 4.1 electron-sea model, now with copper, and a table comparing copper pipe with PEX tubing. The PEX facts come from the Plastics Pipe Institute's report TR-52, and the page names the institute as a trade group of plastic pipe makers. PEX typically stretches through a freeze and returns to size, but it may split in concrete or packed soil, and no pipe should be left to freeze. Task: name delocalized electrons as what lets copper conduct; answer a plumber who says PEX never bursts. The report's claim about how many freezes PEX survives is not used.

File 5 · Carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide · C.7(B) C.7(C)

The safety context sits in a dashed box labeled "Context, not assessed." Lewis structures and turnable models of CO and CO2 follow. Task: count 16 valence electrons in CO2; read di- as two oxygen atoms; compare one triple bond with two double bonds, and see that both molecules are linear.

File 6 · Salt or sand on the sidewalk · C.7(D)

A new turnable comparison: a space-filling sodium chloride crystal beside a ball-and-stick fragment of quartz built from the Levien, Prewitt and Weidner (1980) structure. A table shows that sodium chloride dissolves (about 360 g in 1 L), its solution conducts, and it melts at 801 °C; quartz does not dissolve and melts at about 1700 °C. A note adds that limestone, calcium carbonate, barely dissolves and that solid salt does not conduct. Task: explain the difference with moving ions on one side and a covalent network on the other. Wrong options include "every ionic compound dissolves" and "covalent bonds are weaker".

Recommendation · claim, evidence, reasoning · SEP 3(A), 3(C)

Students choose one of three claims: pretreat the walks with brine and let faucets drip in pipes through cold spots; replace damaged lines with PEX and still protect every pipe; or spread sand alone and shut every faucet tight. They select at least three files and mark what the district must still check in its own buildings. The check requires File 3 for every claim, File 1 or 6 for the brine claim and File 4 for the PEX claim. It also requires the three site questions: which pipes run through cold spots, how much brine to spread and how early, and how long each building stays warm without power. It rejects the two questions that the files already answer. The sand claim gets feedback that sand melts no ice and that a shut faucet traps water. After a successful check, a sample recommendation for the chosen claim appears. Students then write their reasoning in notebooks, with sentence starters and a word bank.

Grading the notebook. A strong recommendation explains expansion on freezing by hydrogen bonding, with the 9% figure. It says the pipe splits in trapped liquid water, not at the ice. It contrasts the ions in salt with the covalent network in sand, and it names what only a check of the buildings can settle.

Case-file boundary. Freezing-point depression is described in one sentence and is never explained or assessed. Carbon monoxide safety is context only, with no counts. The PEX facts are credited to a trade group report. No death toll appears.

Using the results

Read notebook reasoning and drawings before judging mastery. Mission choice feedback checks a selected answer; it cannot assess the quality of an unwritten explanation. Revisit charge balance for formula errors, electron totals for Lewis errors, central lone pairs for shape errors, and mobile charge for conductivity errors. Score CER claim, evidence and reasoning separately, 0 to 2 per part.

Data and accuracy notes

Inherited glossary definitions remain verbatim. Their valence-electron wording is an introductory main-group definition. Noble-gas and halogen lists retain the earlier radon and astatine endpoints. Physical-property summaries for metals and nonmetals have exceptions. Dissolved salt conducts through mobile ions that were already present in its lattice.

Pauling values, atomic masses and halogen phase data are shared with Unit 3. Do not substitute rounded OpenStax values in ΔEN calculations. Boundaries 0.4 and 1.8 are a guide with exceptions. Ionic naming follows composition even when metal-compound ΔEN lies in a polar range.

NIST molecular geometry: H₂O 104.5°, NH₃ 106.7°, CH₄ 109.5°, CO₂ and HCN 180°, CH₂O 116.1° H–C–H and 121.9° H–C–O. Ideal VSEPR angles are distinguished from measured angles. Ball sizes blend covalent radii (Cordero et al. 2008) with van der Waals radii, and they never set bond lengths. Alvarez radii size space-filling models. CCl₄ and CH₂Cl₂ also come from CCCBDB. The six polyatomic ions are not in CCCBDB, so they use typical bond lengths from crystal structures and the angles their symmetry fixes.

The lattice energy of NaCl is given as about 787 kJ/mol (CRC Handbook, from a Born–Haber cycle); OpenStax lists 769 kJ/mol, and the unit uses the CRC value throughout. NaCl uses six-coordinate Shannon radii, Na⁺ 102 pm and Cl⁻ 181 pm, with a 564 pm conventional cell and 282 pm nearest spacing. Ice uses an ideal hexagonal oxygen framework, O–O 276 pm; hydrogen directions are simplified and edge neighbors omitted. All motion is illustrative rather than molecular dynamics.

At 1 atm: CH₄ −161.5 °C, NH₃ −33.3 °C, H₂O 100.0 °C boiling. NaCl melts at 801 °C, copper at 1085 °C; quartz near 1700 °C with form dependence. The quartz fragment in File 6 uses the Levien, Prewitt and Weidner (1980) structure. At 0 °C: ice 0.9167 g/cm³; water 0.99984 g/cm³, about 9% volume expansion. CO₂ sublimes at −78.5 °C at 1 atm and is not given a liquid boiling point there. Graphite conducts; calcium carbonate is sparingly soluble.

Out of core: formal charge, resonance, hybridization, molecular orbitals, expanded octets, higher-domain geometries, lattice-energy and bond-energy calculations, hydrates and extended organic naming. Forward pointers: acid names C.12(A), water in solutions C.11(A), solubility rules C.11(D). The notebook tasks never assess freezing-point depression.
