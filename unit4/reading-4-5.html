4.5

Bonds, forces and properties

Methane melts at −182.5 °C and boils at −161.5 °C, so it is a gas even on the coldest winter day. Table salt stays solid until 801 °C, and quartz, the main mineral in sand, melts only near 1700 °C. Bonds hold every one of these substances together. The difference lies in what must come apart when each solid melts. This chapter separates the bonds within a substance from the weaker attractions between its molecules.Methane melts at −182.5 °C. It is a gas even on the coldest day. Table salt stays solid until 801 °C. Quartz, the mineral in sand, melts near 1700 °C. Bonds hold all of these substances together. What differs is what comes apart when each one melts. This chapter compares bonds with the weaker attractions between molecules.

Driving question

How do the forces within and between particles explain the melting point, conductivity and hardness of a substance?

§4.5.1

Bonds and attractions

Lesson 4.1 described three kinds of . Covalent bonds join the atoms of a molecule, ionic bonds hold the ions of a crystal together, and metallic bonds hold metal cations in a sea of electrons. The TEKS call all three , even though ionic and metallic crystals contain no molecules. This chapter uses a plainer name for them: bonds within a substance.Lesson 4.1 described three kinds of bond. Covalent bonds join the atoms of a molecule. Ionic bonds hold ions together in a crystal. Metallic bonds hold metal cations in a sea of electrons. The TEKS call all three intramolecular forces. Salt and copper have no molecules, though. So this chapter calls them bonds within a substance.

Separate molecules also attract one another. These act between molecules, and they are much weaker than the covalent bonds within them. In chlorine, a covalent bond joins the two atoms of each Cl₂ molecule, while weak attractions hold neighboring molecules close together. Sodium chloride and copper have no separate molecules, so their bonds run through the whole crystal.Separate molecules also attract each other. These attractions are called intermolecular forces. They are much weaker than the bonds inside each molecule. In chlorine, a covalent bond joins the two atoms of each Cl₂ molecule. Weak attractions hold the molecules near each other.

Key idea

Bonds hold atoms or ions together within a substance. Much weaker attractions act between separate molecules.

§4.5.1

Dispersion forces

act between all molecules, polar or not. The electrons in a molecule are always moving, so at any instant they can crowd slightly toward one end. That end becomes slightly negative, and the other end slightly positive. The brief charges shift the electrons of a neighboring molecule and attract it. Each attraction lasts only an instant, but new ones form all the time.Dispersion forces act between all molecules. Electrons in a molecule are always moving. For an instant, more of them may sit at one end. That end becomes slightly negative. The other end becomes slightly positive. These brief charges attract a nearby molecule. Each attraction lasts only an instant, but new ones keep forming.

Unit 3 listed the boiling points of the . All four are nonpolar molecules, so dispersion forces are the only attractions between them. Fluorine, with 18 electrons, boils at −188.1 °C, while iodine, with 106 electrons, boils at 184.4 °C. Larger molecules hold more electrons, which shift more easily, so their dispersion forces are stronger. In 1930, the physicist Fritz London explained these forces, so they are often called London dispersion forces.Unit 3 listed the boiling points of the halogens. All four are nonpolar, so dispersion forces are their only attractions. F₂ has 18 electrons and boils at −188.1 °C. I₂ has 106 electrons and boils at 184.4 °C. More electrons give stronger dispersion forces. Fritz London explained these forces in 1930. So they are often called London dispersion forces.

Key idea

Dispersion forces act between all molecules. Molecules with more electrons have stronger dispersion forces and higher boiling points.

§4.5.2

Dipole-dipole forces

Lesson 4.4 showed that a has a partial negative end, δ−, and a partial positive end, δ+. In a liquid or a solid, polar molecules turn so that the δ+ end of one faces the δ− end of the next. This attraction between permanent partial charges is called . Polar molecules have dispersion forces as well, so the dipole-dipole forces add to them.Lesson 4.4 showed polar molecules. Each has a δ− end and a δ+ end. Polar molecules turn so that opposite ends face each other. The δ+ end of one attracts the δ− end of the next. This attraction is called dipole-dipole forces. Polar molecules have dispersion forces too.

Fluorine, F₂, and hydrogen chloride, HCl, each have 18 electrons. F₂ is nonpolar and boils at −188.1 °C. HCl is polar and boils at −85.1 °C, about 100 °C higher. The attraction between the HCl dipoles helps explain the difference.F₂ and HCl each have 18 electrons. F₂ is nonpolar and boils at −188.1 °C. HCl is polar and boils at −85.1 °C. That is about 100 °C higher. The attraction between HCl dipoles helps explain the gap.

Key idea

Polar molecules attract one another through dipole-dipole forces, in addition to their dispersion forces.

§4.5.2

Hydrogen bonding

Ammonia and water boil far above methane because of , an especially strong dipole-dipole attraction. It forms when a hydrogen atom bonded to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine is attracted to a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of another molecule. These three atoms are so electronegative that an H atom bonded to them carries a large δ+ charge. The small H atom can also come very close to the lone pair of its neighbor.Ammonia and water boil far above methane because of hydrogen bonding. It is an extra strong dipole-dipole attraction. It needs an H atom bonded to N, O or F. That H is attracted to a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of another molecule. N, O and F pull hard on electrons. So an H bonded to them is strongly δ+.

A hydrogen bond is not a covalent bond. It is an attraction between molecules, only about 5 to 10% as strong as a covalent bond. Methane forms no hydrogen bonds, because its H atoms are bonded to carbon. Water boils highest of the three, since each molecule has two H atoms and two lone pairs and can form up to four hydrogen bonds.A hydrogen bond is not a covalent bond. It is an attraction between molecules. It is only 5 to 10% as strong as a covalent bond. Methane has no hydrogen bonds. Its H atoms are bonded to carbon. Each water molecule can form up to four hydrogen bonds.

§4.5.3

Molecules stay whole

When a molecular solid melts, its molecules break free of their fixed places but stay close together. When the liquid boils, the molecules separate and spread out as a gas. Both changes overcome only the attractions between molecules. The covalent bonds within each molecule stay intact, so the steam above boiling water is still made of H₂O molecules, each with its two O–H bonds.When a molecular solid melts, its molecules leave their fixed places. They still stay close together. When the liquid boils, the molecules spread far apart. Both changes overcome only the attractions between molecules. The covalent bonds inside each molecule stay whole. Steam is still made of H₂O molecules.

The energies show how different the two are. Boiling 18 g of water at 100 °C takes 40.7 kJ, while breaking every O–H bond in the same water would take about 928 kJ, more than 20 times as much. Since only the attractions must be overcome, the and of a molecular substance rise as those attractions grow stronger.Energy data show the gap. Boiling 18 g of water takes 40.7 kJ. Breaking all its O–H bonds would take about 928 kJ. That is more than 20 times as much. Stronger attractions between molecules mean higher melting and boiling points.

Key idea

Melting and boiling separate molecules. They do not break the covalent bonds within them.

§4.5.3

Why ice floats

In most substances, the solid is denser than the liquid, so the solid sinks. Water is an exception. In ice, each molecule forms hydrogen bonds to four neighbors, which hold the molecules in an open pattern of six-sided rings. When ice melts, some of these attractions are overcome, the rings collapse and the molecules pack closer together.For most substances, the solid sinks in its own liquid. Water is different. In ice, each molecule forms hydrogen bonds to four neighbors. These hold the molecules in open, six-sided rings. When ice melts, the rings collapse. The molecules then pack closer together.

At 0 °C, ice has a density of 0.9167 g/cm³ and liquid water 0.99984 g/cm³. Ice is less dense, so it floats, with about 8.3% of a block above the surface. The same 99.98 g of water fills 100.0 cm³ as a liquid but 109.1 cm³ as ice, about 9% more. This expansion can split rocks and burst water pipes in a hard freeze.At 0 °C, ice has a density of 0.9167 g/cm³. Liquid water has 0.99984 g/cm³. Ice is less dense, so it floats. Water grows by about 9% when it freezes. This can burst water pipes in a hard freeze.

Key idea

Hydrogen bonds hold ice in open rings, so ice is less dense than liquid water and floats.

§4.5.4

Electrical conductivity

An electric current is a flow of charge. A substance conducts electricity only if it contains charged particles that are free to move. Metals conduct as solids and as liquids, because their move through the whole metal. This is why copper is used for electrical wiring.An electric current is a flow of charge. A substance conducts only if it has charged particles that can move. Metals conduct as solids and as liquids. Their delocalized electrons move through the whole metal. That is why wires are made of copper.

Solid sodium chloride contains ions, but they are locked in place in the crystal, so the solid does not conduct. When NaCl melts at 801 °C, or dissolves in water, its ions are free to move and the liquid conducts. A substance that forms ions in water, like NaCl, is an . Sugar water does not conduct, because sugar dissolves as whole molecules with no charge.Solid NaCl has ions, but they are locked in the crystal. So solid salt does not conduct. Melted or dissolved, its ions can move, and the liquid conducts. NaCl is an electrolyte, since it forms ions in water. Sugar water does not conduct. Sugar dissolves as whole molecules with no charge.

§4.5.4

Dissolving in water

Many substances dissolve in water, but many others do not. Table salt and sugar both dissolve easily, although salt is ionic and sugar is molecular. Sand, which is mostly quartz, does not dissolve, and neither does copper. Iodine dissolves only slightly, giving a pale yellow-brown solution.Many substances dissolve in water, but many do not. Salt and sugar both dissolve easily. Salt is ionic, and sugar is molecular. Sand and copper do not dissolve. Iodine dissolves only slightly.

Being ionic does not mean that a compound dissolves. Calcium carbonate, CaCO₃, is ionic, yet it barely dissolves in water; it makes up limestone, marble and seashells. §4.5.5

Malleable or brittle

Metals are : they can be hammered into thin sheets or drawn into wire without breaking. In a metal, the cations sit in layers within a sea of delocalized electrons. When a hammer pushes one layer past another, the electrons flow along with the cations. Every cation is still surrounded by electrons, so the metallic bonding holds and the metal bends instead of breaking.Metals are malleable. They can be hammered into sheets or pulled into wire. Their cations sit in layers in a sea of electrons. A hammer blow pushes one layer past another. The electrons move along with the cations. So the bonding holds, and the metal bends.

Ionic crystals are hard, but they are . A sharp blow can shift one layer of ions by the width of a single ion. Each ion then sits beside ions of the same charge, and like charges repel. The repulsion pushes the layers apart, so the crystal splits along a flat face instead of bending.Ionic crystals are hard but brittle. A sharp blow can shift one layer of ions. Then each ion sits next to ions with the same charge. Like charges repel. The layers push apart, and the crystal splits.

Molecular solids, such as solid iodine and dry ice, are usually soft, because only the attractions between molecules hold them together.Molecular solids, like solid iodine, are usually soft. Only weak attractions hold their molecules together.

Key idea

A metal bends because its electrons move with the shifting cations. An ionic crystal splits because shifted layers put like charges side by side.

§4.5.5

Covalent network solids

In a , covalent bonds join every atom to its neighbors throughout the crystal, so there are no separate molecules. Melting such a solid means breaking covalent bonds, so network solids melt only at very high temperatures, and most are very hard. Diamond, a network of carbon atoms, is the hardest natural substance, and quartz, SiO₂, melts near 1700 °C.In a covalent network solid, covalent bonds join every atom to its neighbors. There are no separate molecules. To melt it, covalent bonds must break. So network solids melt only when very hot. Most are also very hard. Diamond is a network of carbon atoms. Quartz, SiO₂, melts near 1700 °C.

Carbon dioxide and silicon dioxide have similar formulas, yet they behave very differently. CO₂ forms separate molecules, held together in the solid only by dispersion forces. Dry ice, which is solid CO₂, at −78.5 °C, turning straight into a gas. SiO₂ forms a network instead, so quartz stays solid until about 1700 °C.CO₂ and SiO₂ have similar formulas, but they act very differently. CO₂ forms separate molecules. Only dispersion forces hold them together. Dry ice, solid CO₂, sublimes at −78.5 °C. It turns straight into a gas. SiO₂ forms a network, so quartz stays solid until about 1700 °C.

An exception: graphite

Graphite is a covalent network of carbon, yet it conducts along its layers. Each carbon atom bonds to three neighbors, and its fourth valence electron is delocalized across its layer. Only dispersion forces hold the layers together, so they slide and graphite is soft.

Honors · Rank by the strongest attraction

To rank molecular substances by boiling point, name the strongest type of attraction in each. For molecules of similar size, hydrogen bonding is stronger than dipole-dipole forces, which are stronger than dispersion forces alone. Methane, hydrogen chloride and water boil at −161.5, −85.1 and 100.0 °C, in that order.

The ranking holds only for molecules of similar size. Dispersion forces grow with the number of electrons, so a large nonpolar molecule can outweigh a small polar one. Bromine, with 70 electrons and dispersion forces only, boils at 58.8 °C, far above polar HCl.

Ionic compounds, metals and network solids do not fit this ranking, because melting them breaks bonds within the substance. No type of bond is always the strongest, so compare substances with data.
