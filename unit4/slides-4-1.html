Forming ions

Predict

Which atom lets go of its outer electron, Na or Cl?

What happened

Na lost one electron. It is now Na+. Cl gained it. It is now Cl−.

Rule

A metal atom loses its valence electrons and becomes a + ion (cation). A nonmetal atom gains them and becomes a − ion (anion). The transfer alone costs energy.

Check

Magnesium loses two electrons. Which ion forms?

  1. AMg2−
  2. BMg+
  3. CMg2+

C. Mg keeps 12 protons and has 10 electrons: Mg2+.

Energy (Honors box, not assessed): removing Na’s electron costs 496 kJ/mol; Cl releases 349 kJ/mol; the transfer alone costs 147 kJ/mol. The attraction in the crystal (slide 7) pays it back.

The crystal

Predict

Do the ions stay in pairs? Show thumbs up or down.

crystal (cristal)

ions in a pattern that repeats in every direction

Rule

The pull (attraction) between + ions and − ions is the ionic bond. The formula NaCl gives a ratio, not a molecule.

Check

How many Cl− ions touch each Na+ ion?

  1. A1
  2. B6
  3. C8

B. Each Na+ touches six Cl−.

Misconception: "the transfer is the ionic bond". The transfer makes the ions; the attraction in the lattice is the bond. Building the crystal releases about 787 kJ/mol (lattice energy), far more than the transfer costs. Shannon radii: Na⁺ 102 pm, Cl⁻ 181 pm; the lattice model shrinks both so the center ion stays visible.

Covalent bonds

Recap

An even pull keeps the knot in the middle. Atoms can share electrons this way.

H–H

Two H atoms share one pair. Each H now has two electrons, like helium.

Cl–Cl

Each Cl has seven outer electrons. With the shared pair, each Cl has eight around it.

Rule

A pair of electrons shared by two atoms is a covalent bond. Atoms joined this way form a molecule (molécula).

Check

In Cl2, how many electrons does each Cl count around itself?

  1. A7
  2. B8
  3. C14

B. Six unshared electrons plus the shared pair make 8.

Lewis (1916) proposed the shared pair; Langmuir (1919) named covalence. Lesson 4.3 draws Lewis structures for larger molecules.

Polar and nonpolar

H–H

Equal pull: the pair stays in the middle. The bond is nonpolar.

Rule

The electrons spend more time near one atom. It is the atom with the higher electronegativity. That atom gets a small negative charge, written δ− (delta minus). The other atom gets a small positive charge, δ+.

Predict

Electronegativity (pull on electrons)H2.20Cl3.16

Which atom gets δ−?

H–Cl

Cl pulls harder, so the pair leans to Cl. Cl gets δ−, and H gets δ+. This bond is polar.

O–H and H–F

A bigger difference makes the pair lean more.

Check

Electronegativity (pull on electrons)O3.44H2.20

In O–H, which atom is δ+?

  1. AH
  2. BO
  3. Cneither

A. H pulls less, so H is δ+. O is δ−.

Differences: H–H 0.00, H–Cl 0.96, O–H 1.24, H–F 1.78 (Pauling values). C–H is only 0.35: almost equal sharing. The rule comes before the first δ drawing, so students meet the words before the badges.

Metallic bonds

A concert crowd

Beach balls bounce over everyone. No one owns a ball; the crowd shares them all.

Cations

Each head is now a + ion: a metal cation.

Electron sea

In a metal, the outer electrons are the balls. They belong to the whole metal.

Current

A battery pushes the electrons one way. That flow is a current.

Magnesium

Each Mg atom gives two electrons to the sea.

Aluminum

Each Al atom gives three electrons to the sea.

Copper

Each Cu atom gives one electron to the sea.

Rule

Metal cations sit in a sea of delocalized electrons. Their attraction is the metallic bond. Real electrons behave as waves, not dots.

Check

Why can a copper wire carry a current?

  1. AIts electrons can move.
  2. BIts copper ions move.
  3. CIts atoms share pairs.

A. The electrons in the sea move. The cations stay in place.

Model limits (objective 4.1.3): electrons are drawn as dots but behave as waves; the model shows one flat layer of a 3D crystal. Each electron dot carries a − wherever the cores carry a +. Copper gives one electron per core in this model. Lesson 4.5 uses the model for other metal properties.

The electronegativity difference

The difference

ΔEN means the difference: the larger value minus the smaller value.

Guide

The lines at 0.4 and 1.8 are a guide, not a wall. Bonds change slowly along the bar.

Rule

Subtract the smaller value from the larger one. Below 0.4, the bond is nonpolar covalent. From 0.4 to 1.8, it is polar covalent. Above 1.8, it is ionic.

Check

Electronegativity (pull on electrons)Ca1.00O3.44

What does the guide predict for Ca–O?

  1. Anonpolar
  2. Bpolar
  3. Cionic

C. 3.44 − 1.00 = 2.44, above 1.8: ionic.

Guide from OpenStax Chemistry 2e: "just a general guide, however, with many exceptions". The bar colors blend at 0.4 and 1.8 on purpose.

Evidence from the table

Na and Cl: a metal and a nonmetal. The bond is ionic.

H and Cl: two nonmetals. They share a pair: covalent.

Cu alone: one metal. The bond is metallic.

A metal and a nonmetal: electrons transfer. Ionic bond.

Two nonmetals: electrons are shared. Covalent bond.

A metal alone: electrons are delocalized, a sea. Metallic bond.

Check: Which pair forms a covalent bond?

  1. AK–Br
  2. BN–O
  3. CMg–O

B. N and O are both nonmetals. They share pairs.

Second line of evidence. Metals (left and middle) have low ionization energies and electronegativities; nonmetals (upper right) have high ones. Metalloids along the staircase are left untinted.

Near the line

Bonding argument: I do, we do

I do: potassium fluoride, KF.

We do: hydrogen chloride, HCl. Say each part first.

Potassium fluoride (KF) is ionic.

Electronegativity (pull on electrons)K0.82F3.98The difference is 3.16. K is a metal; F is a nonmetal.

A difference above 1.8 predicts ionic. A metal gives electrons to a nonmetal.

Strong. 3.16 is far above 1.8, and both kinds of evidence agree.

The bond in HCl is polar covalent.

Electronegativity (pull on electrons)H2.20Cl3.16The difference is 0.96. Both are nonmetals.

0.96 is between 0.4 and 1.8. Two nonmetals share the pair unequally. Cl is δ−.

Strong. 0.96 is far from both lines, and both kinds of evidence agree.

Check

Which evidence best supports “KF is ionic”?

  1. Ametal + nonmetal, difference 3.16
  2. BKF is a white solid.
  3. CF is a gas.

A. Values and positions are evidence. Color and state are not.

Four parts: claim, evidence (both values, the difference, positions on the table), reasoning (the guide and what metals and nonmetals do), strength (distance from 0.4 and 1.8, and whether both kinds of evidence agree).
