Molecular shapes: the VSEPR model

Count the electron domains on a Lewis structure (electron dot structure), then predict the shape of the molecule in three dimensions.

  • Pacing. Two 45 to 50 minute periods. Period 1: slides 1 to 11 (electron domains, arrangements, lone pairs and the five shapes). Period 2: slides 12 to 24 (practice, wedge drawings, measured angles, polarity, the limits of the model, checkpoint, exit ticket).
  • Textbook link. Each slide's label shows the matching reading section, for example §4.4.2. Assign the reading before or after each period.
  • Safety. No chemicals are handled in this lesson. Every molecule appears as a model or a drawing.
  • Scope. TEKS C.7(C): five shapes from two, three and four electron domains. BF3 appears only as an exception to the octet rule. Shapes with five or six domains, hybrid orbitals and resonance are not taught. SO2, bent with three domains, is Honors only, in the reading.
  • Data. The 3D models and the measured angles are experimental values from NIST CCCBDB: CH4 109.5°, NH3 106.7°, H2O 104.5°, CH2O 116.1° (H–C–H) and 121.9° (H–C–O), BF3 120°, CO2 and HCN 180°. Electronegativities are the Pauling values used in Lesson 4.1.
  • History. Nevil Sidgwick and Herbert Powell linked shape to electron pairs in 1940. Ronald Gillespie and Ronald Nyholm developed the idea into the VSEPR model in 1957.
  • Practice. Missions for 4.4 (Classify the shape, Draw with wedges, Polar or nonpolar) follow this lesson.

        Domains · Textbook §4.4.1 · SEP 1(G)

          Electron domains

          Electron domain

          An electron domain is a region of electrons around the central atom: a lone pair, or a bond to another atom.

          Count

          Count the atoms bonded to the central atom, then add its lone pairs. For ammonia, NH3: 3 + 1 = 4.

          Try

          Choose water, then methane. Count the domains on paper before you read the message.

          1. Electron domain · 2 min. The Count step rings and numbers each domain on the Lewis structure. Ammonia's nitrogen has three bonding pairs and one lone pair, so it has four domains.
          2. Count · 1 min. Count only the central atom. A shared pair belongs to a bond, and each bond to an outer atom is one domain.
          3. Try · 2 min. Water: two bonds and two lone pairs, 4 domains. Methane: four bonds, 4 domains. Misconception: a lone pair takes no room, so it is not a domain.

          Domains · Textbook §4.4.1

            Multiple bonds count once

            One direction

            A double or triple bond holds more than one shared pair. All of its pairs lie between the same two atoms, so it counts as one domain.

            Outer atoms

            Lone pairs on the outer atoms do not count. In CO2, carbon has 2 domains.

            1. One direction · 2 min. Point to formaldehyde: the C=O double bond is ringed once and numbered once, so carbon has three domains. Carbon dioxide has two double bonds and two domains.
            2. Outer atoms · 1 min. The numbers mark domains around the central atom only. The lone pairs on each oxygen atom of CO2 belong to oxygen.
            3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: each shared pair of a multiple bond counts as its own domain.

            Domains · Textbook §4.4.2 · SEP 1(G)

              Domains spread apart

              Repulsion

              Domains are all negative, so they push each other away. In the VSEPR model, they spread as far apart as they can.

              Three arrangements

              DomainsAngleArrangement

              Try

              Drag one domain toward another, let go, and watch them spread again.

              1. Repulsion · 2 min. VSEPR stands for valence shell electron pair repulsion. Press Spread and watch the domains move apart. Read the label aloud: the animation shows the arrangement with the least repulsion, and electron pairs do not travel like this.
              2. Three arrangements · 2 min. Use the Domains buttons to show 2, 3 and 4. The directions the domains point in are the electron-domain arrangement, and each has its own ideal angle.
              3. Try · 2 min. Invite a student to drag one domain toward another. The repulsion rises, and on release the domains settle back. Misconception: four domains spread evenly in a flat circle, 90° apart.

              Domains · Textbook §4.4.2 · SEP 2(A)

                Four domains in three dimensions

                On paper

                A Lewis structure is flat, so methane's four bonds look 90° apart.

                In three dimensions

                The bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron. Each H–C–H bond angle is 109.5°.

                1. On paper · 1 min. Draw methane's Lewis structure on the board. Its angles look like 90° only because paper is flat.
                2. In three dimensions · 2 min. Drag to turn methane and switch on the angles. Then choose CH2O, flat with 116.1° and 121.9°, and CO2, straight at 180°. This settles prediction 1. Angles from NIST CCCBDB.
                3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: the angles in a Lewis structure are the real bond angles.

                Shapes · Textbook §4.4.3 · SEP 1(G)

                  Lone pairs and molecular shape

                  Arrangement

                  Counts every domain, lone pairs included.

                  Molecular geometry

                  The molecular geometry is named from the atoms only, not the lone pairs.

                  1. Arrangement · 1 min. Start from methane. Press Lone pairs 1: one bond becomes a lone pair, and the four domains keep their tetrahedral arrangement.
                  2. Molecular geometry · 2 min. Press Lone pairs 1, then 2, and read the message each time: tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal, bent. The lone pairs are drawn as lobes, and the shape is named from the atoms.
                  3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: the molecular geometry and the arrangement always have the same name.

                  Shapes · Textbook §4.4.3

                    Methane, ammonia and water

                    Same arrangement

                    All three have four domains in a tetrahedral arrangement.

                    Three shapes

                    Methane is tetrahedral. Ammonia, with one lone pair, is trigonal pyramidal. Water, with two, is bent.

                    1. Same arrangement · 1 min. Each model is built from measured bond lengths and angles, with the central lone pairs drawn as lobes.
                    2. Three shapes · 2 min. Name each shape from the atoms. Ammonia's three H atoms sit below N, like the legs of a short stool. This settles prediction 2.
                    3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: NH3 is trigonal planar because it has three H atoms.

                            Polarity · Textbook §4.4.5 · SEP 3(A)

                              Polar molecules

                              Polar bonds

                              In a polar bond, the more electronegative atom takes a partial negative charge, δ−.

                              Turn each molecule

                              Turn each molecule. Look for one side that is δ− and another that is δ+.

                              Two kinds

                              A polar molecule has a δ− side and a δ+ side. A nonpolar molecule has no such sides.

                              1. Polar bonds · 1 min. From Lesson 4.1: O–H differs in electronegativity by 1.24 and N–H by 0.84, so both are polar. C–H differs by only 0.35, so it counts as nonpolar. The δ labels are drawn from electronegativity, not a computed charge map.
                              2. Turn each molecule · 2 min. Turn water, then CO2. In water, both H atoms sit on one side of the oxygen atom. In CO2, the two O atoms sit at opposite ends.
                              3. Two kinds · 1 min. Misconception: any molecule with polar bonds is polar.

                                Limits · Textbook §4.4.5 · SEP 2(A)

                                  A model with limits

                                  History

                                  In 1940, Sidgwick and Powell linked shape to the electron pairs on the central atom. In 1957, Gillespie and Nyholm built the VSEPR model.

                                  Strengths

                                  The model predicts all five shapes in this lesson. It also explains why lone pairs make bond angles smaller.

                                  Limits of the model

                                  MoleculeDomainsLone pairsMeasured angle

                                  The model predicts angles slightly under 109.5° for all four. It fits water and ammonia, but not H2S or PH3.

                                  1. History · 1 min. Sidgwick and Powell gave the Bakerian Lecture to the Royal Society in 1940. Gillespie and Nyholm published their rules in Quarterly Reviews in 1957. The model grew as chemists tested it against measured shapes.
                                  2. Strengths · 1 min. A useful model makes predictions that measurements can test. VSEPR predicts every shape on slide 11.
                                  3. Limits · 2 min. H2S and PH3 appear only as a limit of the model; do not assess them. Their angles are from NIST CCCBDB.
                                  4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: a model that is not exact is wrong.

                                            Wrap-up · Exit ticket · SEP 3(A)

                                              Answer the driving question

                                              How does a Lewis structure predict the shape of a molecule in three dimensions, and why does that shape matter? Write your answer in your notebook.

                                              Word bank

                                              Claim

                                              One sentence that answers the question.

                                              1. A Lewis structure gives… and the shape decides…

                                              Sample claim

                                              A Lewis structure gives the domains and lone pairs on the central atom, which predict the shape, and the shape decides whether a molecule is polar.

                                              Evidence

                                              Use carbon dioxide and water.

                                              1. Carbon dioxide has…
                                              2. Water has…

                                              Sample evidence

                                              Carbon has two domains and no lone pairs, so CO2 is linear and nonpolar. Oxygen has four domains, two of them lone pairs, so water is bent, at 104.5°, and polar.

                                              Reasoning

                                              Explain why domains spread out and how shape decides polarity.

                                              1. Electron domains repel, so…
                                              2. In a bent molecule, the bond pulls…

                                              Sample reasoning

                                              Electron domains repel, so they spread as far apart as they can. In linear CO2, the two bond pulls point opposite ways and cancel. In bent water, both pulls point toward O, so the molecule has a δ− side.

                                              Self-check: mark each part your answer meets, then compare with the sample.

                                              3. Reasoning · 3 min. Must use repulsion between domains and explain how the bond pulls cancel or add.
