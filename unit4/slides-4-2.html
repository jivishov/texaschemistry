        Formulas · Textbook §4.2.1

          Charges from the group

          Main groups

          Metals in groups 1, 2 and 13 lose their valence electrons. Nonmetals in groups 15, 16 and 17 gain electrons until they have eight.

          Transition metals

          Iron and copper each form two common ions. Silver forms only Ag+, and zinc only Zn2+.

          1. Main groups · 2 min. Each shaded cell shows the element's common ion. A main-group element loses or gains electrons until it has as many as the nearest noble gas. Each of these ions is a monatomic ion, one atom with a charge. Groups 14 and 18 are left blank: carbon and silicon rarely form simple ions, and noble gases form none.
          2. Transition metals · 1 min. The charge of a transition metal ion cannot be read from its group. This course uses four of them: iron and copper, with two ions each, and silver and zinc, with one each.
          3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: a metal gains electrons and forms a negative ion.

          Formulas · Textbook §4.2.2 · SEP 1(G)

            Balancing the charges

            Zero total

            An ionic compound has a total charge of zero. One Ca2+ needs two Cl−.

            Try it

            Choose other ions. Add tiles until the two rows are the same length.

            1. Zero total · 2 min. Each tile is one ion, and its length is its charge. Each + pairs with a −, so the rows match when the total charge is zero. Step from Ions to Balance and watch the tiles added one at a time.
            2. Try it · 2 min. Ask a student to predict the number of tiles in each row before you press Balance. Then try aluminum with oxide: two Al3+ and three O2−.
            3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: one cation always pairs with one anion.

            Formulas · Textbook §4.2.2

              Writing the formula

              The lowest whole-number ratio

              Rules

              Ordercation first, then anion

              Subscriptsthe number of each ion, in the lowest ratio

              Onea subscript of 1 is not written

              Try two

              With a partner, write the formulas of potassium sulfide and calcium nitride.

              Answers

              K2Stwo K+ give 2+, one S2− gives 2−

              Ca3N2three Ca2+ give 6+, two N3− give 6−

              1. Two examples · 2 min. Aluminum oxide: the rows match at 6+ and 6−, with two Al3+ and three O2−. Magnesium oxide: two of each also balance, but 2 : 2 reduces to 1 : 1, so the formula is MgO. A chemical formula gives the elements and the ratio of their ions; for an ionic compound it describes one formula unit.
              2. Rules · 1 min. A subscript counts the ions just before it.
              3. Try two · 2 min. Pairs find each ion's charge first, from the group number.
              4. Answers · 1 min. For calcium nitride, 2 and 3 both divide into 6, so the rows match at 6+ and 6−.

              Formulas · Textbook §4.2.2

                The crisscross shortcut

                A shortcut, then a check

                Crosseach charge's number becomes the other ion's subscript

                Reducedivide both subscripts by any common factor

                Checkthe total charge is zero

                1. Shortcut · 1 min. The number of each charge crosses over to become the other ion's subscript. It works because it makes both totals equal: 2 × 3+ and 3 × 2−.
                2. Reduce · 1 min. For magnesium oxide the shortcut gives Mg2O2, which is balanced but not the lowest ratio. The tile model on slide 6 never needs this step, because it adds one tile at a time.
                3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: the crisscross result is always the final formula.

                Names · Textbook §4.2.3

                  Naming binary ionic compounds

                  Two parts

                  potassiumthe cationsulfidethe anion: root + -ide

                  A binary compound contains two elements. K2S is potassium sulfide.

                  No prefixes: the charges fix the ratio, so CaCl2 is calcium chloride.

                  Anion names

                  ElementIonName
                  fluorineF−fluoride
                  chlorineCl−chloride
                  bromineBr−bromide
                  iodineI−iodide
                  oxygenO2−oxide
                  sulfurS2−sulfide
                  nitrogenN3−nitride

                  1. Two parts · 2 min. Name the cation first, using the element's name, then the anion with the ending -ide. These rules come from IUPAC. IUPAC also accepts the prefix name calcium dichloride; the course teaches the common name and never marks a prefix name wrong.
                  2. Anion names · 1 min. Keep the root of the element's name and add -ide. Lesson 4.3 uses prefixes for compounds of two nonmetals, which can combine in several ratios.
                  3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: the anion keeps its element's name, as in calcium bromine.

                  Names · Textbook §4.2.3 · SEP 1(G)

                    The Stock system

                    Two ions

                    The Stock system gives the charge of the metal ion as a Roman numeral: iron(III) is Fe3+.

                    One ion

                    Silver and zinc each form one common ion, so they take no numeral: silver chloride, zinc oxide.

                    1. Two ions · 2 min. Switch between iron(II) and iron(III) with chloride: the numeral changes, and so does the formula. Then try copper(I) and copper(II). Write the numeral right after the metal's name, with no space. Alfred Stock proposed this kind of name in 1919.
                    2. One ion · 1 min. Choose silver, then zinc: the name has no numeral. Metals in groups 1 and 2, and aluminum, never need one. Honors: the older names (ferrous, ferric, cuprous, cupric) and the ions of tin and lead are in the reading's Honors box.
                    3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: the numeral counts the metal ions in the formula.

                    Names · Textbook §4.2.3

                      Worked example: copper oxides

                      Black powder, CuO

                      Anionone O2− gives 2−

                      Metalone copper ion balances it, so it is Cu2+

                      Namecopper(II) oxide

                      Red powder, Cu2O

                      Anionone O2− gives 2−

                      Metaltwo copper ions share 2+, so each is Cu+

                      Namecopper(I) oxide

                      What the numeral means

                      Cu2O has two copper ions, yet its name has (I). The numeral is the charge of one metal ion, never a count.

                      1. Black oxide · 1 min. Always start from the anion, whose charge is fixed. One O2− needs one Cu2+.
                      2. Red oxide · 1 min. Two copper ions together balance 2−, so each carries 1+.
                      3. Meaning · 1 min. This answers the driving question for copper: a different ion charge gives a different formula and a different name.

                      Polyatomic ions · Textbook §4.2.4 · SEP 2(A)

                        Polyatomic ions

                        One unit

                        A polyatomic ion: atoms joined by covalent bonds, with one charge for the whole group.

                        Six ions

                        ammoniumNH4+
                        hydroxideOH−
                        nitrateNO3−
                        carbonateCO32−
                        sulfateSO42−
                        phosphatePO43−

                        1. One unit · 2 min. Turn each ion. Nitrate and carbonate are flat triangles; sulfate and phosphate point to the corners of a tetrahedron. The charge shown belongs to the whole ion, not to one atom.
                        2. Six ions · 1 min. Ammonium is the only positive one. Watch the endings: nitrate is not nitride, and sulfate is not sulfide.
                        3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: each oxygen atom in a polyatomic ion is an oxide ion.

                        Polyatomic ions · Textbook §4.2.4 · SEP 1(G)

                          Parentheses in formulas

                          Parentheses

                          More than one of a polyatomic ion: parentheses, with the subscript outside. Ca(OH)2 has two OH−.

                          Try it

                          Choose ammonium with sulfate, then sodium with nitrate.

                          1. Parentheses · 1 min. Without them, CaOH2 would mean one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms. Never change the subscripts inside an ion: SO32− is a different ion, sulfite.
                          2. Try it · 2 min. Ammonium sulfate, a common fertilizer, is (NH4)2SO4. Sodium nitrate has one of each ion, so NaNO3 needs no parentheses.
                          3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: a subscript inside the ion can be changed to balance the charges.

                                    Sample answer

                                    Each aluminum ion has a charge of 3+, and each oxide ion a charge of 2−. The smallest numbers of ions that balance are two Al3+, for 6+, and three O2−, for 6−. The total charge is zero, and 2 : 3 cannot be reduced. So the formula is Al2O3.

                                    4. Self-check · 30 s.
                                    5. Compare · 1 min. Misconception to watch: "Al3O2, because aluminum is 3+." The subscripts cross over.

                                          Wrap-up · Exit ticket · SEP 3(A)

                                            Answer the driving question

                                            How do the charges of ions decide the formula and the name of an ionic compound? Write your answer in your notebook.

                                            Word bank
                                            ionchargetotal chargelowest whole-number ratiosubscriptStock systemRoman numeral

                                            Claim

                                            One sentence that answers the question.

                                            1. The charges of the ions decide…

                                            Sample claim

                                            The charges of the ions decide the ratio in the formula, because the total charge must be zero, and the name states which ions are present.

                                            Evidence

                                            Use the two copper oxides.

                                            1. Copper(I) oxide is…
                                            2. Copper(II) oxide is…

                                            Sample evidence

                                            Copper(I) oxide is Cu2O: two Cu+ balance one O2−. Copper(II) oxide is CuO: one Cu2+ balances one O2−.

                                            Reasoning

                                            Explain why the charges fix the formula and the name.

                                            1. An ionic compound has…
                                            2. The Roman numeral…

                                            Sample reasoning

                                            An ionic compound has no overall charge, so the numbers of ions must make the positive and negative totals equal, in the lowest ratio. Copper forms two ions, so the Roman numeral tells which one is present, and that fixes the formula.

                                            Self-check: mark each part your answer meets, then compare with the sample.

                                            1. Claim · 2 min. One sentence that covers both the formula and the name.
                                            2. Evidence · 3 min. Must give both formulas and both copper ion charges.
                                            3. Reasoning · 3 min. Must explain zero total charge and the meaning of the numeral.
                                            4. Self-check · 2 min. Students self-check, then you show the sample. Score 0 to 2 per part.
