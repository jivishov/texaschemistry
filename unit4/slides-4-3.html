Prefix names

Two nonmetals

Carbon and oxygen form both CO and CO2. So a name must count the atoms.

First element

Write the whole name of the first element. Leave mono- off it.

Second element

Give the second element the ending -ide. A prefix counts its atoms.

One name

CO2 is carbon dioxide.

Two prefixes

N2O4 is dinitrogen tetraoxide. Both elements take a prefix here.

One of each

CO has one carbon atom and one oxygen atom.

Mono- stays

The second element always takes a prefix, even mono-. So CO is carbon monoxide.

Rule

Name the first element, then the second with -ide. A prefix counts the atoms of each element. Leave mono- off the first element.

B. Tri- counts the three oxygen atoms. The first element takes no mono-.

Reading §4.3.1: write the whole name of the first element, then the root of the second with -ide; a prefix counts the atoms of each; mono- is left off the first element; the second always takes a prefix, even mono- (CO, carbon monoxide). IUPAC keeps every letter when a prefix meets a vowel except mono + oxide = monoxide: tetraoxide (tetroxide accepted, notes only). Thread: the counting code. Di- counts atoms the way bi- counts wheels.

Formulas and accepted names

Name to formula

Each prefix gives the subscript of the element after it.

The formula

Di- gives N2 and tri- gives O3: N2O3.

Same ratio

NO2 and N2O4 have the same 1 : 2 ratio. They are different substances.

Never reduced

NO2 is a reddish-brown gas. N2O4 is colorless. A molecular formula is never reduced.

Accepted names

Some compounds were known before the naming rules. They keep their older names.

Rule

A molecular formula counts the atoms in one molecule. It is never reduced. Water, ammonia and methane keep their accepted names.

Check

The formula of dinitrogen trioxide is…

  1. ANO3
  2. BN3O2
  3. CN2O3

C. Di- gives two N, tri- gives three O.

Reading §4.3.1: a molecular formula counts the atoms in one molecule and is never reduced; NO₂ (reddish-brown) and N₂O₄ (colorless) have the same 1 : 2 ratio but are different substances. Water, ammonia and methane keep their accepted names. Honors (notes only): two NO₂ molecules join to form N₂O₄. Thread: the counting code. Each prefix turns back into a subscript; a formula counts the atoms in one molecule.

Counting valence electrons

Group number

The group number gives the valence electrons. For groups 13 to 18, subtract 10.

Dots

Each dot stands for one valence electron.

Count every atom

Add the electrons of every single atom. Count each atom, even when an element repeats.

Carbon dioxide

CO2 has 4 + 6 + 6 = 16 valence electrons.

Fixed total

The total never changes while you draw. Bonds do not make or destroy electrons.

Rule

Count first: add the valence electrons of every atom. A correct Lewis structure shows exactly that many.

Check

How many valence electrons does NH3 have?

  1. A5
  2. B8
  3. C10

B. 5 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 8.

Reading §4.3.2: the group number gives the valence electrons of a main-group atom; in groups 13 to 18 subtract 10 (C 4, N 5, O 6, Cl 7; H 1). The total is fixed while drawing: bonds neither create nor destroy electrons. Thread: the seating chart. Count the seats first: every valence electron needs one.

Bonding pairs and lone pairs

Two kinds of pairs

A bonding pair is shared by two atoms. A lone pair belongs to just one atom.

Around one atom

Add its pairs at 2 electrons each. A shared pair counts for both of its atoms.

Ammonia

Ammonia has three bonding pairs and one lone pair.

Methane

Methane has four bonding pairs and no lone pairs.

Rule

A line is a bonding pair shared by two atoms. Two dots are a lone pair on one atom. Each stands for 2 electrons.

Check

HCl has 8 valence electrons. One pair is the H–Cl bond. How many lone pairs sit on Cl?

  1. Atwo
  2. Bthree
  3. Cfour

B. 4 pairs in all. One is the bond, so three are lone pairs.

Reading §4.3.2: a bonding pair is shared by two atoms and drawn as a line; a lone pair belongs to one atom and is drawn as two dots; each stands for 2 electrons. Around one atom, count its bonding pairs and lone pairs at 2 electrons each; a shared pair counts for both atoms. Thread: the seating chart. Seats come in pairs: a line or two dots.

Count, bond, fill: ammonia

Four steps for NH3

  1. SkeletonN is central. H is never central.
  2. Count5 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 8 valence electrons
  3. BondThree N–H bonds use 6.
  4. Fill2 are left: one lone pair on N. H never holds lone pairs.
  5. CheckN has 8. Each H has 2.

Rule

Count, bond, fill, share. Single bonds join the outer atoms to the central atom. The electrons left become lone pairs, outer atoms first.

Check

In H2O, two O–H bonds use 4 of 8. Where do the other 4 go?

  1. Atwo lone pairs on O
  2. Bone lone pair on each H
  3. Cthey are left out

A. H holds none; O takes both.

Reading §4.3.3: count, bond, fill, share. Ammonia: 5 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 8; three N–H bonds use 6; 2 are left; hydrogen never holds lone pairs, so the last pair goes on nitrogen. N then has 8 and each H has 2, so no share is needed. The central atom is bonded to the most atoms; H is never central. Thread: the seating chart. Bond first, then seat the rest as lone pairs, outer atoms first.

Rule

Double bonds

Single bonds first

  1. Count4 + 6 + 6 = 16
  2. BondTwo single bonds use 4.
  3. FillThe other 12 go on the two O atoms.
  4. ShortEach O has 8. C has only 4.

Predict

How can carbon reach 8 without adding electrons?

Share

  1. ShareEach O shares one lone pair with C. Each link becomes a double bond.
  2. Done4 bonding pairs + 4 lone pairs = 8 pairs = 16

Rule

An atom can be short of an octet. A neighbor’s lone pair then becomes another shared pair. A double bond is two shared pairs.

Check

After sharing, how many electrons does the CO2 drawing show?

  1. A16
  2. B20
  3. C12

A. Sharing moves electrons. It never adds any.

Atom colors show the element, not the charge.

Reading §4.3.4 Double bonds: in CO₂ two single bonds use 4 of 16; the other 12 give each oxygen three lone pairs; carbon has only 4. One lone pair on each oxygen becomes a second shared pair: two double bonds. The total stays 16: four bonding pairs and four lone pairs. Misconception to listen for: "multiple bonds add electrons". Thread: the seating chart. When the seats run out, two atoms share a bench: a double bond, no new electrons.

Triple bonds

Single bond first

  1. Count5 + 5 = 10 valence electrons
  2. BondOne single bond uses 2.
  3. FillThe 8 left fill only one N atom.
  4. ShortOne N has 8. The other has only 4.

Share two pairs

  1. Share 1One lone pair becomes a second shared pair.
  2. Share 2A second pair makes a triple bond. Each N keeps one lone pair and has 8.

Hard to break

This triple bond is very hard to break. That is why nitrogen in air rarely reacts. Factories break it with heat, pressure and a catalyst.

Rule

A triple bond is three shared pairs. In N2, each nitrogen atom also keeps one lone pair, so each has 8.

Check

In N≡N, how many electrons are in the triple bond?

  1. A3
  2. B6
  3. C10

B. Each line is 2 electrons. Three lines are 6.

Atom colors show the element, not the charge.

Reading §4.3.4 Triple bonds: N₂ has 10 valence electrons; a single bond uses 2; the remaining 8 can give only one nitrogen an octet; sharing two more pairs makes a triple bond, and each nitrogen keeps one lone pair. The bond is very hard to break, which is why nitrogen in air is so unreactive; ammonia factories use an iron catalyst at 400 to 500 °C and 150 to 250 atm. Thread: the seating chart. Three shared benches make a triple bond; still no new electrons.

Checking a structure

Atom colors show the element, not the charge.

Three checks

  1. 1Count the electrons drawn: 2 per line, 2 per dot pair.
  2. 2Compare with the valence count.
  3. 3Check each atom: 8, or 2 for hydrogen.

Not the shape

A Lewis structure shows bonds and lone pairs. It does not show the shape. Water drawn straight and water drawn bent are the same structure.

3D model

A 3D model adds the angles. Lesson 4.4 explains them.

Rule

Check the total first, then each atom: 8 electrons, or 2 for hydrogen. A Lewis structure shows bonds and lone pairs, not the shape.

Reading §4.3.5: check every drawing in the same order: count the electrons drawn (2 per line, 2 per dot pair), compare with the valence count, then check each atom for 8, or 2 for hydrogen. A Lewis structure shows the bonds and the pairs, not the shape. In ammonia the three H sit below N like a short stool; the H–N–H angle measures 106.7° (NIST CCCBDB). Lesson 4.4 explains the shapes.
