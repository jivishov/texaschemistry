4.4

Molecular shapes: the VSEPR model

Carbon dioxide and water each contain three atoms, yet they behave very differently. At room temperature, carbon dioxide is a gas, while water is a liquid that dissolves salt and sugar. Part of the difference comes from shape, because a carbon dioxide molecule is straight and a water molecule is bent. A (electron dot structure) shows the bonds and lone pairs in a molecule, but not its shape. This chapter uses a model called VSEPR to predict the shape from the Lewis structure.Carbon dioxide and water each have three atoms, yet they act very differently. At room temperature, carbon dioxide is a gas. Water is a liquid that dissolves salt. Part of the reason is their shape. A carbon dioxide molecule is straight, and a water molecule is bent. A Lewis structure (electron dot structure) does not show shape. This chapter uses a model called VSEPR to predict it.

§4.4.1

Electron domains

The shape of a molecule depends on the electrons around its . Each region where these electrons are found is called an . A lone pair on the central atom is one domain. A bond to another atom is also one domain, whether it is a single, a double or a triple bond.A molecule's shape depends on the electrons around its central atom. Each region of these electrons is called an electron domain. A lone pair on the central atom is one domain. Each bond to another atom is one domain too, even a double or triple bond.

To count the domains, start from the Lewis structure. Count the atoms bonded to the central atom, then add the lone pairs on the central atom. Lone pairs on the outer atoms do not count, because they belong to those atoms. In ammonia, NH₃, nitrogen has three bonds and one lone pair, so it has four domains.Start from the Lewis structure to count domains. Count the atoms bonded to the central atom, then add its lone pairs. Skip the lone pairs on the outer atoms. Ammonia's nitrogen has three bonds and one lone pair, so it has four domains.

Key idea

Count the domains on the central atom: one for each bonded atom and one for each lone pair.

§4.4.1

Multiple bonds count once

A double bond holds two shared pairs, and a triple bond holds three. All of these pairs lie between the same two atoms, so they point in the same direction from the central atom. For that reason, a double or triple bond counts as one domain, just as a single bond does.A double bond holds two shared pairs, and a triple bond holds three. All of these pairs sit between the same two atoms. So they point the same way from the central atom. That is why each bond counts as just one domain.

In hydrogen cyanide, H–C≡N, carbon forms a single bond to hydrogen and a triple bond to nitrogen. Carbon has no lone pairs, so it has two domains. The lone pair on nitrogen belongs to nitrogen and is not counted.In HCN, carbon has a single bond to H and a triple bond to N. Carbon has no lone pairs, so it has two domains. The lone pair on N belongs to N, so it does not count.

§4.4.2

Domains spread apart

Every electron domain is a region of negative charge, so the domains repel one another. The , short for valence shell electron pair repulsion, states that the domains around a central atom spread as far apart as possible. The directions in which they settle are called the .Electron domains are all negative, so they push each other away. The VSEPR model says they spread as far apart as they can. The directions they end up in are called the electron-domain arrangement.

The number of domains decides the arrangement. Two domains point in opposite directions, 180° apart, which is a linear arrangement. Three domains spread into a flat triangle, 120° apart, which is trigonal planar. Four domains point to the corners of a tetrahedron, 109.5° apart, which is tetrahedral.The number of domains sets the arrangement. Two domains point opposite ways, 180° apart, which is called linear. Three make a flat triangle, 120° apart, called trigonal planar. Four point to the corners of a tetrahedron, 109.5° apart. This is called tetrahedral.

Key idea

Domains repel, so they spread as far apart as possible. Two are linear, three are trigonal planar and four are tetrahedral.

§4.4.2

Four domains in three dimensions

A Lewis structure is drawn on flat paper, so the four bonds of methane, CH₄, appear 90° apart. A real molecule is three-dimensional, and in three dimensions four domains can spread farther apart. Methane's bonds point to the four corners of a tetrahedron, a pyramid with four triangular faces, and every H–C–H angle is 109.5°.A Lewis structure is drawn on flat paper, so methane's bonds look 90° apart. A real molecule is not flat. In 3D, four domains can spread farther apart than that. Methane's bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron. Every H–C–H angle in methane is 109.5°.

The angle between two bonds that share an atom is called a . Each arrangement has its own ideal bond angle. Measured angles come close to these values when every domain is a bond, as in methane, carbon dioxide and boron trifluoride.The angle between two bonds on one atom is a bond angle. Each arrangement has its own ideal angle. Real angles are close to these when every domain is a bond, as in methane.

§4.4.3

Lone pairs and molecular shape

The of a molecule is named from the positions of its atoms only. Lone pairs still take up room, but they are not atoms, so they are left out of the name. When the central atom has no lone pairs, the geometry matches the arrangement. When it has lone pairs, the two names differ.The molecular geometry is named from the atoms only. Lone pairs still take up room, but they are not atoms. So the name of the shape leaves them out. With no lone pairs, the shape matches the arrangement. With lone pairs, the two names are different.

Methane, ammonia and water all have four domains in a tetrahedral arrangement. Methane's four domains are all bonds, so the molecule is . Ammonia has one lone pair, and its three hydrogen atoms form a pyramid below nitrogen, a shape. Water has two lone pairs, so its three atoms form a shape.Methane, ammonia and water each have four domains. All four of methane's domains are bonds, so methane is tetrahedral. Ammonia has one lone pair on its nitrogen atom. Its three H atoms make a pyramid below N, a trigonal pyramidal shape. Water has two lone pairs, so it is bent.

§4.4.4

Wedges and dashes

A dashed wedge is a bond that points behind the page, away from you.A wedge-and-dash drawing shows a 3D shape on flat paper. A plain line is a bond in the page. Linear and trigonal planar molecules are flat, so plain lines are enough to draw them. §4.4.5

Polar molecules

Lesson 4.1 showed that a bond between two atoms with different values is a . The more electronegative atom pulls the shared electrons toward itself and carries a , δ−. Whether a whole molecule is polar depends on two features: its polar bonds and its shape.Lesson 4.1 showed that some bonds are polar. One atom pulls the shared electrons harder than the other. That atom carries a partial negative charge, δ−. Whether a whole molecule is polar depends on its polar bonds and its shape.

In carbon dioxide, each C=O bond is polar, but the molecule is linear. The two oxygen atoms pull equally hard in opposite directions, so the pulls cancel and CO₂ is a . Water's two O–H bonds are polar too, but the molecule is bent. Both pulls point partly toward oxygen, so they add up, and water is a .In CO₂, each C=O bond is polar, but the molecule is linear. The two O atoms pull equally hard in opposite ways. The pulls cancel, so CO₂ is nonpolar. Water's O–H bonds are polar too, but water is bent. Both pulls point toward O, so water is polar.

Ammonia is polar for the same reason, because its three N–H pulls all point partly toward nitrogen. Methane is nonpolar, since each C–H bond has an electronegativity difference of only 0.35 and the four bonds point evenly in all directions.Ammonia is polar for the same reason, since its three pulls all point toward N. Methane is nonpolar. Its C–H bonds are nearly nonpolar, and they point evenly in all directions.

Key idea

Equal polar bonds spread evenly around the central atom cancel, so the molecule is nonpolar. In a bent or pyramidal shape they do not cancel, so the molecule is polar.

§4.4.5

A model with limits

In 1940, Nevil Sidgwick and Herbert Powell gave a lecture to the Royal Society of London. They proposed that a molecule's shape follows from the number of electron pairs around its central atom. In 1957, Ronald Gillespie and Ronald Nyholm turned the idea into a more detailed set of rules. Their work became the VSEPR model used in this chapter.In 1940, the chemists Nevil Sidgwick and Herbert Powell proposed an idea. A molecule's shape follows from the electron pairs around its central atom. In 1957, Ronald Gillespie and Ronald Nyholm added more detailed rules. Their work became the VSEPR model.

The model predicts all five shapes in this chapter, and it explains why lone pairs make angles smaller. It does not give exact angles, which come from measurements. It also does not describe how electrons move, so the animations show only the arrangement with the least repulsion.The model predicts all five shapes in this chapter. It also explains why lone pairs make angles smaller. It does not give exact angles, which come from measurements. It does not show how electrons move, either.

Limit of the model

Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, has four domains like water, yet its H–S–H angle measures 92.1°. Phosphine, PH₃, has four domains like ammonia, and its angle measures 93.3°. VSEPR predicts angles slightly under 109.5° for both, so the simple model does not fit them.

Key idea

VSEPR is a model. It predicts the five shapes well, but not every exact angle, and it does not show electrons moving.

Honors · Polarity of all five shapes, and SO₂

HCN is linear, but its two ends differ. Nitrogen pulls harder than hydrogen does, so HCN is polar. CH₂O is trigonal planar, and its C=O bond is far more polar than its C–H bonds, so CH₂O is polar. BF₃ is trigonal planar with three identical B–F bonds, so their pulls cancel and BF₃ is nonpolar.

Sulfur dioxide, SO₂, is bent with only three domains: two bonds and one lone pair on sulfur. Its measured O–S–O angle is 119.5°. One Lewis structure shows one S=O bond and one S–O bond, yet both measured bonds are 143.2 pm long. Chemists draw two resonance structures for SO₂ and treat the real molecule as a blend of the two.
