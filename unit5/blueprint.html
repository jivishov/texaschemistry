The Mole: unit blueprint

One set of objectives drives four surfaces: a student reading, a teacher slide deck, missions and a case file. Every check item carries an objective ID, so alignment to TEKS C.8(A), C.8(B), C.8(C) and C.8(D) can be traced item by item.

1. The package

Eighteen pages: five readings, five slide decks, five mission sets, one case file, this blueprint and the unit index. Each surface has one job, and none of them tests content that the reading does not teach.

ReadStudent reading in book or continuous mode, with a simpler-language toggle, worked examples, stop-and-check items and interactives.
TeachTeacher deck. Each slide shows its reading section, and the notes give timings and the wrong answers to expect.
PracticeMissions. Everyday samples and real data for each idea, with feedback on every wrong answer.
ApplyCase file. One Texas decision that needs the whole unit.

Teacher flow, one lesson

  1. Open the deck. The driving question and three predictions come first.
  2. Teach through the deck. Students follow the matching reading section or read it as homework.
  3. Students work the worked examples and stop-and-check items, then the missions.
  4. The CER exit ticket and the self-rating close the lesson. Students write in their notebooks.

Design rules

  • Every number is computed from the formula by one shared calculator, so the same value appears on every surface.
  • One objective ID per item. Every wrong choice and every common wrong number gets feedback that names the faulty step.
  • Writing happens in notebooks. Pages offer choices, numbers, sliders and reveals, never text boxes.
  • Always name the particle: atoms, molecules, formula units or ions.
  • Models are labeled: ball-and-stick molecules use measured shapes, and drawings are marked as illustrations.

4. Lesson 5.1 · Counting particles with the mole

Driving question. A bag of nickels: a bank counts coins by weighing, and because every nickel has a mass of 5.000 g, a bag whose coins have a mass of 1,250.0 g holds exactly 250 nickels. How can chemists count particles that are far too small and too many to count one by one?

Objectives

IDStudents willTEKSSlides
5.1.1Explain how a large number of identical objects can be counted by weighing, using one object's mass or the average mass of a mixture.C.8(A)5, 6
5.1.2Define the mole as the amount of substance that contains exactly 6.02214076 × 1023 specified particles, and use Avogadro's number and the Avogadro constant correctly.C.8(A)7 to 11, 15
5.1.3Convert between moles and numbers of atoms, molecules, formula units or ions, always naming the particle.C.8(B)12, 13
5.1.4Use a chemical formula to find the moles and numbers of atoms of each element in a sample of a compound.C.8(B)14

Evidence and model

Counting by weighing works when every piece has the same mass (the nickel, 5.000 g) or when the average mass of a mix is known. A jar of cents is the second case: most cents made before 1982 are 95% copper and weigh 3.11 g, later ones are copper-plated zinc and weigh 2.500 g, and 1982 has both. That is the same idea as the average atomic mass of Lesson 2.3. The mole is the chemist's counting unit. Since May 20, 2019 one mole holds exactly 6.02214076 × 1023 specified particles; calculations use 6.022 × 1023, and the pages say it is rounded. The older definition (as many particles as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12) appears once, as history, because older books still print it. The size of the number: counting one particle each second, 6.022 × 1023 s is about 1.9 × 1016 years, about 1.4 million times the age of the universe (13.8 billion years). The number was last measured by counting the atoms in spheres of silicon enriched in silicon-28, from the sphere's volume and the spacing of its atoms. History runs from Avogadro's hypothesis (1811) and Loschmidt's estimate (1865) to Ostwald's word "Mol" (the 1890s), Perrin's measurements (1909; Nobel Prize in Physics, 1926), the mole as an SI base unit (1971) and the redefinition (voted November 16, 2018; in force May 20, 2019). The lesson closes the loop on the kJ/mol of Unit 3.

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
A mole is a mass or a volume.A mole is a count, like a dozen or a ream. One mole of copper (63.546 g) and one mole of aluminum (26.982 g) hold the same number of atoms.
A jar of mixed cents can be counted with the average of the two coin masses.Halving 2.500 g plus 3.11 g works only if the jar holds equal numbers of each kind. The jar needs the average mass of its own mix.
To get particles, divide by Avogadro's number.More moles means more particles: 2.00 mol of copper × 6.022 × 1023 = 1.20 × 1024 atoms. Dividing goes from particles to moles.
A count smaller than 6.022 × 1023 cannot be written in moles.Moles can be fractions: 3.011 × 1023 molecules is 0.5000 mol.
Ionic compounds are made of molecules, or the particle does not matter.Sodium chloride is counted in formula units. "2.50 mol of sodium chloride contains 1.51 × 1024 molecules" has the right number and the wrong particle.
One mole of water contains one mole of hydrogen atoms.The subscript multiplies: 1 mol of H2O holds 2 mol of H atoms and 1 mol of O atoms; 0.50 mol of methane holds 2.0 mol of H atoms.
The silicon spheres were weighed to find the mass of one atom directly.The project counted atoms: the sphere's volume divided by the volume each atom occupies, from the spacing measured by X-rays.

5. Lesson 5.2 · Molar mass

Driving question. One mole of copper and sugar: a lab procedure calls for one mole of each, but no balance reads in moles, and no one can count 6.022 × 1023 atoms. How can a chemist weigh out one mole of any substance?

Objectives

IDStudents willTEKSSlides
5.2.1Calculate the molar mass of an element, a diatomic element, a molecular compound and an ionic compound, including formulas with parentheses.C.8(A)5 to 10, 15
5.2.2Explain why the molar mass of a substance in grams per mole has the same number as the average mass of one particle in atomic mass units.C.8(A)11, 12
5.2.3Use molar mass to convert between the mass of a sample in grams and the amount in moles.C.8(A)13, 14

Evidence and model

The molar mass of an element is its average atomic mass from the periodic table in g/mol (Lesson 2.3 explained why it is an average). A diatomic element counts both atoms: O2 is 32.00 g/mol, not 16.00. A compound adds every atom in its formula, and a subscript outside parentheses multiplies everything inside: (NH4)2SO4 is 132.13 g/mol. An ionic compound's molar mass is per formula unit; the electron sodium loses goes to chlorine, so the total mass does not change. The number matches the mass in u because the mole was chosen that way: 6.022 × 1023 u is 1 g. Grams to moles divides by the molar mass, and moles to grams multiplies, written as n = m/M and as a conversion factor whose units cancel. The one-mole bench shows each sample at true relative size.

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
The molar mass of carbon is its mass number (12) or its atomic number (6).The periodic table gives 12.011, the average over carbon's isotopes, so one mole of carbon is 12.011 g.
The molar mass of oxygen gas is 16.00 g/mol.Oxygen gas is O2, two atoms per molecule: 32.00 g/mol.
A subscript outside parentheses multiplies only the atom beside it.(NH4)2SO4 has two N and eight H: 132.13 g/mol. One ammonium group gives 114.10 and doubling only N gives 128.10.
An ionic compound has a different molar mass from its atoms because ions gain or lose electrons.The electron that Na loses goes to Cl, so NaCl has the mass of one Na atom and one Cl atom, 58.44 g/mol.
The match between u and g/mol is a coincidence.The mole was defined so that 6.022 × 1023 u is 1 g. The Honors box shows how close the match is today.
To find moles, multiply the mass by the molar mass.10.0 g of sucrose ÷ 342.30 g/mol = 0.0292 mol. Multiplying gives 3,423 and dividing the wrong way gives 34.2: both are far more than a 10 g spoonful could be.
One mole of every substance has the same mass or takes the same space.The bench: same number of particles, very different masses and volumes.

6. Lesson 5.3 · Converting mass, moles and particles

Driving question. A one-carat diamond: one metric carat is exactly 0.200 g, and a diamond is almost pure carbon. How can weighing a sample tell you how many atoms are in it?

Objectives

IDStudents willTEKSSlides
5.3.1Convert between the mass of a sample and the number of particles in it, going through moles and setting up each conversion factor so that units cancel.C.8(A) C.8(B)5 to 8
5.3.2Enter numbers in scientific notation on a calculator correctly, and recognize the two common errors: dividing where you should multiply, and mistyping the power of ten.C.8(B)9 to 11
5.3.3Find the number of atoms of each element in a sample of a compound, and check that an answer is reasonable before reporting it.C.8(B)12 to 14

Evidence and model

Grams to particles always goes through moles: divide by the molar mass, then multiply by 6.022 × 1023, each factor written so the unit before it cancels. Treated as pure carbon (12.011 g/mol), the 0.200 g diamond is 0.01665 mol and 1.00 × 1022 carbon atoms, rounded to three significant figures like the data. The stone, a nickel and a ruler are drawn to the same scale, with a zoom into the diamond crystal. On a calculator, EE (or EXP) already means "× 10", so typing 6.022 × 10 EE 23 gives a value ten times too large; and a calculator works × and ÷ from left to right, so dividing by 6.022 × 10 ^ 23 without parentheses multiplies by 1023 instead of dividing. In a compound, moles of molecules times the subscript gives moles of each kind of atom. The reasonableness checks: a visible sample always holds a huge number of particles and a modest number of moles, and a sample smaller than one molar mass holds fewer than 6.022 × 1023 particles.

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
Avogadro's number alone goes straight from grams to atoms.Avogadro's number counts particles in a mole, not in a gram. The map has moles in the middle, and the units show which way each factor goes. The two factors may be combined into one, Avogadro's number ÷ molar mass in atoms per gram, which still passes through the mole.
Grams to particles multiplies by the molar mass.Multiplying the diamond's mass by 12.011 g/mol gives 1.45 × 1024 atoms, about 144 times the right 1.00 × 1022; the grams do not cancel.
The calculator is wrong when it gives a strange answer.It does exactly what was typed. 1.505 EE 24 ÷ 6.022 × 10 ^ 23 gives 2.499 × 1046; 1.505 EE 24 ÷ 6.022 EE 23 gives 2.499 mol.
The number of molecules is the number of atoms.11.0 g of CO2 is 0.250 mol and 1.51 × 1023 molecules, but 3.01 × 1023 oxygen atoms, because each molecule has two.
A very small or very large answer is fine if the steps look right.A visible sample cannot hold 10−25 atoms or 1046 moles. The size check catches the error before it is reported.

7. Lesson 5.4 · Percent composition

Driving question. Two iron minerals: hematite, Fe2O3, and magnetite, Fe3O4, supply most of the iron ore mined in the United States and contain only iron and oxygen. Which pure mineral holds more iron in each kilogram? How can you find what share of a compound's mass comes from each element?

Objectives

IDStudents willTEKSSlides
5.4.1Calculate the percent composition of a compound by mass from its chemical formula.C.8(C)5 to 7
5.4.2Use percent composition to find the mass of an element in a sample, and tell a percent by mass from a percent of atoms.C.8(C)8 to 10
5.4.3Calculate percent composition from laboratory masses, and decide whether a stated error would make the result too high or too low.C.8(C)11 to 14

Evidence and model

Percent by mass of an element = (atoms of it in the formula × its molar mass) ÷ the compound's molar mass × 100, built on the law of definite proportions from Lesson 2.1. Water is 11.19% hydrogen and 88.81% oxygen, although two of its three atoms are hydrogen. Rounded percentages can add to 99.99% or 100.01%, and the reading says why. Table salt is 39.34% sodium; the Dietary Guidelines for Americans keep people 14 and older under 2,300 mg of sodium a day, which the FDA calls about one teaspoon of salt, and the reading checks it (5.85 g × 0.3934 = 2.30 g, while a level teaspoon holds about 6 g). The mass of an element in a sample is the percent times the sample mass; hematite (Fe2O3, 69.94% Fe) and magnetite (Fe3O4, 72.36% Fe) compare the iron in each kilogram of pure mineral, and the pages say a real ore is rock that holds these minerals, so it holds less iron. In the simulated crucible experiment pure MgO is 60.30% magnesium. Each error moves only the product mass: smoke escaping, heating too briefly and magnesium nitride forming make the product too light and the percent too high; water absorbed makes it too heavy and the percent too low. Honors: the water in copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate (249.68 g/mol, 36.08% water) and heating a hydrate to constant mass.

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
Percent by mass is the percent of atoms.Two of water's three atoms are hydrogen (67%), yet hydrogen is 11.19% of its mass.
Count each element once, whatever its subscript.Urea, CO(NH2)2, has two nitrogen atoms: 46.65% N. Counting one gives 23.32%.
Divide by the sum of every atomic mass, or by the number of atoms.Divide by the molar mass of the compound, the mass of one mole of its formula.
Percentages that do not add to exactly 100 show a mistake.Rounding each percent can make the total 99.99% or 100.01%.
The mineral with the larger molar mass holds more iron.Magnetite (231.53 g/mol) is 72.36% iron and hematite (159.69 g/mol) 69.94%: the share decides, not the size of the formula.
Any laboratory error makes the result too low.The magnesium mass stays correct; only the product mass moves. A product that is too light gives a percent of magnesium that is too high.

8. Lesson 5.5 · Empirical and molecular formulas

Driving question. Formaldehyde, acetic acid and glucose: a sharp-smelling gas, the acid in vinegar and the sugar in blood are all 40.00% carbon, 6.71% hydrogen and 53.28% oxygen by mass. How can different substances have the same percent composition?

Objectives

IDStudents willTEKSSlides
5.5.1Tell an empirical formula from a molecular formula, and reduce a molecular formula to its empirical formula.C.8(D)5 to 8
5.5.2Find a molecular formula from an empirical formula and a molar mass.C.8(D)9 to 11
5.5.3Extension: find an empirical formula from percent composition or from masses, including ratios that must be multiplied to whole numbers.C.8(D) Extension12 to 15

Evidence and model

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms; the molecular formula gives the actual number of each atom in one molecule. Reducing divides by the greatest common factor. Some formulas are already simplest (H2O, CO2, CH4). The families are CH2O (formaldehyde, acetic acid C2H4O2, glucose C6H12O6), CH (acetylene C2H2, benzene C6H6), HO (hydrogen peroxide H2O2) and NO2 (dinitrogen tetraoxide N2O4). NO2 is also a real molecule, nitrogen dioxide, so an empirical formula can name a substance of its own. The multiple k is the molar mass ÷ the empirical formula mass: acetic acid 60.05 ÷ 30.03 = 2, glucose 180.16 ÷ 30.03 = 6. An ionic formula is the ratio of ions in a formula unit and is never called a molecular formula; the ionic formulas in this unit are simplest ratios, but the teacher notes warn that a few are not (Hg2Cl2, Na2O2). The labeled extension goes from percent to grams in a 100 g sample, to moles, to division by the smallest, and multiplies a ratio such as 1.5 or 1.33 to whole numbers instead of rounding it. Honors: combustion analysis, and a hydrate's formula from heating data.

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
Substances with the same percent composition are the same substance.Formaldehyde, acetic acid and glucose share 40.00% C, 6.71% H and 53.28% O but are a gas, a liquid and a solid.
The empirical formula is the formula of the molecule.Benzene is C6H6; its empirical formula CH gives only the ratio. The molar mass is needed for the molecule.
Any common factor gives the empirical formula.Divide every subscript by the greatest common factor: C6H12O6 ÷ 6 gives CH2O. Dividing by 2 gives C3H6O3, a smaller ratio but not the simplest.
The multiple is the empirical formula mass ÷ the molar mass.The division upside down gives 0.200 for a k of 5; k is always a whole number of 1 or more.
A ratio of 1.5 can be rounded to 2.Rounding changes the formula. Multiply every ratio by 2 so that 1.5 becomes 3.
NO2 can only be an empirical formula.Nitrogen dioxide, NO2, is a real molecule. N2O4 shares its empirical formula.
NaCl is the molecular formula of salt.Salt has no molecules. NaCl gives the ratio of ions in a formula unit.

9. Case file: Checking a fertilizer label

Students act as analysts at a fictional Texas testing laboratory. A fictional farm supply cooperative on the Texas High Plains, which sells fertilizer to cotton growers and to lawn and garden customers, asks one question: does each product match its label?

Briefing

The memo explains the task and lists the six files with their TEKS, beside a 3D bench with a 46-0-0 bag and a laboratory balance drawn to scale. A fact box gives the real context: Texas law defines a grade as the whole-number percentages of total nitrogen, available phosphoric acid (P2O5) and soluble potash (K2O) guaranteed (Agriculture Code, Sections 63.001 and 63.021); the label lists minimum percentages (Section 63.051); the Texas Feed and Fertilizer Control Service, run by the Office of the Texas State Chemist, may sample fertilizer (Section 63.091) and attach a stop-sale order that lapses after 30 days unless it goes to court (Section 63.121); and Texas grew 4.03 million bales of upland cotton in 2024, of 13.94 million in the United States (USDA NASS, December 9, 2025). The cooperative, the laboratory, the supplier, the bags and every measured result are fictional, and the page says so. Prices and brands are not part of the case.

Each file has three steps: a calculation, the Unit 5 idea it uses, then a decision about a claim. Every molar mass and percent is computed from the formula by the shared calculator.

File 1 · What 46-0-0 means · C.8(C)

Urea, CO(NH2)2, 60.056 g/mol. Task: 28.014 g ÷ 60.056 g × 100 = 46.65% nitrogen; decide what the 46 means (a guaranteed minimum written as a whole number, which nearly pure urea meets with a small margin), not 46% urea and not a rounding rule for N2 gas.

File 2 · Counting what is in the sample · C.8(A) C.8(B)

A 5.000 g sample, treated as pure urea, beside a 3D model of the molecule. Task: 0.083256 mol, 5.014 × 1022 molecules and 1.003 × 1023 nitrogen atoms; choose the reasoning that goes grams to moles to molecules to atoms.

File 3 · Two nitrogen sources · C.8(C)

Urea (46-0-0) against ammonium sulfate (21-0-0, 132.13 g/mol). Task: ammonium sulfate is 21.20% nitrogen; 10.0 kg of nitrogen needs 21.44 kg of urea or 47.17 kg of ammonium sulfate; explain why the same two nitrogen atoms in a heavier formula give less nitrogen per kilogram (the rest is mostly sulfate, 24.26% S).

File 4 · An unknown slow-release product · C.8(D)

A product offered for lawn and garden customers: 41.37% C, 8.10% H, 32.16% N and 18.37% O, with a measured molar mass near 174 g/mol. Task: moles in 100 g and ratios give the empirical formula C3H7N2O (87.10 g/mol); 174 ÷ 87.10 gives k = 2, so the molecular formula is C6H14N4O2, which fits isobutylidene diurea (IBDU), 174.20 g/mol, among the choices offered (a formula alone cannot rule out an isomer). A 3D model from the database lets students check the formula.

File 5 · Reading P and K · C.8(C)

Why a grade reports phosphorus as P2O5 and potassium as K2O (a convention; the bag contains neither oxide), with the shares 0.4364 and 0.8302. Task: pure KCl would report 63.18% K2O (0-0-63), so a 0-0-60 bag that meets its guarantee must be at least 94.97% KCl; answer a customer who reads 0-0-60 as 60% potassium (the guarantee means at least 49.8%).

File 6 · Does the bag match the label? · C.8(C) SEP 2(B)

Two bags labeled 21-0-0, measured with the laboratory's uncertainty of ±0.3 percentage points: Bag A 20.8% (20.5% to 21.1%), Bag B 20.4% (20.1% to 20.7%). The laboratory's own rule, labeled as part of its fictional report: a shortfall is reported only when the whole range lies below the guarantee. Task: Bag B's highest value is 20.7%, so only Bag B shows a shortfall; Bag B is about 96.23% ammonium sulfate; Bag A's range includes 21%, so more samples are needed.

Recommendation · claim, evidence, reasoning · SEP 3(A), 3(C)

Students choose a claim (hold the shipment and ask the state to test an official sample because Bag B falls short; hold the ammonium sulfate until more bags are tested; or reject the whole shipment because both bags measured below 21%), select at least three files, and mark what the tests cannot show. They then write the reasoning in their notebooks using sentence starters and a word bank. The check requires File 6 and at least one formula file (File 1 or File 3), and the four limits: whether the other bags match the two tested, whether the urea bag itself is pure enough to meet its guarantee (at least about 98.6% urea), whether Bag A falls short by less than the uncertainty, and how fast IBDU releases its nitrogen in soil. The third claim gets feedback that Bag A's range includes 21%. After a successful check, a sample report for the chosen claim appears for comparison.

Grading the notebook. A strong report compares each measured result with a value computed from the formula, uses the uncertainty to decide what the test can and cannot show, and names what more sampling would settle. A report that calls Bag A short because 20.8 is less than 21 ignores the uncertainty and misses the point of File 6.

Case-file boundary. The decision rule in File 6 is the fictional laboratory's own. No allowance for a shortfall was found in Agriculture Code Chapter 63, so the case does not apply a state rule, and only the state service can take an official sample or issue a stop-sale order. Crop agronomy, prices and how fast nitrogen is released in soil are context only and are not assessed. Ammonium nitrate is not part of the case.

11. Using the results

What the pages record

  • Each mission set shows the cases solved and the first-try accuracy, so a first answer can be told apart from eventual completion.
  • Numeric answers accept stated rounding; the reference values stay fixed. Each common wrong number (multiplying where one should divide, a factor upside down, one atom counted instead of two) gets its own feedback.
  • Progress is saved in the student's own browser only. Nothing is sent to a server, so the pages are not a gradebook.

What they do not show

  • A correct number does not prove a correct setup. Ask for the units written and cancelled in the notebook.
  • Judge explanations from notebooks and discussion: the exit tickets, the checkpoints and the case file report.
  • A completed self-rating is a reflection, not proof of mastery.

Each deck runs checkpoints as Think, Talk, Write, Self-check and Compare, with 30 second thinking and 60 second partner timers. The three predictions from slide 3 are revisited near the end, and the CER exit ticket returns to the driving question.

13. Data and accuracy notes

  • One shared calculator. Molar masses and percentages are computed from each formula with the course's average atomic masses (CIAAW abridged standard atomic weights), so the same value appears on every surface. Molar masses are shown to two decimals in most places and to three where a division is printed, so the division shown gives the value shown.
  • The Avogadro constant. Exact since May 20, 2019 at 6.02214076 × 1023 mol−1; calculations use 6.022 × 1023, and the pages say it is rounded. Answers are given to the significant figures of the data (the diamond: 1.00 × 1022 atoms from 0.200 g).
  • Coins. The nickel (5.000 g) and the post-1982 cent (2.500 g) are from the U.S. Mint's specifications; the 3.11 g of the older cent was found only in secondary sources. The Mint struck its last circulating cent on November 12, 2025, so no page describes the cent as made today.
  • Models and scale. Molecule shapes use measured bond lengths and angles from NIST CCCBDB, and computed shapes from PubChem for acetic acid, glucose and IBDU. Crystal views use measured lattice constants: diamond 356.7 pm, silicon 543.102 pm, copper 361.5 pm and sodium chloride 564 pm (the value Unit 4 used). The one-mole bench, the diamond and the fertilizer bench are drawn to scale; diagrams that are not are labeled illustrations.
  • Laboratory data. The crucible runs, the IBDU analysis and the fertilizer bag results are simulated and say so. Pure MgO is 60.30% magnesium; real runs scatter around it for the reasons the lesson names.
  • Mission rounding. The 5.4 element-mass missions compute their answers from the two-decimal molar masses printed on the page, and their tolerances also accept the full-precision values.
  • Fertilizer facts. Grade and label rules are quoted from the current text of Agriculture Code Chapter 63. The oxide shares (0.4364, 0.8302) are computed from the course masses; the extension sheet prints 0.4365 and 0.8301. The cotton figures are from the December 9, 2025 Crop Production report and should be updated when a newer report appears.
