# Lesson 5.1: Counting particles with the mole  (lesson content sheet, written by the orchestrator in step 00 from the reading and the blueprint; every line verified against `unit5/reading-5-1.html` and `unit5/blueprint.html` on 2026-11-06)

Sources: `unit5/reading-5-1.html` (opener, §5.1.1 to §5.1.4 in nine sections plus a Review, nine "Watch / Finish it / On your own" examples, one Honors box, five "Before you read" statements, the glossary with Spanish terms, the item bank `u51-r1` to `u51-r14` and `u51-s1` to `u51-s8` in the page script), `unit5/blueprint.html` (section `l51`, lesson map, misconception table, data and accuracy notes, matrix rows C.8(A) and C.8(B)). TEKS C.8(A): define mole and apply the concept of molar mass to convert between moles and grams; C.8(B): calculate the number of atoms or molecules in a sample of material using Avogadro's number. This lesson defines the mole and converts between moles and particles; Lessons 5.2 and 5.3 add mass.

Driving question (blueprint): "A bag of nickels: a bank counts coins by weighing, and because every nickel has a mass of 5.000 g, a bag whose coins have a mass of 1,250.0 g holds exactly 250 nickels. How can chemists count particles that are far too small and too many to count one by one?" The reading's box: "How can chemists count particles that are far too small and too many to count one by one?" (17 words; the deck says it in two sentences, plan section 7).

Objectives (verbatim from the blueprint; four, not five):
- 5.1.1 Explain how a large number of identical objects can be counted by weighing, using one object's mass or the average mass of a mixture. (C.8(A))
- 5.1.2 Define the mole as the amount of substance that contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified particles, and use Avogadro's number and the Avogadro constant correctly. (C.8(A))
- 5.1.3 Convert between moles and numbers of atoms, molecules, formula units or ions, always naming the particle. (C.8(B))
- 5.1.4 Use a chemical formula to find the moles and numbers of atoms of each element in a sample of a compound. (C.8(B))

Reading sections and their key ideas (the reading's "Key idea" boxes, verbatim; the deck's rule cards restate them per RULES section 7):
- Opener: a bank teller counts a sealed bag of nickels by weighing; every nickel 5.000 g; 1,250.0 g holds exactly 250 nickels; "a few grams of any substance hold more atoms than anyone could ever count one by one"; the mole links a mass on a balance to a number of particles.
- §5.1.1 Counting by weighing: "When every object has the same mass, the number of objects equals the total mass divided by the mass of one object." The scale is zeroed with the empty bag on it; 1,250.0 g ÷ 5.000 g = 250 nickels; "the grams cancel, leaving a pure number".
- §5.1.1 Counting with an average mass: "A mixture of objects can be counted by weighing only through the average mass of one object. Average atomic masses let chemists count atoms the same way." Cents made before 1982 are 95% copper, 3.11 g; cents made after 1982 are zinc coated with copper, 2.500 g; in 1982 both kinds. Dividing by 2.500 g gives too many, by 3.11 g too few; weigh a sample, divide by its count, then divide the total by that average. Same idea as the average atomic mass of Lesson 2.3 (isotopes).
- §5.1.2 The mole: "One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ particles, rounded to 6.022 × 10²³ in calculations. The Avogadro constant is 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹." A baker counts eggs by the dozen; the mole, symbol mol, is the SI unit of amount of substance; since May 20, 2019 exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified particles (Avogadro's number); the rounded value "is the exact number written with fewer digits, not a measurement"; the Avogadro constant N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ "is used in calculations because its unit cancels moles"; a mole "is a count, like a dozen. It is not a mass or a volume"; one mole of aluminum atoms and one mole of copper atoms hold the same number of atoms but have different masses. Figure: 1 dozen = 12, 1 ream = 500, 1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³. "A mole must always name its particles": "one mole of oxygen" could mean O atoms or O₂ molecules, the second holding twice as many atoms; name the particle every time: atoms, molecules, formula units or ions.
- §5.1.2 The size of a mole: "Counting one mole of particles at one each second would take about 1.4 million times the age of the universe, yet a mole of water is only about 18 g." 6.022 × 10²³ s is about 1.9 × 10¹⁶ years; the universe is about 13.8 billion years old; one mole of water molecules has a mass of about 18 g, "less than a large spoonful"; "Avogadro's number is so large because atoms are so small".
- §5.1.2 How the number was found (no Key idea box; the facts): 1811 Amedeo Avogadro: equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of particles, "he never knew how large that number was"; 1865 Josef Loschmidt: the first estimate of the size of molecules, hence of the number; 1890s Wilhelm Ostwald: the word Mol as a unit; around 1909 Jean Perrin measured the number by several independent methods and proposed naming it after Avogadro, Nobel Prize in Physics 1926; 1971 the mole became an SI base unit, then defined as the amount with as many particles as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12, "a definition that older books still print"; the international Avogadro project counted the atoms in nearly perfect spheres of silicon; November 16, 2018 the vote to fix the number at exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³; in force May 20, 2019; "the number is now exact and is no longer measured". The sphere is a single crystal; X-rays give the edge of one cube; each cube holds the equivalent of 8 silicon atoms; the density gives the mass of one cube and so of one atom; mass of one mole of silicon ÷ mass of one atom = the number of atoms in one mole.
- §5.1.3 Moles to particles: "Particles = moles × 6.022 × 10²³. Moles = particles ÷ 6.022 × 10²³. Always name the particle." The Avogadro constant is a conversion factor: multiply by 6.022 × 10²³ particles/mol so that mol cancels; particles to moles divides, "the same as multiplying by 1 mol/6.022 × 10²³ particles". Copper and other metals: atoms; water, carbon dioxide and other molecular compounds: molecules; sodium chloride and other ionic compounds have no molecules: formula units, one formula unit of NaCl being one Na⁺ ion and one Cl⁻ ion (Unit 4).
- §5.1.3 Particles to moles: "To change particles into moles, divide by 6.022 × 10²³. A visible sample is a small number of moles but an enormous number of particles." 9.03 × 10²³ iron atoms ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.50 mol; scientific notation (a number from 1 to less than 10 times a power of ten; Lesson 5.3 shows the calculator).
- §5.1.4 Atoms in a molecule: "Moles of an element's atoms = moles of molecules (or formula units) × the subscript of that element." In H₂O the subscript 2 means two hydrogen atoms, no subscript after O means one; one mole of water molecules holds two moles of hydrogen atoms and one mole of oxygen atoms; to count the atoms, multiply those moles by 6.022 × 10²³; 1 mol of NaCl holds 1 mol of Na⁺ ions and 1 mol of Cl⁻ ions.
- §5.1.2 The mole in earlier lessons: "A value 'per mole' describes 6.022 × 10²³ particles. Dividing by that number gives the value for one particle." Lesson 3.2's first ionization energy of sodium, 496 kJ/mol, is the energy to remove the most loosely held electron from each atom in a mole of gaseous sodium atoms; for one atom 8.24 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, "very small", so chemists give such energies per mole; Lesson 3.3 gave heat "per mole of metal".
- Review table: Number of coins ← total mass: divide by the mass of one coin, or by the average mass of a mixture · Particles ← moles: multiply by 6.022 × 10²³ particles/mol · Moles ← particles: divide by 6.022 × 10²³ particles/mol · Moles of an element's atoms ← moles of a compound: multiply by the element's subscript. Misconceptions and corrections (blueprint table, plus the reading's checks): a mole is a mass or a volume → a mole is a count, like a dozen or a ream; one mole of copper (63.546 g) and one mole of aluminum (26.982 g) hold the same number of atoms. A jar of mixed cents can be counted with the average of the two coin masses → halving 2.500 g plus 3.11 g works only with equal numbers of each kind; the jar needs the average mass of its own mix. To get particles, divide by Avogadro's number → more moles means more particles: 2.00 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.20 × 10²⁴ atoms; dividing goes from particles to moles. A count smaller than 6.022 × 10²³ cannot be written in moles → moles can be fractions: 3.011 × 10²³ molecules is 0.5000 mol. Ionic compounds are made of molecules, or the particle does not matter → sodium chloride is counted in formula units; "2.50 mol of sodium chloride contains 1.51 × 10²⁴ molecules" has the right number and the wrong particle. One mole of water contains one mole of hydrogen atoms → the subscript multiplies: 1 mol of H₂O holds 2 mol of H atoms and 1 mol of O atoms; 0.50 mol of methane holds 2.0 mol of H atoms. The silicon spheres were weighed to find the mass of one atom directly → the project counted atoms: the sphere's volume divided by the volume each atom occupies, from the spacing measured by X-rays. From the reading's items: "multiply the total mass by the mass of one nickel" (gives g², not a count); "Avogadro measured the number in 1811" (he never did; Perrin measured it around 1909 and named it); "Avogadro's number is measured and the constant is defined" (same value, the constant carries mol⁻¹); "the number is the atoms in 12 g of carbon-12, which never changes" (the older definition); "methane and ammonia, one mole each, hold the same number of atoms" (same molecules, 5 and 4 atoms each); "one mole is 22.4 L" (a volume belongs to Unit 7).
