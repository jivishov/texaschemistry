# Lesson 4.5: Bonds, forces and properties  (lesson content sheet, written by the orchestrator in step 00 from the reading and the blueprint; every line verified against `unit4/reading-4-5.html` on 2026-11-03)

Sources: `unit4/reading-4-5.html` (opener, §4.5.1 to §4.5.5 in ten sections, two "Watch / Finish it / On your own" triplets, two Honors boxes, Review table, glossary with Spanish terms, item bank `u45-r1` to `u45-r15` and `u45-s1` to `u45-s9` in the page script), `unit4/blueprint.html` (section 4.5, data and accuracy notes, matrix rows 4.5.1 to 4.5.5). TEKS C.7(D): analyze the properties of ionic, covalent, and metallic substances in terms of intramolecular and intermolecular forces.

Driving question: How do the forces within and between particles explain the melting point, conductivity and hardness of a substance?

Objectives (verbatim from the blueprint):
- 4.5.1 Distinguish the bonds within a substance from the attractions between molecules, and use the halogen boiling points as evidence for dispersion forces.
- 4.5.2 Identify dipole-dipole forces and hydrogen bonding, and compare the boiling points of molecular substances by their strongest attraction.
- 4.5.3 Explain melting and boiling as molecules separating with their covalent bonds intact, and explain why ice is less dense than liquid water.
- 4.5.4 Explain the electrical conductivity of solids, liquids and solutions from charged particles that can move, and describe which substances dissolve in water.
- 4.5.5 Compare the hardness, brittleness and malleability of ionic, metallic, molecular and covalent network solids.

Reading sections and their key ideas (the reading's "Key idea" boxes, verbatim; the deck's rule cards restate them per RULES section 7):
- Opener: methane melts at −182.5 °C and boils at −161.5 °C; table salt stays solid until 801 °C; quartz, the main mineral in sand, melts near 1700 °C. "The difference lies in what must come apart when each solid melts."
- §4.5.1 Bonds and attractions: "Bonds hold atoms or ions together within a substance. Much weaker attractions act between separate molecules." The TEKS call bonds "intramolecular forces" even though ionic and metallic crystals contain no molecules; the reading says "bonds within a substance". Model: Cl₂ molecules (covalent bond within, dashed attractions between), NaCl (ionic bonds throughout, space filling to scale), one layer of copper cations among delocalized electrons.
- §4.5.1 Dispersion forces: "Dispersion forces act between all molecules. Molecules with more electrons have stronger dispersion forces and higher boiling points." Electrons crowd to one end for an instant, δ− and δ+ appear briefly, a neighbor is attracted; Fritz London, 1930, "London dispersion forces".
- §4.5.2 Dipole-dipole forces: "Polar molecules attract one another through dipole-dipole forces, in addition to their dispersion forces." Polar molecules turn so the δ+ end of one faces the δ− end of the next. F₂ and HCl both have 18 electrons; F₂ boils at −188.1 °C, HCl at −85.1 °C, "about 100 °C higher".
- §4.5.2 Hydrogen bonding: "Hydrogen bonding needs an H atom bonded to N, O or F, and a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of another molecule." An especially strong dipole-dipole attraction; not a covalent bond; "only about 5 to 10% as strong as a covalent bond"; methane forms none (H on C); each water molecule can form up to four.
- §4.5.3 Molecules stay whole: "Melting and boiling separate molecules. They do not break the covalent bonds within them." Steam is still H₂O, each with its two O–H bonds. Boiling 18 g of water at 100 °C takes 40.7 kJ; breaking every O–H bond in it would take about 928 kJ, "more than 20 times as much".
- §4.5.3 Why ice floats: "Hydrogen bonds hold ice in open rings, so ice is less dense than liquid water and floats." Each molecule bonds to four neighbors in open six-sided rings; melting collapses the rings. This expansion "can split rocks and burst water pipes in a hard freeze."
- §4.5.4 Electrical conductivity: "A substance conducts when it has charged particles that can move: ions in a liquid or solution, or delocalized electrons in a metal." Solid NaCl has ions locked in place (dark); molten NaCl and salt water light; sugar water dark; copper lights; distilled water dark (very few ions). "A substance that forms ions in water, like NaCl, is an electrolyte."
- §4.5.4 Dissolving in water: "Some ionic and some molecular substances dissolve in water, and some do not. The type of bond alone does not predict it." NaCl yes, sucrose yes, CaCO₃ barely (limestone, marble, seashells), I₂ slightly (pale yellow-brown), quartz no, copper no. The chapter "describes which substances dissolve without explaining why."
- §4.5.5 Malleable or brittle: "A metal bends because its electrons move with the shifting cations. An ionic crystal splits because shifted layers put like charges side by side." Molecular solids (iodine, dry ice) are usually soft.
- §4.5.5 Covalent network solids: covalent bonds join every atom to its neighbors, no separate molecules; melting means breaking covalent bonds, so very high melting points and most are very hard; diamond is the hardest natural substance; quartz SiO₂ melts near 1700 °C; dry ice (CO₂) sublimes at −78.5 °C. Core aside "An exception: graphite": conducts along its layers (one delocalized electron per carbon), layers held by dispersion forces only, soft. (This section has no Key idea box; the Review line is "Metals are malleable, ionic crystals are brittle, and most covalent network solids are very hard.")
- Review table (one picture): Molecular I₂ · melting overcomes attractions between molecules, low melting point · conducts: no · under a hammer: soft. Ionic NaCl · ionic bonds, high melting point · when molten or dissolved · hard, brittle. Metallic Cu · metallic bonds, melting point usually high · yes, as a solid and a liquid · malleable. Covalent network SiO₂ · covalent bonds, very high melting point · no, except graphite · very hard, except graphite.

Data the deck may use (values with sources as the reading gives them):
- Melting points at 1 atm: CH₄ −182.5 °C; ice 0.0 °C; NaCl 801 °C; Cu 1085 °C; quartz "near 1700 °C" (sources 1710 to 1722 °C). Dry ice sublimes at −78.5 °C (no liquid at 1 atm; never give CO₂ a boiling point).
- Boiling points at 1 atm and electrons per molecule: F₂ 18 e⁻, −188.1 °C; Cl₂ 34 e⁻, −34.0 °C; Br₂ 70 e⁻, 58.8 °C; I₂ 106 e⁻, 184.4 °C; CH₄ 10 e⁻, −161.5 °C; NH₃ 10 e⁻, −33.3 °C; H₂O 10 e⁻, 100.0 °C; HCl 18 e⁻, −85.1 °C. Halogens at 25 °C: F₂ gas, Cl₂ gas, Br₂ liquid, I₂ solid.
- Electronegativity (Pauling, shared data): H 2.20, Br 2.96 (HBr 0.76); other values as in `02b-shared.html`.
- Hydrogen bond strength: about 5 to 10% of a covalent bond. Water: up to four hydrogen bonds per molecule; in ice four neighbors at O···O 276 pm (hexagonal ice; data notes: ideal hexagonal oxygen framework, hydrogen directions simplified).
- Energy for 18 g of water: boiling at 100 °C 40.7 kJ; breaking all O–H bonds about 928 kJ (OpenStax). Shown as the reading's comparison only; no bond-energy calculation is asked (out of core).
- Densities at 0 °C (CRC): ice 0.9167 g/cm³, liquid water 0.99984 g/cm³; 8.3% of a floating block above the surface; 100.0 cm³ of water becomes 109.1 cm³ of ice, "about 9% more"; the same 99.98 g.
- Conductivity table (six samples): solid NaCl, Na⁺ and Cl⁻, no, dark · molten NaCl, yes, lights · salt water, yes, lights · sugar water, none, dark · copper, delocalized electrons, yes, lights · distilled water, very few ions, dark.
- Dissolving table: NaCl ionic yes · sucrose molecular yes · CaCO₃ ionic barely · I₂ molecular slightly · quartz SiO₂ covalent network no · copper metallic no. NaCl about 360 g in 1 L (case file File 6; use only if needed).
- Radii for drawings: Na⁺ 102 pm, Cl⁻ 181 pm (Shannon), NaCl nearest spacing 282 pm; Cu 128 pm metallic radius (reading model note); covalent radii (Cordero 2008) for ball-and-stick, van der Waals (Alvarez 2013) for space filling; radii never set bond lengths. Geometries from NIST CCCBDB (H₂O 104.5°, NH₃ 106.7°, CH₄ 109.5°, CO₂ 180°).
- Graphite conducts along its layers; calcium carbonate is sparingly soluble (data notes).

Misconceptions and corrections (blueprint): boiling breaks water molecules → track intact O–H bonds through every phase; charge alone guarantees conduction → require mobile charge carriers; all covalent solids melt low → compare molecular water with network quartz and diamond. From the reading's checks: "the I–I bond is stronger, so iodine boils higher" (boiling does not break the bond); "HCl forms hydrogen bonds" (H on Cl, not N, O or F); "melting turns the atoms into ions" (solid NaCl is already ions, locked in place); "methane melts low because its C–H bonds are weak" (melting leaves C–H intact); "ionic bonds are weak, so salt splits" (salt is hard; like charges repel when layers shift); "every ionic compound dissolves" (CaCO₃ barely does).
