B2. **Checks**: the 4.2 `check()` or `checkrow()` helper with `right`, `ans` (the reason line) and `fb` (one feedback line per option, shown at 34 px or more; each sentence 14 words or fewer). Options in one row when every option is two words or fewer, else stacked; four options may use 2 × 2; never 2 + 1. The texts:
   - Slide 6 (4.5.1): "In liquid chlorine, what holds neighboring Cl₂ molecules near each other?" A "weak attractions between the molecules" · B "covalent bonds between the molecules" · C "ionic bonds". Right A. Reason: "A. The Cl–Cl bond is inside each molecule. Between molecules there are only weak attractions." Feedback A "Between molecules there are only weak attractions." B "A covalent bond joins the two atoms inside one molecule, not two molecules." C "Chlorine is neutral molecules, not ions. It has no ionic bonds."
   - Slide 7 (4.5.1): "Chlorine boils at −34.0 °C. Bromine boils at 58.8 °C. Why does bromine boil higher?" A "Br₂ has more electrons, so stronger dispersion forces." · B "Br₂ is polar." · C "Boiling must break the Br–Br bond." Right A (stacked). Reason: "A. Br₂ has 70 electrons and Cl₂ has 34. Both are nonpolar." Feedback A "More electrons give stronger dispersion forces." B "Both atoms in Br₂ are the same, so it is nonpolar." C "Boiling separates whole Br₂ molecules. The Br–Br bond stays."
   - Slide 8 (4.5.2): "Which substance has dipole-dipole forces between its molecules?" A "hydrogen chloride, HCl" · B "carbon dioxide, CO₂" · C "methane, CH₄". Right A. Reason: "A. HCl is polar, so dipole-dipole forces add to its dispersion forces." Feedback A "HCl is polar. The δ+ end of one pulls the δ− end of the next." B "CO₂ is linear. Its two polar bonds cancel, so it is nonpolar." C "Methane is nonpolar. It has dispersion forces only."
   - Slide 9 (4.5.2): "Which substance has hydrogen bonding between its molecules?" A "water, H₂O" · B "methane, CH₄" · C "hydrogen chloride, HCl". Right A. Reason: "A. Each H is bonded to O, and O has two lone pairs." Feedback A "Each H is on O, and O has lone pairs for the next molecule's H." B "Methane's H atoms are bonded to carbon, not to N, O or F." C "HCl is polar, but its H is on Cl. It forms no hydrogen bonds."
   - Slide 11 (4.5.3): "What happens to the water molecules when water boils?" A "The attractions let go, and the molecules move far apart." · B "Their O–H bonds break." · C "They split into hydrogen and oxygen." Right A (stacked). Reason: "A. The molecules separate. Each keeps its two O–H bonds." Feedback A "Only the attractions between molecules let go." B "Boiling does not break covalent bonds. Steam is still H₂O." C "Boiling is a change of state, not a reaction. The molecules stay whole."
   - Slide 12 (4.5.3): "100.0 cm³ of water freezes at 0 °C. About what volume of ice forms?" A "about 109 cm³" · B "100.0 cm³" · C "about 92 cm³". Right A. Reason: "A. Ice is less dense. The same mass takes about 9% more space." Feedback A "Ice is less dense, so the same mass takes more space." B "The mass stays the same, but ice is less dense, so the volume grows." C "Most solids shrink when they freeze. Ice expands instead."
   - Slide 15 (4.5.4): "Why does molten NaCl conduct, while solid NaCl does not?" A "In the liquid, the ions are free to move." · B "Melting turns the atoms into ions." · C "Melting frees electrons from sodium." Right A (stacked). Reason: "A. The ions were there all along. Melting lets them move." Feedback A "Moving ions carry the current." B "Solid NaCl is already Na⁺ and Cl⁻. In the solid they are locked in place." C "NaCl has no electron sea. Moving ions carry its current."
   - Slide 17 (4.5.4): "Which statement fits the data?" A "Some ionic compounds, such as CaCO₃, barely dissolve." · B "Every ionic compound dissolves in water." · C "No molecular substance dissolves in water." Right A (stacked). Reason: "A. Being ionic does not mean it dissolves. Check the data." Feedback A "The type of bond alone does not predict it." B "Calcium carbonate is ionic, yet it barely dissolves." C "Sugar is molecular, and it dissolves easily."
   - Slide 18 (4.5.5): "Why does a salt crystal split when it is struck hard?" A "Shifted layers put like charges together, and they repel." · B "Ionic bonds are weak." · C "The electrons flow out of the crystal." Right A (stacked). Reason: "A. The repulsion pushes the layers apart along a flat face." Feedback A "Like charges side by side repel, and the layers push apart." B "Ionic bonds are strong, so salt is hard. Like charges repel when layers shift." C "Salt has no electron sea. Its ions stay charged, and like charges repel."
   - Slide 19 (4.5.5): "Which substance is a covalent network solid?" A "quartz, SiO₂" · B "dry ice, CO₂" · C "iodine, I₂". Right A. Reason: "A. Covalent bonds join every atom in quartz. It melts only near 1700 °C." Feedback A "Covalent bonds join every atom in quartz. No molecules." B "Dry ice is separate CO₂ molecules held by dispersion forces." C "Solid iodine is separate I₂ molecules held by dispersion forces."
