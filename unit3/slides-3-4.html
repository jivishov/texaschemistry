Driving question · Textbook 3.4 opener

    Astatine

    Astatine, group 17

    Every isotope is radioactive. The most stable one has a half-life of about 8.1 hours.

    No one has ever collected a piece large enough to see.

    What chemists predict

    7 valence electrons, At− ions, a solid at room temperature, less reactive than iodine.

    How can they predict anything about an element no one has seen?

        Families · Textbook §3.4.1 · SEP 2(B)

          Families share valence electrons

          Same family, same valence electrons

          Group 1: 1. Group 2: 2. Group 17: 7. Group 18: 8 (helium 2).

          Predict in three steps

          1. Family: valence electrons predict the ion and the chemistry.

          2. Trend: larger or smaller than the element above?

          3. Neighbors: their values give a likely range.

          1. Families · 2 min. Click one element in each family; each family reacts in its own way. Transition metals: the group number does not give the valence electrons (Unit 2).
          2. Three steps · 1 min. Students copy the three steps; every prediction today uses them.
          3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: neighbors in a period are more alike than members of a group.

            Alkali metals · Textbook §3.4.1 · SEP 2(C)

              Predict rubidium

              Predict

              Choose in every row, then check. The family gives the chemistry; the trend gives the direction.

              Average the neighbors

              Melting point: (63.4 + 28.4) ÷ 2 = 45.9 °C.

              Reference value: 39.3 °C.

              A range of values

              The real value lies between the neighbors, as the trend predicts, but it is not the average.

              1. Predict · 3 min. Pairs agree on every row before anyone clicks Check.
              2. Average · 1 min. Density: (0.89 + 1.93) ÷ 2 = 1.41 g/cm³; reference 1.53 g/cm³.
              3. A range · 1 min. SEP 2(C): a prediction is checked against data, and it can be close without being exact.

              Alkaline earth metals · Textbook §3.4.1 · SEP 2(C)

                The alkaline earth metals

                Group 2

                2 valence electrons, 2+ ions such as Mg2+. Harder, denser and higher melting than the alkali metal beside each one.

                An uneven trend

                Ionization energy falls smoothly down group 2. Melting point and density do not.

                1. Group 2 · 1 min. Unit 2 taught Mg2+ and Ca2+. Lesson 3.3 showed why a group 2 metal reacts more slowly than its group 1 neighbor.
                2. Estimate · 2 min. Students use the slider to estimate strontium's first ionization energy from calcium (590) and barium (503), then reveal. Ionization energy changes smoothly down group 2, so the estimate is close: 546.5 against 549 kJ/mol.
                3. Uneven trends · 1 min. Melting point and density do not change smoothly in group 2. Predictions from neighbors are less reliable there.
                4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: every property changes smoothly down a group.

                Alkaline earth metals · Textbook §3.4.1 · SEP 2(C)

                  Predict strontium

                  Predict

                  Strontium sits between calcium and barium in group 2, and beside rubidium in period 5.

                  Rubidium or strontium?

                  Rubidium loses 1 electron; strontium must lose 2. Like sodium and magnesium in Lesson 3.3, the group 1 metal is more reactive.

                  1. Predict · 3 min. Students predict alone, then compare with a partner before checking.
                  2. Compare · 1 min. Strontium compounds give fireworks their red color (Lesson 3.2).

                  Halogens · Textbook §3.4.2 · SEP 2(B)

                    States of the halogens

                    Group 17

                    7 valence electrons; 1− ions such as Cl−. Molecules: F2, Cl2, Br2, I2.

                    At 25 °C

                    Fluorine, chlorine: gases. Bromine: red-brown liquid. Iodine: gray-black solid.

                    Why

                    Larger molecules attract each other more strongly, so melting and boiling points rise down the group.

                    1. Family · 1 min. Same colors as Unit 2 and Lesson 3.3.
                    2. States · 2 min. Drag the temperature line. Return to prediction 1: bromine is a liquid from −7.2 °C to 58.8 °C.
                    3. Why · 1 min. Keep this to one sentence. Do not teach bond types here.
                    4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: below the boiling point, a substance is always a liquid.

                    Halogens · Textbook §3.4.2 · SEP 2(C)

                      Predicting astatine

                      Never seen

                      Its melting point, boiling point, color and density have never been measured.

                      Measured on a few atoms

                      In 2013, lasers at CERN measured its first ionization energy: 899 kJ/mol, lower than iodine's 1008, as the trend predicts.

                      1. Predict · 3 min. The card checks against the trend, because there are no data to check against.
                      2. Measured · 1 min. Some websites list a melting point for astatine. It is a predicted value, not a measurement.
                      3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: a value printed in a table must have been measured.

                      Noble gases · Textbook §3.4.3 · SEP 2(C)

                        The noble gases

                        A full outer level

                        8 valence electrons (2 for helium) and the highest ionization energy in each period, so they rarely react.

                        Estimate xenon

                        Boiling points rise down the group. Estimate xenon's from krypton (−153.2 °C) and radon (−61.7 °C).

                        Uses

                        Argon protects hot metal in welding. Helium cannot burn and fills balloons.

                        1. Full level · 1 min. Connect to Lesson 3.2: each noble gas ends a period with the highest ionization energy. They are colorless gases made of single atoms.
                        2. Estimate · 2 min. The average of krypton and radon, −107.5 °C, is close to xenon's −108.1 °C.
                        3. Uses · 30 s. Neon signs glow red-orange (Lesson 3.2).
                        4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: noble gases are unreactive because they are gases.

                        Noble gases · Textbook §3.4.3

                          Xenon compounds

                          Use the family and the group 18 trends.

                          Bartlett, 1962

                          Removing an electron from O2: 1165 kJ/mol. From xenon: 1170 kJ/mol. PtF6 did it to O2, so Neil Bartlett predicted it would react with xenon. It did: an orange-yellow solid, the first noble gas compound.

                          1. 1962 · 2 min. Return to prediction 2. Xenon forms compounds with fluorine and oxygen; helium, neon and argon form no compounds under ordinary conditions.
                          2. Predict · 2 min. SEP 2(C): Bartlett used data to make a prediction, then tested it.
                          3. Check · 1 min. Misconception: a family prediction is a law that cannot fail.

                            Transition metals · Textbook §3.4.4 · SEP 2(B)

                              Transition metals across a row

                              Period 4 data

                              Scandium to copper: 633 to 745 kJ/mol, a rise of 112. Gallium to krypton: 579 to 1351, a rise of 772.

                              An inner level fills

                              Across the transition metals, each added electron goes into an inner level. The outer level keeps 1 or 2 electrons, so the outer electrons feel a similar pull, and neighbors are alike.

                              Zinc

                              Zinc forms only Zn2+; some chemists do not count it as a transition metal. This course includes groups 3 to 12.

                              1. Data · 2 min. Click the points from potassium to krypton.
                              2. Inner level · 2 min. Keep this at the level of "inner level" and "outer level". Orbital names are not needed here.

                                  Cesium is below rubidium in group 1. Predict cesium's ion, how fast it reacts with water compared with rubidium, and whether it melts at a higher or lower temperature. Explain each prediction.

                                  valence electronionenergy levelshieldingmelting pointtrend

                                  Success criteria

                                  Sample answer

                                  Cesium has 1 valence electron, so it forms Cs+, like the other alkali metals. Its outer electron is in level 6, farther from the nucleus and more shielded than rubidium's, so it is lost more easily and cesium reacts faster with water. Melting points in group 1 fall down the group, so cesium melts at a lower temperature than rubidium: 28.4 °C against 39.3 °C.

                                  4. Self-check · 30 s.
                                  5. Compare · 1 min. Misconception to watch: "heavier, so it melts at a higher temperature."

                                  Checkpoint · Question 2 · Objective 3.4.2

                                    Chlorine is a gas at room temperature, but iodine is a solid. Use the group 17 trend to explain why, then predict astatine's state. How sure can we be?

                                    Think silently. Plan what you will say.

                                    0:30

                                    Explain your idea to your partner, then switch.

                                    1:00

                                    Write your answer in your notebook. Use these sentence starters:

                                    1. Down group 17, the boiling points…
                                    2. This is because larger molecules…
                                    3. So astatine should be…, but…
                                    Word bank
                                    boiling pointmelting pointdiatomic moleculepredictionradioactiveevidence

                                    Success criteria

                                    Sample answer

                                    Down group 17, the boiling points rise. Chlorine boils at −34.0 °C, so it is a gas at 25 °C; iodine melts at 113.7 °C, so it is a solid. This is because larger molecules attract each other more strongly. Astatine should therefore be a solid, but no sample large enough to see has ever been made, so this has never been tested.

                                    1. Think · 30 s.
                                    2. Talk · 1 min.
                                    3. Write · 2 min. Watch for "iodine is a solid because it is more reactive." Reactivity falls down the group, and state depends on boiling point.
                                    4. Self-check · 30 s.
                                    5. Compare · 1 min.

                                    Checkpoint · Question 3 · Objective 3.4.4

                                      Nickel and sodium are both metals. Give three ways nickel differs from sodium, and explain why the transition metals across a row are so alike.

                                      Think silently. Plan what you will say.

                                      0:30

                                      Explain your idea to your partner, then switch.

                                      1:00

                                      Write your answer in your notebook. Use these sentence starters:

                                      1. Nickel melts at…, but sodium…
                                      2. In cold water, …
                                      3. Sodium forms only…, but nickel…
                                      4. Across the transition metals, the added electrons…
                                      Word bank
                                      transition metalmelting pointdensityioninner levelouter level

                                      Success criteria

                                      Sample answer

                                      Nickel melts at 1454.9 °C, but sodium melts at 97.8 °C, and nickel is about nine times as dense. In cold water, sodium reacts quickly, while nickel does not react. Sodium forms only Na+, but nickel usually forms Ni2+ and can also have a charge of 3+. Across the transition metals, the added electrons go into an inner level, so the outer electrons feel a similar pull and neighbors in a row are alike.

                                      1. Think · 30 s.
                                      2. Talk · 1 min.
                                      3. Write · 2 min. Watch for "nickel is in group 10, so it has 10 valence electrons." Unit 2: the group number does not give a transition metal's valence electrons.
                                      4. Self-check · 30 s.
                                      5. Compare · 1 min.

                                        Wrap-up · Exit ticket · SEP 3(A)

                                          Answer the driving question

                                          How can the periodic table predict the properties of an element you have never seen? Write your answer in your notebook.

                                          Word bank
                                          familyvalence electrontrendpredictionevidenceneighborrange

                                          Claim

                                          One sentence that answers the question.

                                          1. An element's family and the trends in its group…

                                          Sample claim

                                          An element's family and the trends in its group predict its chemistry and a likely range for its properties.

                                          Evidence

                                          A prediction that was checked, with data.

                                          1. For rubidium, the trend predicted…
                                          2. The data show…

                                          Sample evidence

                                          For rubidium, the trend predicted a melting point between potassium's 63.4 °C and cesium's 28.4 °C. The reference value is 39.3 °C, inside that range.

                                          Reasoning

                                          Why do families make good predictions, and when do they fail?

                                          1. Elements in a family share…
                                          2. But a prediction…

                                          Sample reasoning

                                          Elements in a family share their number of valence electrons, so they react in similar ways, and their properties change steadily down the group. But a prediction must be tested, as xenon showed in 1962, and some, like astatine's state, have never been tested.

                                          Self-check: mark each part your answer meets, then compare with the sample.

                                          1. Claim · 2 min. One sentence. Push for "family" and "trend".
                                          2. Evidence · 3 min. Any checked prediction from today works: rubidium, strontium, xenon or nickel.
                                          3. Reasoning · 3 min. Must link valence electrons to similar chemistry and name a limit.
                                          4. Self-check · 2 min. Students self-check, then you show the sample. Score 0 to 2 per part.
