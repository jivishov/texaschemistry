3.1

Building the table from evidence

In 1871 the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev described a metal that no one had ever seen. He predicted its atomic mass, its density and the formula of its oxide. In 1875 a French chemist found the metal in a zinc ore and named it gallium, and its properties matched the prediction. The prediction came from a table that lined up similar elements and left an empty space where a metal was missing. This chapter follows the evidence that built that table and the later evidence that changed its order.In 1871 Dmitri Mendeleev described a metal nobody had seen. §3.1.1

Triads: families of three

By the early 1800s chemists had identified dozens of elements and measured the atomic masses of many of them. In 1817 Johann Döbereiner, a German chemist, noticed that the mass of strontium lay about halfway between the masses of calcium and barium, two elements with similar properties. By 1829 he had found more sets of three like this, which he called .By the early 1800s chemists knew dozens of elements. They had measured the masses of many. In 1817 Johann Döbereiner looked at calcium, strontium and barium. These three elements behave alike. Strontium's mass was about halfway between the other two. By 1829 he had found more sets of three. He called them triads.

§3.1.1

Octaves: properties repeat

In 1865 John Newlands, an English chemist, listed the known elements in order of atomic mass and numbered them. He saw that an element was often similar to the element seven places after it. Counting the first element, the similar one was the eighth, like the eighth note of a musical scale, so Newlands called the pattern the .In 1865 John Newlands listed the elements in order of mass. He saw a pattern. An element was often like the element seven places later. Counting the first one, that is the eighth. A musical scale also repeats at the eighth note. So Newlands called it the law of octaves.

The pattern works for the lighter elements. Lithium, sodium and potassium fall in one column, and so do fluorine and chlorine. After calcium it fails. Newlands fitted every known element into his grid with no empty spaces, so some unlike elements had to share a column, and in a few places two elements shared one position.The pattern works for light elements. Lithium, sodium and potassium line up. Fluorine and chlorine line up. After calcium, the pattern fails. Newlands left no empty spaces in his grid. Some unlike elements ended up in the same column.

When Newlands presented his table to the Chemical Society in London in 1866, one member asked, as a joke, whether he had tried listing the elements in alphabetical order. The Society did not publish his paper. In 1887 the Royal Society awarded Newlands its Davy Medal for discovering the periodic law.In 1866 Newlands showed his table to chemists in London. One of them joked that alphabetical order might work as well. They did not publish his paper. In 1887 he won a medal for this discovery.

Key idea

Newlands showed that properties repeat when elements are listed by atomic mass. His octaves failed for heavier elements, partly because he left no gaps for elements not yet discovered.

§3.1.2

Mendeleev's periodic table

In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev, a chemistry professor in St. Petersburg, was writing a textbook and looking for a way to organize the 63 elements then known. He listed them in order of atomic mass, as Newlands had, but he also compared their chemical properties, especially the formulas of the compounds each element forms with oxygen and with hydrogen. Elements whose compounds had similar formulas went in the same column.In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev was writing a chemistry textbook. He wanted to organize the 63 known elements. He listed them by atomic mass. He also compared their properties. He looked closely at their compounds with oxygen and hydrogen. Elements with similar compounds went in the same column.

When mass order and properties disagreed, Mendeleev followed the properties. Measured masses put tellurium after iodine, but iodine is clearly like chlorine and bromine, so he placed tellurium first. He suggested that tellurium's mass had been measured wrongly, and his 1871 table lists it as 125, below iodine's 127. Later measurements confirmed that tellurium is the heavier element (§3.1.3).Sometimes mass order and properties disagreed. Then Mendeleev followed the properties. Tellurium is heavier than iodine. But iodine behaves like chlorine and bromine. So he put tellurium first. He thought tellurium's mass was measured wrong. Later measurements showed the mass was right.

Most important, he left gaps. Where no known element had the right properties, he left a space and stated that an undiscovered element belonged there. In 1871 he described three of these missing elements in detail. He named them with the Sanskrit word eka, meaning "one": eka-boron, eka-aluminum and eka-silicon, one place below boron, aluminum and silicon.Mendeleev also left gaps. A gap meant a missing element. He said these elements would be found. In 1871 he described three of them in detail. He named them eka-boron, eka-aluminum and eka-silicon. "Eka" means "one". Eka-silicon is one place below silicon.

Key idea

Mendeleev ordered the elements by atomic mass but placed them by their properties when the two disagreed. He left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties.

Lothar Meyer, a German chemist, built a similar table at about the same time. In 1870 he published a graph of atomic volume against atomic mass whose peaks repeat at regular intervals. Mendeleev usually receives more of the credit because he used his table to make predictions that could be tested.Lothar Meyer, a German chemist, made a similar table. In 1870 he showed a graph with repeating peaks. Mendeleev usually receives more credit. He used his table to make predictions that could be tested.

§3.1.2

Predictions put to the test

Mendeleev estimated each missing element's properties from its neighbors in the table: the elements above and below it in its group and those on either side in its row. Eka-aluminum sits between aluminum and indium, so its mass and density should lie between theirs. He predicted an atomic mass of about 68 and a density of about 5.9 g/cm³.Mendeleev used the neighbors of each gap. He looked at the elements above, below and beside it. Eka-aluminum is between aluminum and indium. Its mass should be between theirs. He predicted a mass of about 68. He predicted a density of about 5.9 g/cm³.

In 1875 Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran, a French chemist, found a new metal in a zinc ore by its spectrum and named it gallium. He first reported a density of 4.7 g/cm³. Mendeleev wrote that the value should be close to 6. When the purified metal was measured again, its density was 5.9 g/cm³.In 1875 a French chemist found a new metal. He named it gallium. He first measured its density as 4.7 g/cm³. Mendeleev said it should be close to 6. The metal was purified and measured again. The new value was 5.9 g/cm³.

Two more gaps were filled soon after. Lars Nilson, a Swedish chemist, found scandium in 1879, and another Swedish chemist, Per Cleve, showed that it matched eka-boron. Clemens Winkler, a German chemist, found germanium in 1886, and it matched eka-silicon. Each discovery was a test the table could have failed. Because the predictions held, most chemists accepted Mendeleev's arrangement.Scandium was found in 1879. It matched eka-boron. Germanium was found in 1886. It matched eka-silicon. Each discovery tested the table. The table passed each test. After that, most chemists accepted it.

§3.1.3

A new group: the noble gases

Mendeleev's table had no place for one whole family, because none of its members had been found. In 1894 Lord Rayleigh and William Ramsay, two British scientists, investigated a puzzle: nitrogen taken from air was slightly denser than nitrogen made from compounds. They traced the difference to an unknown gas mixed with the nitrogen in air. The gas reacted with nothing they tried, and they named it argon, from the Greek word for "lazy".Mendeleev's table had no place for one family. None of its elements had been found yet. In 1894 two British scientists studied nitrogen. Nitrogen from air was a bit denser than nitrogen from compounds. A new gas was mixed into the air sample. It did not react with anything they tried. They named it argon, from the Greek word for "lazy".

Helium had been seen in 1868 as a yellow line in the spectrum of sunlight. Ramsay found it on Earth in 1895, in a uranium mineral. In 1898 Ramsay and Morris Travers separated neon, krypton and xenon from liquefied air, and radon was identified as a radioactive gas between 1899 and 1900. Ramsay received the 1904 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.Helium was first seen in 1868. It showed up as a yellow line in sunlight. Ramsay found helium on Earth in 1895. In 1898 Ramsay and Morris Travers found neon, krypton and xenon in air. Radon was found between 1899 and 1900. Ramsay won the 1904 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.

The new gases matched no existing group. They formed no compounds that anyone could make, and their masses placed them between the halogens and the alkali metals, so they were added as a new column. The table took in a whole family without being rebuilt, which was more evidence that it described a real pattern. Argon, however, is heavier than potassium, so in this one place mass order put the new column in the wrong position.The new gases fit no group in the table. They formed no compounds. They were placed in a new column, between the halogens and the alkali metals. The table did not need to be rebuilt. That was more evidence that the table was right. But argon is heavier than potassium. Mass order put argon in the wrong place.

Key idea

The noble gases, found from 1894 to 1900, formed a new column. The table took them in, but argon's mass placed it after potassium.

§3.1.3

Mass order breaks down

Argon and potassium are not the only problem. As atomic masses were measured more precisely, three more neighboring pairs kept the heavier element first: cobalt and nickel, tellurium and iodine, and, among the heaviest elements, thorium and protactinium. In each pair, the order that fits the properties is the reverse of mass order.Argon and potassium are not the only problem pair. Three more pairs put the heavier element first. They are cobalt and nickel, tellurium and iodine, and thorium and protactinium. In each pair, properties say one order and mass says the other.

§3.1.3

Atomic number sets the order

In 1911 Ernest Rutherford showed that each atom has a small, positively charged . Antonius van den Broek, a Dutch amateur physicist, then proposed that an element's position number in the table equals the positive charge of its nucleus.In 1911 Ernest Rutherford found that atoms have a small, positive nucleus. Then a Dutch scientist, Antonius van den Broek, made a proposal. He said an element's place number equals the charge of its nucleus.

In 1913 and 1914 Henry Moseley, a young English physicist, tested this idea. He aimed fast electrons at targets made of different elements and measured the frequency of the each element gave off. The square root of the frequency rose by about the same step from one element to the next. Plotted against atomic mass, cobalt and nickel come out of order; plotted against position number, every point falls on one straight line.In 1913 and 1914 Henry Moseley tested this idea. He hit samples of many elements with fast electrons. Each element gave off X-rays with its own frequency. The square root of the frequency rose in steady steps from one element to the next. Against mass, cobalt and nickel are out of order. Against place number, every point falls on one straight line.

Moseley concluded that each element has a whole number, its , that rises by one from each element to the next. Atomic number is now known to be the number of protons in the nucleus, equal to the . Ordering by atomic number puts all four pairs in the order their properties require, with no exceptions. The is stated in these terms: when elements are arranged in order of atomic number, their properties repeat at regular intervals.Moseley showed that each element has its own whole number. It goes up by one from element to element. It is the atomic number. It equals the number of protons in the nucleus. Atomic number puts all four problem pairs in the right order. The periodic law says that properties repeat when elements are in order of atomic number.

Key idea

Moseley's X-ray data showed that atomic number, the charge of the nucleus, sets each element's place. The modern table is ordered by atomic number, not by mass.

Moseley's line also showed where elements were missing: a skipped step meant an atomic number with no known element. His 1914 paper pointed to missing elements at 43, 61 and 75, and element 72 was also unknown. All four were found or made later: hafnium (72) in 1923, rhenium (75) in 1925, technetium (43) in 1937, the first element made artificially, and promethium (61) in 1945.Moseley's line showed gaps. A gap meant a missing element. Elements 43, 61, 72 and 75 were missing. All four were found or made later. The last was promethium (61), in 1945.

Moseley was killed in battle at Gallipoli, Turkey, on 10 August 1915. He was 27.

Honors · Moseley's law

For the strongest X-ray line of each element, Moseley found that √f = a(Z − 1), where f is the frequency, Z is the atomic number and a is the same for every element. The 1 appears because one inner electron partly screens the nucleus, so the electron that produces the X-ray feels a charge of about Z − 1. Lesson 3.2 uses this idea of screening, called shielding, to explain the size of atoms.

§3.1.5

Why the inversions happen

Atomic number counts only protons. Average atomic mass also depends on neutrons and on how common each isotope is. Argon in air is almost all argon-40, with 18 protons and 22 neutrons. Potassium is mostly potassium-39, with 19 protons and 20 neutrons. A potassium atom usually has one more proton but two fewer neutrons, so potassium's average atomic mass is smaller.Atomic number counts only protons. Mass also depends on neutrons. Most argon atoms are argon-40. They have 18 protons and 22 neutrons. Most potassium atoms are potassium-39. They have 19 protons and 20 neutrons. Potassium has one more proton but two fewer neutrons. Its average mass is smaller.
