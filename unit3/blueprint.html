1. The package

Fifteen pages: four readings, four slide decks, four mission sets, one case file, this blueprint and the unit index. Each surface has one job, and none of them tests content that the reading does not teach.

ReadStudent reading in book or continuous mode, with a simpler-language toggle, stop-and-check items and interactives.
TeachTeacher deck. Each slide shows its reading section, and the notes give timings.
PracticeMissions. Real data and everyday uses of each idea, with feedback on every wrong choice.
ApplyCase file. One Texas decision that needs the whole unit.

Teacher flow, one lesson

  1. Open the deck. The driving question and three predictions come first.
  2. Teach through the deck. Students follow the matching reading section or read it as homework.
  3. Students answer the stop-and-check items and complete the missions.
  4. The CER exit ticket and the self-rating close the lesson. Students write in their notebooks.

Design rules

  • Evidence first, then the model. Students see data before the explanation.
  • One objective ID per item. Every wrong choice gets feedback that names the faulty idea.
  • Writing happens in notebooks. Pages offer choices, numbers, sliders and reveals, never text boxes.
  • Reactive metals and halogens are shown by video only. Drawings are labeled as illustrations.
  • "Including" items in a TEKS statement are taught and assessed. "Such as" items are examples.

4. Lesson 3.1 · Building the table from evidence

Driving question. A metal described before anyone found it: in 1871 Mendeleev predicted eka-aluminum, and gallium, found in 1875, matched it (predicted density about 5.9 g/cm³, measured 5.9 g/cm³). How did evidence turn a list of elements into a table that predicts?

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
Mendeleev's gaps meant his table was unfinished.The gaps were deliberate. He predicted the properties of the missing elements, and gallium matched them.

5. Lesson 3.2 · Atomic radius and ionization energy

Driving question. More protons, but a smaller atom? Fluorine has three times the protons of lithium but less than half its radius (57 pm against 128 pm), while potassium is larger still (203 pm). Why do size and the energy to remove an electron change in a regular pattern?

Objectives

IDStudents willTEKSSlides
3.2.1Read and graph atomic radius and first ionization energy data against atomic number to identify their repeating patterns.C.5(C)5 to 7
3.2.2Describe the trend in atomic radius across a period and down a group, and explain it using energy levels, nuclear charge and shielding by inner electrons.C.5(C)8 to 11
3.2.3Describe the trend in first ionization energy across a period and down a group, and explain it with the same model.C.5(C)12 to 16
3.2.4Use the trends to rank elements by atomic radius and first ionization energy and to predict missing values from their neighbors.C.5(C)17, 18

Evidence and model

Covalent radii (Cordero 2008) shrink across period 3 from sodium, 166 pm, to chlorine, 102 pm, and grow down group 1 from lithium, 128 pm, to potassium, 203 pm. First ionization energies (NIST) peak at the noble gases, with helium highest at 2372 kJ/mol, and fall down each group. One model explains both trends: across a period, more protons pull on the same energy level with about the same shielding; down a group, each new energy level is farther out and shielded by more inner electrons. The noble gases have no covalent radius in this data set, and the graphs show that as a gap, not a zero.

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
More protons and electrons make a bigger atom.Across period 3 the radius falls from 166 pm to 102 pm as protons are added to the same level.
An atom has a hard surface that can be measured.The covalent radius is half the distance between two bonded nuclei.
Outer electrons or neutrons do the shielding.Inner electrons lie between the nucleus and the outer electrons and cancel part of the pull. Neutrons have no charge.
Down a group, extra protons push electrons outward.Protons attract electrons. The atom grows because its outer electrons are in a higher energy level.
A heavy metal such as iron is hardest to ionize.Helium needs the most energy. Ionization energy is highest at the upper right of the table.

6. Lesson 3.3 · Electronegativity and reactivity

Driving question. Same group, very different reactions: lithium fizzes gently in water and cesium explodes, while fluorine is the most reactive halogen. Why does reactivity increase down the metal groups but up the halogen group?

Objectives

IDStudents willTEKSSlides
3.3.1Describe electronegativity on the Pauling scale and its trends across a period and down a group.C.5(C)5 to 8
3.3.2Use observations and ionization energy to explain why the reactivity of metals increases down groups 1 and 2.C.5(C)9 to 11
3.3.3Use first and second ionization energies to explain why a group 2 metal is less reactive than the group 1 metal beside it.C.5(C)12, 13
3.3.4Use displacement reactions and atomic size to explain why the reactivity of halogens increases up group 17.C.5(C)14 to 17

Evidence and model

Pauling electronegativities have no unit and rise to fluorine, 3.98; cesium is lowest at 0.79, and the noble gases have no values. Reactions with water are reported observations (Chemguide), shown by video only: lithium fizzes gently, sodium melts into a ball and darts, potassium burns with a lilac flame, cesium explodes. These are observations, not measured rates, and the lesson never turns them into a score. Metals react by losing electrons, which gets easier down a group. Magnesium must lose two electrons (738 + 1451 = 2189 kJ/mol) while sodium loses one (496 kJ/mol), so magnesium reacts more slowly. Halogens react by gaining an electron; smaller atoms attract it more strongly, so a halogen displaces any halogen below it from its halide (chlorine displaces bromide and iodide).

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
Electronegativity and ionization energy are the same thing.Ionization energy is measured on separate gaseous atoms in kJ/mol. Electronegativity describes an atom in a bond and has no unit.
The noble gases have the highest electronegativity.This unit's data leave out all the noble gases: helium, neon and argon form no compounds under ordinary conditions, and krypton and xenon form only a few. Fluorine is highest.
More protons always pull harder on bonding electrons.Iodine (2.66) is lower than chlorine (3.16). Extra protons are shielded by extra inner electrons.
Heavier metals react faster because they are heavy.Mass does not set the rate. The outer electron is easier to remove down the group.
Magnesium reacts faster than sodium because it has more protons.Losing two electrons costs about 4.4 times as much energy as losing one, so magnesium reacts more slowly.
Halogens grow more reactive down the group, like the metals.The trend reverses: displacement shows fluorine and chlorine are the most reactive.

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
Members of a family share a mass or a number of energy levels.Lithium is 6.94 u and cesium 132.91 u. What they share is the number of valence electrons.
Every metal forms a 1+ ion.Group 1 metals form 1+ ions and group 2 metals 2+ ions. Metals never form negative ions.
A heavier family member always melts at a higher temperature.Alkali metal melting points fall down the group: rubidium melts below potassium.
A trend gives an exact value.Neighbors give a likely range. The average of potassium and cesium is 45.9 °C; rubidium's value is 39.3 °C.
All halogens are gases.Bromine is a liquid and iodine a solid at room temperature. Melting and boiling points rise down the group.
Noble gases never react.Xenon forms compounds with fluorine and oxygen.

8. Case file: Can sodium stand in for lithium?

Students act as science advisers to a fictional Texas electric cooperative that is planning a battery storage site. The board asks whether its pilot test should include sodium-ion batteries next to the lithium-ion ones its engineers know.

Briefing

The memo explains the task in plain words and lists the six files with their TEKS. A dated fact box gives the real context: on May 8, 2023 the Texas Governor's office celebrated a $375 million lithium refinery in Robstown; on November 6, 2024 Pacific Northwest National Laboratory described sodium as abundant and sodium-ion batteries as storing less energy for their weight, with shorter lifespans and high thermal stability; on January 28, 2026 Tesla reported pilot production at that refinery; on February 20, 2026 the EIA reported that 12.9 GW of the 24 GW of utility-scale battery storage planned for 2026 (53%) is in Texas. The client, its board, its supplier and its site are fictional, and the page says so. How a battery works is not assessed.

Each file has three steps: a calculation or count, the Unit 3 idea it uses, then a decision about a claim.

File 1 · Three metals that act alike · Jena, 1829 · C.5(A)

Döbereiner's triad of lithium, sodium and potassium. Task: average the masses of lithium and potassium ((6.94 + 39.098) ÷ 2 = 23.019 u) and compare with sodium's 22.990 u; decide what made the three a triad (similar reactions, then the mass pattern).

File 2 · One outer electron · C.5(B)

Configurations of lithium and sodium. Task: count sodium's valence electrons (1) and answer a board member who asks whether the shared group makes the two metals the same (alike chemistry, not identical properties).

File 3 · Weighing the atoms · C.5(C)

Reference atomic masses. Task: 22.990 ÷ 6.94 = 3.31; name where the extra mass comes from (the nucleus); decide what the comparison can and cannot show about a battery.

File 4 · Measuring atom size · C.5(C)

Covalent radii: lithium 128 pm, sodium 166 pm, potassium 203 pm, with 2, 3 and 4 occupied energy levels. Task: 166 − 128 = 38 pm; choose the explanation based on energy levels.

File 5 · Holding on to electrons · C.5(C)

First ionization energies 520, 496, 419 kJ/mol and electronegativities 0.98, 0.93, 0.82. Task: 520 − 496 = 24 kJ/mol; tell ionization energy apart from electronegativity; reject a salesperson's claim that these data set a battery's voltage.

File 6 · Into the water · C.5(B) C.5(C)

Observations from video demonstrations, as in Lesson 3.3. Task: link sodium's faster reaction to its lower ionization energy; answer a board member who says faster reaction with water proves sodium-ion batteries are more dangerous (a pure metal in water is not a battery safety test).

Recommendation · claim, evidence, reasoning · SEP 3(A), 3(C)

Students choose a claim (include sodium-ion batteries in the pilot, keep the pilot to lithium-ion for now, or replace everything with sodium now), select at least three files, and mark what only a test of real batteries can show. They then write the reasoning in their notebooks using sentence starters and a word bank. The check accepts the first two claims when the reasoning uses File 2, at least one of Files 3 to 6, and the three battery measurements (energy stored for its weight, number of charge cycles, behavior when overheated). The third claim gets feedback explaining why a full switch skips the test. After a successful check, a sample recommendation for the chosen claim appears for comparison.

Grading the notebook. A strong recommendation names the shared group and its single valence electron as the reason the chemistry is alike, uses at least one difference with a number (mass ratio, radius or ionization energy), and states what the periodic table cannot show. A claim that sodium is a drop-in replacement because it sits in the same group misses the point of the case.

Case-file boundary. Battery chemistry, voltage and cost are context only and are not assessed. The ionization energy file does not claim to predict voltage, and the water file does not claim to measure battery safety.

10. Using the results

What the pages record

  • Each mission set shows the cases solved and the first-try accuracy, so a first answer can be told apart from eventual completion.
  • Numeric answers accept stated rounding; the reference values stay fixed.
  • Progress is saved in the student's own browser only. Nothing is sent to a server, so the pages are not a gradebook.

What they do not show

  • Choice items check recall and short application. They are not, by themselves, evidence of extended reasoning.
  • Judge explanations from notebooks and discussion: the exit tickets, the checkpoints and the case file recommendation.
  • A completed self-rating is a reflection, not proof of mastery.

Each deck runs checkpoints as Think, Talk, Write, Self-check and Compare, with 30 second thinking and 60 second partner timers. The three predictions from slide 3 are revisited near the end, and the CER exit ticket returns to the driving question.

12. Data and accuracy notes

  • One shared data set. Average atomic masses from CIAAW (abridged); first ionization energies from the NIST Atomic Spectra Database, converted from eV with 96.485 and rounded to whole kJ/mol; covalent radii from Cordero and others (2008); electronegativities on the Pauling scale as revised by Allred (1961). Melting points, boiling points and densities come from PubChem. Missing values stay missing and are shown as gaps, never as zeros.
  • Classification. Six metalloids are used: boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium. Astatine and the elements from 104 on are marked "too little data".
  • Astatine. Its melting point, boiling point, color and density have never been measured, and the pages say so. Values sometimes listed for them are estimates and are not shown.
  • Observations and measurements. Reactions with water and halogen displacement are described as reported observations and shown by video. No page gives a reactivity score.
  • Illustrations. The atom diagram enlarges the nucleus and draws energy levels as rings; radius disks compare sizes and do not show atoms with hard edges. Graph lines guide the eye between real data points.
  • History. Triad arithmetic uses modern masses. Mendeleev's first table is from 1869 and his detailed predictions from 1871. The Moseley plot is drawn from modern values, not his raw 1914 measurements.
