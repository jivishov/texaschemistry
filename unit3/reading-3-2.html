3.2

Atomic radius and ionization energy

Lithium is in rechargeable batteries, fluorine is in the fluoride of toothpaste and potassium is in bananas. Their atoms differ in size in a way that seems backward at first: a fluorine atom has three times as many protons as a lithium atom, yet it is less than half as wide. This chapter uses measured data to find the patterns in atomic size and in the energy needed to pull an electron off an atom, and a simple model of the atom to explain them.Lithium is in batteries. Fluorine is in toothpaste. Potassium is in bananas. Their atoms have different sizes. A fluorine atom has three times as many protons as a lithium atom. But it is less than half as wide. This chapter looks at data on atom size. It also looks at the energy needed to remove an electron. A simple model explains both patterns.

Driving question

Why do atomic size and the energy to remove an electron change in a regular pattern across the periodic table?

§3.2.1

How large is an atom?

An atom has no hard outer surface. Its electrons occupy a region around the nucleus that fades out gradually, so the size of an atom depends on how it is measured. Chemists measure the distance between the nuclei of two atoms that are bonded together. For two bonded atoms of the same element, half of that distance is the element's .An atom has no hard edge. Its electrons fade out slowly. So chemists measure size in a set way. They find the distance between the centers of two bonded atoms of the element. Half of that distance is the covalent radius.

Atoms are tiny, so radii are given in (pm). One picometer is 10−12 m, a trillionth of a meter. In this unit's data, covalent radii range from 31 pm for hydrogen to 244 pm for cesium. In this lesson, means the covalent radius.Atoms are very small. Their radii are given in picometers (pm). One picometer is 10−12 m. In this unit, radii go from 31 pm (hydrogen) to 244 pm (cesium). In this lesson, atomic radius means the covalent radius.

A radius measured another way, such as in a metal crystal, can differ from the covalent radius by tens of picometers, so a fair comparison uses one kind of radius from one source. This unit uses the covalent radii of Cordero and coworkers (2008), averaged from many thousands of measured bond lengths. The noble gases are left out, because they form few or no compounds, so there are few bonded atoms to measure.Radii measured in other ways can differ by tens of picometers. A fair comparison uses one kind of radius from one source. This unit uses one set of covalent radii, from 2008. It leaves out the noble gases. They form few or no compounds.

Key idea

Atomic radius is measured as the covalent radius: half the distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms of the element. Compare radii only from one data set.

§3.2.2

A model with three factors

A simple model of the atom explains these patterns with three factors. The first is , the positive charge of the nucleus, equal to its number of protons. Opposite charges attract, so a nucleus with more protons pulls harder on every electron.A simple model explains these patterns. It uses three factors. The first is nuclear charge. That is the number of protons. The nucleus attracts the electrons. More protons pull harder.

The second is the of the outer electrons. Electrons in a higher energy level are, on average, farther from the nucleus. The outer electrons are the , in the highest occupied level, and they set the size of the atom.The second factor is the energy level of the outer electrons. Higher levels are farther from the nucleus. The outer electrons are the valence electrons. They set the size of the atom.

The third is . The lie between the nucleus and the outer electrons, and their negative charge cancels part of the nucleus's pull. In sodium, 10 inner electrons shield the single outer electron from the 11 protons.The third factor is shielding. Inner electrons sit between the nucleus and the outer electrons. Their negative charge blocks part of the pull. In sodium, 10 inner electrons shield the outer electron from 11 protons.

In Unit 2 you may have read that an atom "wants" a full outer level. Atoms do not want anything. The physical reason behind that shorthand is the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons, which this model describes.In Unit 2 you may have read that an atom "wants" a full outer level. Key idea

Three factors set the pull on the outer electrons: nuclear charge (more protons pull harder), energy level (higher levels are farther out) and shielding (inner electrons block part of the pull).

§3.2.2

Across a period, atoms shrink

Across period 3, from sodium to chlorine, each element has one more proton than the one before it. Its added electron goes into the same energy level, level 3, so the number of inner electrons stays at 10. Electrons in the same level shield each other very little. As a result, the pull on the outer electrons grows with each added proton, and they are drawn closer to the nucleus.Across period 3, each element has one more proton. Its new electron goes into the same level, level 3. The inner electrons stay at 10. Electrons in the same level shield each other very little. So the pull grows with each proton. The outer electrons are pulled closer.

§3.2.2

Down a group, atoms grow

Down group 1, each element has one more occupied energy level than the element above it. Lithium's outer electron is in level 2, sodium's in level 3 and potassium's in level 4. An electron in a higher level is farther from the nucleus on average.Down group 1, each element has one more energy level. Lithium's outer electron is in level 2. Sodium's is in level 3. Potassium's is in level 4. Higher levels are farther from the nucleus.

The nuclear charge also rises down the group, from 3 protons in lithium to 55 in cesium. But the number of inner electrons rises just as fast, from 2 to 54, and their shielding cancels most of the extra charge. The added distance wins, so the atoms get larger.Protons also increase down the group. Lithium has 3 and cesium has 55. But inner electrons increase too, from 2 to 54. They shield most of the extra charge. The larger distance wins. The atoms get larger.

§3.2.3

Ionization energy

Every electron in an atom is attracted to the nucleus, so removing one always takes energy. The is the energy needed to remove the most loosely held electron from each atom in a mole of gaseous atoms. Each atom becomes a positive :Every electron is attracted to the nucleus. So removing one always takes energy. The first ionization energy is the energy to remove one electron from each atom in a mole of gas atoms. Each atom becomes a positive ion.

Na(g) + 496 kJ → Na+(g) + e−   (for 1 mol of atoms)

Ionization energies are given in kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol). Sodium's is 496 kJ/mol, and helium's, 2372 kJ/mol, is the highest of any element. This unit uses values from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), rounded to whole kJ/mol.Ionization energy is given in kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol). Sodium needs 496 kJ/mol. Helium needs 2372 kJ/mol. That is the highest of all the elements. The values come from NIST.

The atoms must be gaseous so that each one is separate from its neighbors. Ionization energy describes one isolated atom. It is not the energy change when a solid metal reacts with water or air.The atoms must be a gas, so each atom is alone. Ionization energy is about one separate atom. It is not the energy of a reaction with water or air.

Key idea

First ionization energy is the energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in a mole of gaseous atoms. A larger value means the electron is held more tightly.

§3.2.3

Explaining the ionization energy trends

The same three factors explain ionization energy. Across a period, the nuclear charge grows while the inner electrons stay the same, so the outer electrons are held more tightly, and more energy is needed to remove one.The same three factors explain ionization energy. Across a period, protons increase. Inner electrons stay the same. The outer electrons are held more tightly. So it takes more energy to remove one.

Down a group, the outer electron is in a higher energy level, farther from the nucleus, with more inner electrons shielding it. It is held less tightly, so less energy removes it. Potassium (419 kJ/mol) loses its outer electron more easily than sodium (496 kJ/mol). Lesson 3.3 connects this to how strongly these metals react.Down a group, the outer electron is farther away. More inner electrons shield it. It is held less tightly. Less energy removes it. Potassium needs 419 kJ/mol. Sodium needs 496 kJ/mol. Lesson 3.3 connects this to reactions.

The largest change comes between a noble gas and the next element. Neon's first ionization energy is 2081 kJ/mol, and sodium's is only 496 kJ/mol. Sodium's outer electron is the first electron in level 3, outside 10 inner electrons, so it is farther away and well shielded.The biggest drop is from a noble gas to the next element. Neon needs 2081 kJ/mol. Sodium needs only 496 kJ/mol. Sodium's outer electron is the first one in level 3. It is far away and well shielded.

Key idea

An electron held more tightly needs more energy to remove. More protons with the same shielding raise ionization energy; a higher level and more shielding lower it.

Honors · Two dips in period 2

Boron's first ionization energy (801 kJ/mol) is lower than beryllium's (900 kJ/mol). Boron's outer electron is the first one in the 2p sublevel, which is higher in energy than 2s and partly shielded by the 2s electrons, so it is easier to remove.

Oxygen's (1314 kJ/mol) is lower than nitrogen's (1402 kJ/mol). Oxygen's fourth 2p electron must share an orbital with another electron, and the two electrons repel each other, so one of them is easier to remove. Period 3 shows the same two dips, at aluminum and sulfur.

§3.2.4

Rank and predict

To rank elements by a trend, first find them on the table. If they are in the same period, compare nuclear charge: the element farther right has the smaller radius and usually the higher ionization energy. If they are in the same group, compare energy levels: the element lower down has the larger radius and the lower ionization energy.To rank elements, find them on the table first. Same period? Compare protons. The element farther right is smaller. It usually has a higher ionization energy. Same group? Compare energy levels. The lower element is larger. It has a lower ionization energy.

Radius and ionization energy usually change in opposite directions, but this is a pattern, not a rule. Magnesium (141 pm) is larger than lithium (128 pm), yet its ionization energy (738 kJ/mol) is higher than lithium's (520 kJ/mol). When two elements differ in both period and group, check the data.Radius and ionization energy usually change in opposite ways. This is a pattern, not a rule. Magnesium is larger than lithium. But magnesium needs more energy to lose an electron. If elements are in different periods and groups, check the data.

A trend can also predict a missing value. An element's value usually lies between the values of the elements above and below it in its group. The prediction gives a likely range, not an exact number, so a measured value is still needed.A trend can predict a missing value. The value is usually between the elements above and below it. This gives a range, not an exact number. We still need to measure it.

Key idea

Use position to rank: across a period, radius decreases and ionization energy generally increases; down a group, radius increases and ionization energy decreases. Check the data when elements differ in both.
