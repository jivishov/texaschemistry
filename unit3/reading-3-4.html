Chemists can still say a great deal about it, because it belongs to a family. Lessons 3.2 and 3.3 showed how radius, ionization energy, electronegativity and reactivity change down a group. §3.4.1

Predicting from neighbors

The family gives rubidium's chemistry: 1 valence electron, an Rb+ ion and a violent reaction with water. The neighbors give its numbers. Rubidium sits between potassium and cesium, so a simple estimate of each property is the average of their two values.The family gives rubidium's chemistry. It has 1 valence electron and forms Rb+. It reacts violently with water. The neighbors give its numbers. Rubidium is between potassium and cesium. So we can estimate each value as the average of theirs.

An average is only an estimate. The reference values for rubidium all lie between its neighbors' values, as the trend predicts, but none of them equals the average exactly. A family trend gives a likely range, not an exact value, and a property that does not change smoothly, like the density of potassium, makes the estimate less reliable.An average is only an estimate. Rubidium's real values are between its neighbors' values. But none equals the average. A trend gives a likely range, not an exact value. If a property does not change smoothly, the estimate is less reliable.

Key idea

An element's neighbors in its group give a likely range for a property. The average of the neighbors is an estimate to check against data, not a measurement.

§3.4.1

The alkaline earth metals

The of group 2 each have 2 valence electrons, which they lose to form 2+ ions, such as Mg2+ and Ca2+. Each one is harder, denser and higher melting than the alkali metal beside it. Lesson 3.3 showed that each reacts with water more slowly than the alkali metal beside it, and that their reactivity also increases down the group.Alkaline earth metals are in group 2. Each has 2 valence electrons. They lose both to form 2+ ions, such as Mg2+. They are harder and denser than the alkali metals. They melt at higher temperatures. They react with water more slowly than the alkali metal beside them. Down the group, they react faster.

§3.4.2

Predicting astatine

Astatine, below iodine, is the heaviest halogen found in nature. Every of astatine is . The most stable one, astatine-210, has a of about 8.1 hours: half of any sample decays in that time. Astatine is so rare and decays so quickly that no one has ever collected a sample large enough to see.Astatine is below iodine. Every isotope of astatine is radioactive. Its most stable isotope has a half-life of about 8.1 hours. In that time, half of a sample decays. No one has ever collected enough astatine to see.

So astatine's melting point, boiling point, color and density have never been measured. The family still makes predictions. Astatine should have 7 valence electrons and form At− ions. It should be a solid at room temperature, because iodine already is, and it should be less reactive than iodine.So no one has measured astatine's melting point, boiling point, color or density. The family still predicts. Astatine should have 7 valence electrons. It should form At− ions. It should be a solid, like iodine. It should be less reactive than iodine.

A few properties have been measured on a small number of atoms. In 2013, scientists at CERN, near Geneva in Switzerland, used lasers to measure astatine's first ionization energy: 899 kJ/mol. This fits the group trend, lower than iodine's 1008 kJ/mol. A table that lists a melting point for astatine is showing a predicted value, not a measurement.Some properties have been measured on a few atoms. In 2013, scientists measured astatine's first ionization energy. It is 899 kJ/mol. That fits the trend: it is lower than iodine's, 1008 kJ/mol. A melting point for astatine in a table is a prediction, not a measurement.

Key idea

A family trend can predict properties that have never been measured, such as astatine's state. Until data test it, a prediction stays a prediction.

Honors · Francium, the other unseen element

Francium, below cesium in group 1, is also too radioactive to collect. Its most stable isotope, francium-223, has a half-life of about 22 minutes. Its first ionization energy, 393 kJ/mol, has been measured on a small number of atoms.

The simple group trend predicts a value below cesium's, 376 kJ/mol, but francium's is higher. In very heavy atoms, electrons close to the nucleus move at a large fraction of the speed of light, which pulls the outer electron slightly closer. The model in this unit leaves this effect out, so here its prediction fails.

§3.4.3

The noble gases

The of group 18 have a full outer level: 8 electrons, or 2 for helium. A full outer level is very stable, so each noble gas has the highest first ionization energy in its period, and the noble gases rarely react. They exist as single atoms, not as molecules, and all of them are colorless gases at room temperature.Noble gases are in group 18. Their outer level is full. It has 8 electrons, or 2 for helium. A full outer level is very stable. So noble gases rarely react. Their atoms stay single. All are colorless gases at room temperature.

§3.4.3

Xenon breaks the rule

For decades, the group 18 elements were called the inert gases, and chemists predicted that they could form no compounds at all. In 1962, Neil Bartlett, a chemist at the University of British Columbia in Canada, tested that prediction.For many years, people called group 18 the inert gases. Chemists predicted they could form no compounds. In 1962, a chemist named Neil Bartlett tested this.

Bartlett had found that platinum hexafluoride, PtF6, a very reactive compound, can remove an electron from an oxygen molecule, O2. Removing an electron from O2 takes 1165 kJ/mol, almost the same as xenon's first ionization energy, 1170 kJ/mol. He therefore predicted that PtF6 would react with xenon too. When he mixed xenon gas with PtF6 vapor, an orange-yellow solid formed: the first compound of a noble gas.Bartlett knew that a very reactive compound, PtF6, can take an electron from an oxygen molecule. That takes 1165 kJ/mol. Xenon's first ionization energy is 1170 kJ/mol, almost the same. So he predicted xenon would react too. He mixed xenon with PtF6. An orange-yellow solid formed. It was the first noble gas compound.

Chemists soon made compounds of xenon with fluorine, such as XeF2 and XeF4, and later with oxygen. Krypton forms a few compounds too, but helium, neon and argon still form no compounds under ordinary conditions. The heavier noble gases have lower ionization energies, so the most reactive substances can react with them. New evidence changed a prediction that chemists had trusted for decades.Soon chemists made compounds of xenon with fluorine, such as XeF2. Later they made some with oxygen. Krypton forms a few compounds too. Helium, neon and argon form none under ordinary conditions. Heavier noble gases hold their outer electrons less tightly. New evidence changed an old prediction.

Key idea

Family predictions are strong but not certain. Xenon's lower ionization energy let Bartlett predict, then make, the first noble gas compound in 1962.

§3.4.4

Transition metals

The fill groups 3 to 12, the d block. Unit 2 showed that, for them, the group number does not give the number of valence electrons, and that many form ions with more than one charge, such as Fe2+ and Fe3+, or Cu+ and Cu2+. A transition metal's ion therefore cannot be predicted from its group the way a sodium or chlorine ion can.Transition metals are in groups 3 to 12. Unit 2 showed that their group number does not give their valence electrons. Many form ions with more than one charge, such as Fe2+ and Fe3+. So their group does not predict one ion.

Most transition metals are hard, dense and shiny, with high melting points, and they conduct electricity and heat well. Mercury, a liquid at room temperature, is the main exception. Most are much less reactive than the metals of groups 1 and 2, and many of their compounds are colored.Most transition metals are hard, dense and shiny. They melt at high temperatures. They conduct electricity and heat well. Mercury is a liquid, so it is an exception. Most react much less than group 1 and 2 metals. Many of their compounds are colored.

§3.4.4

Alike across a row

Across period 4, the main-group elements change a great deal. First ionization energy rises from 579 kJ/mol for gallium to 1351 kJ/mol for krypton, and the elements change from a metal to a noble gas. Across the transition metals, the change is much smaller: from 633 kJ/mol for scandium to 745 kJ/mol for copper.Across period 4, the main-group elements change a lot. Ionization energy rises from 579 kJ/mol for gallium to 1351 kJ/mol for krypton. Across the transition metals, it changes much less. Scandium is 633 kJ/mol. Copper is 745 kJ/mol.

In the main groups, each added electron goes into the outer level. In the transition metals, each added electron goes into an inner level, level 3 in period 4, while the outer level, level 4, keeps only 1 or 2 electrons. The extra inner electrons shield much of the extra nuclear charge, so the outer electrons feel a similar pull from one element to the next, and neighbors in the row have similar properties.In the main groups, each new electron goes into the outer level. In the transition metals, each new electron goes into an inner level. The outer level keeps 1 or 2 electrons. The new inner electrons shield the extra protons. So the outer electrons feel a similar pull. Neighbors in the row are alike.

So a transition metal can be predicted from its neighbors in the same row, not only from its group. Zinc, in group 12, forms only Zn2+, and some chemists do not count it as a transition metal. This course follows Unit 2 and includes groups 3 to 12.So we can predict a transition metal from its neighbors in the same row. Zinc forms only Zn2+. Some chemists do not call it a transition metal. This course includes groups 3 to 12, like Unit 2.

Key idea

Across the transition metals, the added electrons go into an inner level, so neighbors in a row are alike. That is how they differ from the main-group families.
