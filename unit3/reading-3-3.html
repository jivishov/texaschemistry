3.3

Electronegativity and reactivity

A small piece of sodium placed on water darts across the surface, fizzing. Cesium, two places below sodium in group 1, explodes. Chlorine kills germs in drinking water and swimming pools, and a solution of iodine is used to clean cuts. Some elements react far more readily than others, and the differences follow the periodic table. This chapter uses data on electronegativity and reactivity, and the model from Lesson 3.2, to explain why.A small piece of sodium on water darts around and fizzes. Cesium explodes in water. Cesium is lower in group 1. Chlorine kills germs in pools. Iodine solution cleans cuts. Some elements react much more than others. The differences follow the periodic table. §3.3.1

Electronegativity

In Lesson 3.2, ionization energy described a single atom in a gas. In most substances, though, atoms are joined to other atoms by . In many bonds, two atoms share a pair of electrons, and both nuclei attract that pair. Unit 4 explains the kinds of bonds.Ionization energy is about one atom alone. But in most substances, atoms are joined by chemical bonds. In many bonds, two atoms share a pair of electrons. Both nuclei pull on the pair. Unit 4 explains bonds.

is the ability of an atom in a chemical bond to attract the shared electrons toward itself. When two different atoms share electrons, the more electronegative atom pulls the shared pair closer to itself, as the figure shows for hydrogen fluoride.Electronegativity is how strongly an atom in a bond pulls on the shared electrons. The atom with the higher value pulls the pair closer. The figure shows this for hydrogen fluoride.

Linus Pauling introduced the most widely used electronegativity scale in 1932, and most of its values were recalculated from newer measurements in 1961. On the , fluorine has the highest value, 3.98, and in this unit's data cesium has the lowest, 0.79. The values compare atoms with each other, so they have no unit. Helium, neon and argon form no compounds under ordinary conditions, so they have no Pauling value, and this unit's data leave out all the noble gases.Linus Pauling made the most used scale in 1932. On the Pauling scale, fluorine is highest, 3.98. Cesium is lowest in this unit's data, 0.79. The values compare atoms, so they have no unit. This unit's data have no values for the noble gases.

Key idea

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom in a chemical bond to attract the shared electrons. On the Pauling scale it has no unit, and fluorine has the highest value, 3.98.

§3.3.1

Across period 2, electronegativity rises steadily, from 0.98 for lithium to 3.98 for fluorine. Down group 17 it falls, from fluorine (3.98) to chlorine (3.16), bromine (2.96) and iodine (2.66). §3.3.1

Explaining the electronegativity trends

The model from Lesson 3.2 explains these trends. Across a period, each element has one more proton, while the inner electrons stay the same. The nucleus pulls harder on the outer level, including the electrons it shares in a bond. Fluorine, with 9 protons and only 2 inner electrons, pulls hardest of all.The model from Lesson 3.2 explains this. Across a period, protons increase. Inner electrons stay the same. So the nucleus pulls harder on shared electrons. Fluorine has 9 protons and only 2 inner electrons. It pulls hardest.

Down a group, the shared electrons are in a higher energy level, farther from the nucleus and shielded by more inner electrons. Iodine has 53 protons, but its outer electrons are in level 5, behind 46 inner electrons, so it attracts shared electrons less strongly than chlorine does.Down a group, shared electrons are farther away. More inner electrons shield them. Iodine has 53 protons. But its outer level is level 5, behind 46 inner electrons. So iodine pulls less than chlorine.

Electronegativity and ionization energy follow similar trends, but they are different properties. Ionization energy is measured on a single gaseous atom losing an electron, and its unit is kJ/mol. Electronegativity describes an atom in a bond attracting shared electrons, and it has no unit.Electronegativity and ionization energy have similar trends. But they are different. Ionization energy is about one gas atom losing an electron. Its unit is kJ/mol. Electronegativity is about an atom in a bond. It has no unit.

Key idea

Electronegativity is high when many protons pull on a small outer level with little shielding, as in fluorine. It is low when the outer level is far out and well shielded, as in cesium.

§3.3.2

Metals in water

is how readily a substance takes part in chemical reactions. Chemists compare the reactivity of metals by how fast they react with the same substance, such as cold water. Every group 1 metal reacts with water. It makes hydrogen gas and a metal hydroxide.

§3.3.3

Sodium and magnesium

Sodium and magnesium sit side by side in period 3, yet sodium reacts with cold water quickly and magnesium only very slightly. Unit 2 said that losing two electrons takes more energy than losing one. Ionization energies show how much more.Sodium and magnesium are next to each other in period 3. Sodium reacts with cold water quickly. Magnesium reacts very slightly. Unit 2 said that losing two electrons takes more energy than losing one. The data show how much more.

A magnesium atom loses two electrons when it reacts, forming Mg2+. Removing the first takes its first ionization energy, 738 kJ/mol. The is the energy needed to remove an electron from each Mg+ ion in a mole of gaseous ions:A magnesium atom loses two electrons when it reacts. It forms Mg2+. The first electron takes 738 kJ/mol. The second ionization energy is the energy to remove the next electron from each Mg+ ion.

Mg+(g) + 1451 kJ → Mg2+(g) + e−   (for 1 mol of ions)

The second electron is pulled away from a positive ion, so it is held more tightly than the first. Magnesium's second ionization energy, 1451 kJ/mol, is about twice its first, even though both electrons come from level 3.The second electron is pulled from a positive ion. So it is held more tightly. Magnesium's second ionization energy is 1451 kJ/mol. That is about twice the first. Both electrons are in level 3.

Key idea

The second ionization energy is the energy to remove an electron from each 1+ ion in a mole of gaseous ions. It is always larger than the first.

§3.3.3

Two electrons cost more

Forming Na+ takes 496 kJ/mol. Forming Mg2+ takes both of magnesium's first two ionization energies: 738 + 1451 = 2189 kJ/mol, about 4.4 times as much. This large energy cost helps explain why magnesium reacts so much more slowly than sodium. It is not the whole story, and the ratio does not tell us how much slower the reaction is. A coating of magnesium hydroxide that forms on the metal also slows the reaction down, and other energy changes, such as water molecules gathering around the new ions, matter too.Forming Na+ takes 496 kJ/mol. Forming Mg2+ takes 738 + 1451 = 2189 kJ/mol. That is about 4.4 times as much. This helps explain why magnesium reacts more slowly. It is not the whole story. A coating also forms on magnesium and slows it down.

Why does sodium not lose a second electron too? Sodium's second electron would come from level 2, close to the nucleus and shielded only by the 2 electrons in level 1. Its second ionization energy, 4562 kJ/mol, is more than 9 times its first, so sodium forms only Na+ in its reactions.Why does sodium not lose a second electron? Its second electron is in level 2. That is close to the nucleus. Only 2 electrons shield it. Removing it takes 4562 kJ/mol, more than 9 times the first. So sodium forms only Na+.

The same comparison holds in period 4: potassium, in group 1, reacts with cold water much faster than calcium, in group 2, which must lose two electrons.Period 4 is the same. Potassium is in group 1. It reacts faster than calcium. Calcium is in group 2. It must lose two electrons.

Key idea

A group 2 metal must lose two electrons, and the second is pulled from a positive ion. It therefore reacts more slowly than the group 1 metal beside it.

§3.3.4

Halogen displacement

The halogens in group 17 are reactive nonmetals. Fluorine is a pale yellow gas, chlorine a greenish-yellow gas, bromine a red-brown liquid and iodine a gray-black solid. When a halogen reacts with a metal, each halogen atom gains one electron and becomes a 1− , a : chlorine becomes chloride, Cl−.The halogens in group 17 are reactive nonmetals. Fluorine and chlorine are gases. Bromine is a red-brown liquid. Iodine is a gray-black solid. When a halogen reacts with a metal, each atom gains one electron. It becomes a halide ion, such as chloride, Cl−.

Halogens can be compared by a . A solution of a halogen is added to a solution of a halide salt. If the added halogen is more reactive than the halogen in the salt, it takes an electron from each halide ion, and the less reactive halogen is set free:We can compare halogens with a displacement reaction. A halogen solution is added to a halide salt solution. A more reactive halogen takes electrons from the halide ions. The less reactive halogen is set free.

chlorine + potassium bromide → potassium chloride + bromine

The color shows whether a reaction happened. Chlorine water is pale yellow-green, bromine water is yellow-orange and iodine solution is brown, while the potassium halide solutions are colorless. A new color means a new halogen has formed; no change means no reaction. In class, these reactions are shown by video only.The color shows the result. Chlorine water is pale yellow-green. Bromine water is yellow-orange. Iodine solution is brown. The salt solutions have no color. A new color means a reaction. No change means no reaction. In class, you see these on video only.

Key idea

In a displacement reaction, a more reactive halogen takes electrons from the halide ions of a less reactive halogen. A color change shows that it happened.

§3.3.4

Why reactivity rises up group 17

The results give a clear order. Chlorine displaces bromine and iodine, bromine displaces only iodine, and iodine displaces neither. Reactivity therefore decreases down group 17: chlorine > bromine > iodine. Fluorine, at the top of the group, is the most reactive halogen.The results give an order. Chlorine pushes out bromine and iodine. Bromine pushes out only iodine. Iodine pushes out neither. So chlorine is most reactive, then bromine, then iodine. Fluorine, at the top, is the most reactive halogen.

A halogen reacts by gaining one electron. Up the group the atoms are smaller: fluorine's radius is 57 pm and iodine's is 139 pm. In a smaller atom, an incoming electron can come closer to the nucleus, with fewer inner electrons shielding it, so it is attracted more strongly. That makes the smaller halogen more reactive.A halogen reacts by gaining one electron. Up the group, the atoms are smaller. Fluorine is 57 pm. Iodine is 139 pm. In a small atom, the new electron gets closer to the nucleus. Fewer inner electrons shield it. It is pulled more strongly. So small halogens are more reactive.

This is the reverse of the metal trend. Metals react by losing electrons, which becomes easier farther from the nucleus. Halogens react by gaining an electron, which becomes harder farther from the nucleus. The same model of distance and shielding explains both trends. Like the metal explanation, it is simplified: the full explanation includes other energy changes.This is the opposite of the metal trend. Metals lose electrons. That is easier far from the nucleus. Halogens gain an electron. That is harder far from the nucleus. One model explains both trends. It is a simple model.

Key idea

Halogens react by gaining an electron. Smaller atoms attract an extra electron more strongly, so reactivity increases up group 17.

Honors · Where the simple model falls short

The energy released when a gaseous atom gains an electron is its electron affinity. The simple model predicts that fluorine should release the most. Measured values show that chlorine releases slightly more: 349 kJ/mol, compared with 328 kJ/mol for fluorine. In fluorine's very small atom, the added electron is crowded close to the other outer electrons, which repel it.

Fluorine is still the more reactive element, for reasons that include the weak bond holding the two atoms of an F2 molecule together. Electron affinity alone does not decide reactivity.
