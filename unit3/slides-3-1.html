          What triads showed, and their limit

          Mass and properties are linked: the middle element is in between in both. Bromine, a liquid, sits between chlorine, a gas, and iodine, a solid.

          Limit: only a few sets of three fit. Most known elements belonged to no triad.

          1. Triads · 1 min. Döbereiner noticed calcium, strontium and barium in 1817 and published more triads in 1829.
          2. Average · 2 min. Students check the calculation on calculators.
          3. Limits · 1 min. Ask: what would a complete system need? (A place for every element.)
          4. Check · 1 min. Misconceptions: adding without dividing (46) or subtracting (32).

          Early ideas · Textbook §3.1.1 · SEP 2(B)

            Newlands' octaves

            Cards are in order of atomic mass. Try rows of 6, 7 and 8. Which row length makes every column match?

            Colors: hydrogen atoms per atom in each element's hydrogen compound.

            Newlands, 1865

            Listed by mass, each element resembles the one seven places later: the eighth repeats the first, the law of octaves.

            Limits

            The pattern fails after calcium. Newlands left no gaps, so unlike elements had to share columns. In 1866 one listener joked that alphabetical order might work as well; in 1887 the Royal Society awarded him the Davy Medal.

            1. Deal · 3 min. Start at rows of 6. Students call out the next row length to try. Rows of 7 make every column match.
            2. Octaves · 1 min. Seven elements per row: counting the first, the eighth repeats it. The noble gases were unknown in 1865.
            3. Limits · 1 min. A good idea can be rejected at first; evidence later earned Newlands the medal.

              Properties first

              Tellurium is heavier than iodine, but iodine matches chlorine and bromine. He put tellurium first and listed its mass as 125.

              Gaps

              A dash marks an element not yet found: eka-boron (44), eka-aluminum (68), eka-silicon (72).

              1. Mass and formulas · 2 min. Point to the oxide row: every group I element forms R2O, every group II element RO. Lothar Meyer built a similar table independently (atomic volume graph, 1870).
              2. Properties first · 1 min. Measured masses put tellurium after iodine; Mendeleev suspected an error. The masses were right (slide 11).
              3. Gaps · 1 min. Return to prediction 3. The gaps were deliberate.
              4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: the table was alphabetical or ordered by discovery date.

                Mendeleev · Textbook §3.1.2

                  Testing Mendeleev's predictions

                  Gallium, 1875

                  Lecoq de Boisbaudran found it in a zinc ore. He first measured a density of 4.7 g/cm³. Mendeleev wrote that it should be close to 6. The purified metal measured 5.9 g/cm³.

                  Germanium, 1886

                  Eka-silicon, 1871Germanium
                  Mass7272.630 u
                  Density5.5 g/cm³5.323 g/cm³
                  OxideEsO2GeO2

                  A real test

                  Each discovery could have failed to match. Gallium (1875), scandium (1879) and germanium (1886) matched, and most chemists then accepted the table.

                  1. Gallium · 1 min. The predicted value led to a better measurement: a model can guide experiments.
                  2. Germanium · 1 min. Clemens Winkler found it. Scandium: Lars Nilson found it in 1879; Per Cleve matched it to eka-boron.
                  3. The test · 1 min. Ask: what result would have counted against the table?
                  4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: any discovery supports the table. Only a matching one does.

                    Discovery

                    Switch the order to atomic number.

                    1. Discovery · 1 min. Rayleigh and Ramsay found argon through a puzzle: nitrogen from air was denser than nitrogen from compounds. Ramsay and Travers then found neon, krypton and xenon. Ramsay won the 1904 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
                    2. New column · 1 min. Rows of 8 now line up. The table took in a new family without being rebuilt.
                    3. Mass order · 2 min. Ordered by mass, argon (39.95 u) falls after potassium (39.098 u). Ask which two cards are in the wrong column. Atomic numbers were not known until 1913 to 1914; slide 12 shows where they came from.
                    4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: elements are grouped by state. Groups are chemical families.

                      Properties decide the column

                      Potassium is a soft metal that reacts with water, like sodium. Argon is an unreactive gas, like neon. Mass order would put each in the wrong column.

                      An unexplained problem

                      Before 1913, mass was the only number measured for every element. Something more basic than mass had to set the order.

                      1. Four pairs · 1 min. Precise measurements confirmed every pair; the masses were not errors.
                      2. Properties · 1 min. Ask: which matters more for a chemist, mass or behavior?
                      3. Open question · 1 min. Set up slide 12: what could be more basic than mass?

                      Atomic number · Textbook §3.1.3 · SEP 2(B)

                        Moseley and atomic number

                        Zoom in on cobalt and nickel. Against mass, the line steps backward.

                        Switch the x-axis to atomic number: one straight line, one step per element.

                        Periodic law

                        Atomic number = protons = nuclear charge. In order of atomic number, properties repeat.

                        1. Against mass · 1 min. Moseley, 1913 to 1914, measured the X-rays each element gives off when struck by fast electrons. The graph is computed from his law, not his data.
                        2. Against number · 2 min. Hollow circles: atomic numbers with no known element in 1914 (43, 61, 72, 75), all found or made later. Return to prediction 1.
                        3. Periodic law · 1 min. Van den Broek proposed that position equals nuclear charge; Moseley's data confirmed it. Moseley was killed at Gallipoli in 1915, aged 27.
                        4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: neutrons set the order. End of period 1.

                        Modern table · Textbook §3.1.4 · SEP 1(G)

                          Reading the modern table

                          Periods and groups

                          Periods are rows 1 to 7: the highest occupied energy level. Groups are columns 1 to 18.

                          Blocks

                          s: groups 1 and 2, plus He. d: groups 3 to 12. p: groups 13 to 18. f: the two detached rows, part of periods 6 and 7.

                          Families

                          Alkali metals (1), alkaline earth metals (2), transition metals (3 to 12), halogens (17), noble gases (18). Switch the coloring to Family.

                          1. Periods and groups · 2 min. Click an element and read its period and group aloud. Period 1 has 2 elements, periods 2 and 3 have 8, periods 4 and 5 have 18, periods 6 and 7 have 32.
                          2. Blocks · 1 min. The f rows are printed below so the table fits a page. This course places lanthanum and actinium in group 3 (Royal Society of Chemistry layout).
                          3. Families · 1 min. Lesson 3.4 predicts the properties of each family.

                          Modern table · Textbook §3.1.4

                            Metals, nonmetals and metalloids

                            Metals · left and middle

                            Most elements. Copper wire, iron, gold.

                            1. Metals · 1 min. About four of every five elements are metals.
                            2. Nonmetals · 1 min. Hydrogen sits in group 1 but is a nonmetal gas. Shine is not a test: sulfur is dull, but iodine crystals are shiny.
                            3. Metalloids · 1 min. Some tables list a few more than six; the boundary is not sharp.
                            4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: every solid is a metal.

                              Mass data · Textbook §3.1.5 · SEP 2(B)

                                Atomic mass against atomic number

                                Trend

                                Mass rises about 2 u per element at first, closer to 3 u for heavier elements.

                                Steps down

                                Find the three places where the line drops: Ar to K, Co to Ni, Te to I.

                                Why

                                Argon-40 has 22 neutrons; potassium-39 has 20. One more proton, two fewer neutrons.

                                1. Trend · 1 min. Heavier nuclei hold more neutrons per proton, so the slope grows.
                                2. Steps down · 2 min. Students click the marked points and read the masses. Return to prediction 2.
                                3. Why · 1 min. Link to Lesson 2.3: average atomic mass depends on isotopes and their abundance.
                                4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: any pair in order is an inversion.

                                          Wrap-up · Exit ticket · SEP 3(A)

                                            Answer the driving question

                                            How did evidence turn a list of elements into a table that predicts? Write your answer in your notebook.

                                            Word bank
                                            propertiesatomic massgappredictiondiscoveryatomic numberX-rayperiodic law

                                            Claim

                                            One sentence that answers the question.

                                            1. The table became predictive when…
                                            2. Its order was settled by…

                                            Sample claim

                                            Chemists grouped elements by repeating properties, and Mendeleev's gaps let the table predict; Moseley's data later fixed its order by atomic number.

                                            Evidence

                                            Two pieces of data or discoveries.

                                            1. Mendeleev predicted… and later…
                                            2. Argon and potassium…
                                            3. Moseley's X-rays…

                                            Sample evidence

                                            Eka-silicon was predicted at about 72 and germanium was found at 72.630 u. Argon (39.95 u) belongs before potassium (39.098 u), and the square root of the X-ray frequency rises in equal steps with atomic number.

                                            Reasoning

                                            Why that evidence supports the claim.

                                            1. A prediction that matches a later discovery…
                                            2. Mass order fails, but atomic number…

                                            Sample reasoning

                                            A prediction that could have failed but matched shows the arrangement reflects a real pattern. Atomic number, the nuclear charge, orders every element correctly, so properties repeat in order of atomic number.

                                            Self-check: mark each part your answer meets, then compare with the sample.

                                            3. Reasoning · 3 min. Must explain why a matching prediction is evidence.
                                            4. Self-check · 2 min. Students self-check, then you show the sample. Score 0 to 2 per part.
