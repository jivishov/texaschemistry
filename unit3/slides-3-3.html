        Electronegativity · Textbook §3.3.1

          Electronegativity

          In a bond, atoms share electrons

          No unit

          Electronegativity compares atoms with each other, so its values have no unit. Ionization energy has the unit kJ/mol.

          The Pauling scale

          Electronegativity: the ability of an atom in a bond to attract the shared electrons.

          Introduced by Linus Pauling in 1932. Highest: fluorine, 3.98. Lowest in this unit's data: cesium, 0.79. Helium, neon and argon form no compounds under ordinary conditions, so they have no value.

          1. Shared pair · 1 min. Do not teach bond types: Unit 4 does that. Here a bond is two atoms sharing a pair of electrons.
          2. No unit · 30 s. Ask: why can a scale that compares atoms have no unit?
          3. Pauling scale · 1 min. Most values in use today were recalculated from newer data in 1961. Krypton and xenon do form a few compounds, but this unit's data leave out all the noble gases.
          4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: electronegativity and ionization energy are the same property.

              Electronegativity · Textbook §3.3.1

                Explaining electronegativity

                Across a period →

                More protons, same inner electrons: a stronger pull on the outer level, including shared electrons.

                Fluorine: 9 protons, only 2 inner electrons. The strongest pull of all.

                Down a group ↓

                A higher level, more shielding: shared electrons are farther out and pulled less.

                Iodine: 53 protons, but level 5 and 46 inner electrons. 2.66, against chlorine's 3.16.

                Similar trends, different properties

                Ionization energy: one gaseous atom loses an electron; kJ/mol.

                Electronegativity: an atom in a bond attracts shared electrons; no unit.

                1. Across · 1 min. Same reasoning as radius in Lesson 3.2: a stronger pull, a smaller atom.
                2. Down · 1 min. Ask: iodine has more protons than chlorine. Why does it pull less?
                3. Compare · 1 min. Students write the two definitions side by side in their notebooks.
                4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: metals have no electronegativity.

                    Metals · Textbook §3.3.2

                      Reactivity down a metal group

                      Metals lose electrons

                      Each sodium atom loses its outer electron and becomes a cation, Na+. The more easily it is lost, the faster the reaction.

                      Group 1

                      LevelkJ/molIn water
                      Li2520fizzes gently
                      Na3496melts, darts
                      K4419lilac flame
                      Rb5403violent
                      Cs6376explodes

                      A simplified explanation

                      Ionization energy describes one gaseous atom; a real reaction happens at a metal surface in water, with other energy changes. The trends still match.

                      Faster is not more heat. Per mole of metal, lithium releases about 222 kJ and cesium about 203 kJ.

                      1. Lose one · 1 min. Avoid "wants to lose". The outer electron is held less tightly down the group.
                      2. Data · 2 min. Ask: as ionization energy falls, what happens to the observations?
                      3. Simplified · 1 min. SEP 2(A): a model can explain a trend and still leave things out. The heat values are from Chemguide.
                      4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: a faster reaction must release more heat.

                      Sodium and magnesium · Textbook §3.3.3 · SEP 1(G)

                        Sodium and magnesium

                        A question from Unit 2

                        Losing two electrons takes more energy than losing one. How much more?

                        Second ionization energy

                        The energy to remove an electron from each 1+ ion in 1 mol of gaseous ions.

                        Mg+(g) + 1451 kJ → Mg2+(g) + e−

                        Try it

                        Remove one electron from each atom, then a second. Compare the totals.

                        1. From Unit 2 · 30 s. Unit 2 said losing two electrons takes more energy than losing one. Today's data show how much.
                        2. Second IE · 1 min. The second electron is pulled from a positive ion, so it is held more tightly. It is always larger than the first.
                        3. Try it · 2 min. Ask a student to predict the totals before clicking. Removing sodium's second electron shows why sodium forms only Na+.

                        Sodium and magnesium · Textbook §3.3.3

                          Energy to lose two electrons

                          Add

                          Na+496 kJ/mol

                          Mg2+738 + 1451 = 2189 kJ/mol

                          Compare

                          Divide2189 ÷ 496 ≈ 4.4

                          One reason magnesium reacts more slowly. A coating of magnesium hydroxide also slows it.

                          Why not Na2+?

                          Sodium's second electron is in level 2, close to the nucleus. 4562 kJ/mol, more than 9 times the first. Sodium forms only Na+.

                          1. Add · 1 min. Both electrons must be removed, so both energies count.
                          2. Compare · 1 min. Return to prediction 2. The same holds in period 4: potassium reacts faster than calcium.
                          3. Sodium · 1 min. Ask: why is sodium's second electron so hard to remove, when magnesium's is not?
                          4. Check · 1 min. Misconception: losing two electrons is twice as fast.

                          Halogens · Textbook §3.3.4

                            The halogens

                            Group 17 at room temperature

                            ElementLooks like
                            F2fluorinepale yellow gas
                            Cl2chlorinegreenish-yellow gas
                            Br2brominered-brown liquid
                            I2iodinegray-black solid

                            Halogens gain one electron

                            Each halogen atom gains one electron to form a halide ion, a 1− anion.

                            chlorine → chloride, Cl− · bromine → bromide, Br− · iodine → iodide, I−

                            Displacement

                            A more reactive halogen takes electrons from the halide ions of a less reactive one:

                            chlorine + potassium bromide → potassium chloride + bromine

                            1. Family · 1 min. Same colors as Unit 2. Chlorine and bromine are toxic; this is why they are shown by video only.
                            2. Gain one · 1 min. Compare with metals: metals lose electrons, halogens gain one.
                            3. Displacement · 1 min. Do not show the results yet: students predict them on the next slide.

                              Halogen displacement

                              Predict first

                              Colors

                              Chlorine water: pale yellow-green. Bromine water: yellow-orange. Iodine solution: brown. Salt solutions: colorless.

                              Halogens · Textbook §3.3.4

                                Reactivity up group 17

                                Sizes

                                Radius: F 57, Cl 102, Br 120, I 139 pm. The atoms get larger down the group.

                                Attracting an extra electron

                                In a smaller atom, an incoming electron comes closer to the nucleus, with less shielding, so it is attracted more strongly. Fluorine is the most reactive halogen.

                                Honors · A limit of the model

                                Chlorine releases slightly more energy than fluorine when it gains an electron (349 against 328 kJ/mol). Fluorine is still more reactive, partly because its F–F bond is weak.

                                1. Sizes · 1 min. Click the bars to read values. Radii from Lesson 3.2.
                                2. Attraction · 2 min. This model is simplified; the full explanation includes other energy changes.
                                3. Honors · 1 min. Optional. Electron affinity is not in the TEKS; use it only to show that a simple model has limits.

                                Halogens · Textbook §3.3.4

                                  Metal and halogen trends

                                  Metals: groups 1 and 2 ↓

                                  Metals lose electrons. Farther from the nucleus, an electron is easier to remove.

                                  Reactivity increases down the group.

                                  Halogens: group 17 ↑

                                  Halogens gain an electron. Farther from the nucleus, it is attracted less strongly.

                                  Reactivity increases up the group.

                                  One model

                                  Distance and shielding explain both trends. The difference is losing electrons against gaining one.

                                        Sample answer

                                        When they react, sodium loses one electron and magnesium loses two. Forming Na+ takes 496 kJ/mol, while forming Mg2+ takes 738 + 1451 = 2189 kJ/mol, about 4.4 times as much. The second electron is harder to remove because it is pulled away from a positive ion. This larger cost helps explain why magnesium reacts with cold water much more slowly than sodium; a coating of magnesium hydroxide on the metal also slows it.

                                          Bromine water turns potassium iodide solution brown, but not potassium chloride solution. What does this show? Explain using atomic size.

                                          Think silently. Plan what you will say.

                                          0:30

                                          Explain your idea to your partner, then switch.

                                          1:00

                                          Write your answer in your notebook. Use these sentence starters:

                                          1. The brown color shows that…
                                          2. No change with potassium chloride shows that…
                                          3. A bromine atom is larger than a chlorine atom, so…
                                          4. The order of reactivity is therefore…
                                          Word bank
                                          displacement reactionhalide ionmore reactiveatomic radiusshieldinggain an electron

                                          Success criteria

                                          Sample answer

                                          The brown color shows that iodine formed: bromine took an electron from each iodide ion, so bromine is more reactive than iodine. No change with potassium chloride shows that bromine cannot take electrons from chloride ions, so chlorine is more reactive than bromine. A bromine atom (120 pm) is larger than a chlorine atom (102 pm), so an extra electron is farther from the nucleus and attracted less strongly. The order of reactivity is therefore chlorine > bromine > iodine.

                                          2. Talk · 1 min.
                                          3. Write · 2 min. Watch for "bromine is lower in the group, so it is more reactive", the metal pattern applied to halogens.

                                              2. So a metal… and a halogen…
