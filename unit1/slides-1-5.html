Driving question · Textbook 1.5 opener

    Two cans in water

    What the teacher did

    A teacher set two unopened cans of soda in a tank of water: one regular, one sugar-free.

    The cans are the same size. Each holds 12 fl oz (355 mL).

    What happened

    The regular can sank to the bottom. The sugar-free can floated, with its top just above the water.

    This is the usual result.

    Our question for this lesson

    Why does one can of soda sink while the other floats?

        Density · Textbook §1.5.1

          Mass per volume

          Density = mass ÷ volume

          Solids in g/cm³, liquids in g/mL. 1 mL is exactly 1 cm³.

          A cube of 1.00 cm³ has a mass of 2.70 g if aluminum, 7.87 g if iron, 19.3 g if gold.

          A copper block

          2.00 × 2.00 × 1.50 = 6.00 cm³. Mass 53.76 g.
          53.76 ÷ 6.00 = 8.96 g/cm³

          1. Rule · 2 min. Ask which has more mass, a kilogram of feathers or a kilogram of iron. Then ask which takes more space. Safety: no equipment on this slide.
          2. Example · 2 min. Three significant figures, like the lengths.
          3. Check · 1 min. Wrong path: volume ÷ mass, the inverted ratio.

          Density · Textbook §1.5.1

            Volume by water displacement

            The volume is the rise

            Final reading minus starting reading. Slide the object down a tilted cylinder so no water splashes out.

            1. Lower · 3 min. Read 30.0 mL, lower the iron bolt in, read 42.6 mL. Repeat with the other two objects. This settles prediction 2.
            2. Rule · 1 min. Safety: goggles on; tilt the cylinder and let the object slide down the wall, so the glass does not crack.
            3. Check · 1 min. Wrong path: 42.6 cm³, the final reading, which includes the water.

            Density · Textbook §1.5.1

              Reporting a density

              Iron bolt

              99.2 g ÷ 12.6 cm³ = 7.873…
              Both have three figures: 7.87 g/cm³.

              Quartz pebble

              Rise 28.0 − 20.0 = 8.0 mL, only two figures.
              21.4 ÷ 8.0 = 2.675, reported as 2.7 g/cm³.

              1. Three figures · 1 min. The rule for quotients from Lesson 1.4: the fewest significant figures.
              2. Two figures · 1 min. A subtraction keeps decimal places, so a small rise can lose figures. Safety: no equipment on this slide.
              3. Check · 1 min. Wrong paths: 7.873, which keeps an unmeasured digit, and 7.9, which drops a measured one.

              Graphs · Textbook §1.5.2

                Graphing mass against volume

                The slope is the density

                Larger samples sit farther along the same line. Read the slope from two points on the line: 27.0 g ÷ 10.0 cm³ = 2.70 g/cm³.

                1. Plot · 3 min. Add the five aluminum samples one at a time. Each message gives that sample's own density, about 2.7. The data are written for this lesson.
                2. Slope · 2 min. This settles prediction 1: size moves a point along the line but does not change the slope. Safety: no equipment on this slide.
                3. Check · 1 min. Wrong path: "the bigger one is denser", which confuses mass with density.

                Graphs · Textbook §1.5.2

                  Comparing two lines

                  Steeper means denser

                  Each cubic centimeter of iron has more mass than one of aluminum, so the iron line climbs faster: 7.87 against 2.70 g/cm³.

                  1. Plot · 2 min. Add the samples and draw both lines.
                  2. Compare · 1 min. Ask where a third metal, copper at 8.96 g/cm³, would fall. Safety: no equipment on this slide.
                  3. Check · 1 min. Wrong path: "a larger sample", which moves along a line rather than steepening it.

                  Identifying · Textbook §1.5.3

                    Identifying a substance

                    Precise enough to decide

                    Taking ±1 in the last digit, 9.0 g/cm³ fits nickel (8.90) and copper (8.96), but 8.96 fits copper only.

                    1. Compare · 2 min. Show sample A at two figures, then at three. Then sample C: gold and tungsten share 19.3 g/cm³, which the case file returns to.
                    2. Precision · 1 min. The band is one unit in the last place each way: a classroom rule, not a full uncertainty estimate, which would also weigh calibration and repeat trials. Safety: no equipment on this slide.
                    3. Check · 1 min. Wrong paths: iron and copper, both far from 2.70.

                    Floating · Textbook §1.5.4

                      Why the regular can sinks

                      The sugar makes the difference

                      Regular: about 370 g of drink, 36.8 g of it sugar, about 1.04 g/mL. Sugar-free: about 355 g, about 1.00 g/mL.

                      1. Test · 2 min. Put the cans in the water and turn the view. This settles prediction 3 and answers the driving question. Safety: unopened cans only, and dry any spills.
                      2. Explain · 2 min. USDA FoodData Central gives the masses per 12 fl oz can. The can's aluminum and the gas at the top also count, and they vary, so some cans of regular soda float.
                      3. Check · 1 min. Wrong path: the gas, which adds volume, not mass.

                      Floating · Textbook §1.5.4

                        Floating and sinking

                        Compare densities

                        Denser than the liquid: it sinks. Less dense: it floats.

                        Overall density

                        A steel ship floats because its hull holds air. A can's overall density counts the drink, the aluminum and the gas at the top.

                        1. Rule · 1 min. Ask for objects that float in water and ones that sink. Safety: no equipment on this slide.
                        2. Overall · 2 min. Ask why a heavy ship floats while a small steel bolt sinks.
                        3. Check · 1 min. Wrong path: "it dissolves", which is a different property.

                        Floating · Textbook §1.5.4

                          Density and temperature

                          Water's density

                          TemperatureDensity (g/mL)
                          4 °C1.0000
                          20 °C0.9982
                          40 °C0.9922
                          80 °C0.9718

                          Tables state a temperature

                          Warming expands most substances. The mass stays the same, so the density falls.

                          50.0 g of water fills 50.1 mL at 20 °C and 51.5 mL at 80 °C.

                          1. Data · 2 min. Ask in which significant figure the change first shows. Values from the CRC Handbook's table of water density. Safety: no equipment on this slide.
                          2. Why · 1 min. Water is densest near 4 °C. That is why a reference value always gives its temperature.

                          History · Textbook §1.5.1 · C.4(B)

                            A story about a crown

                            The story

                            Archimedes checked whether a king's crown was pure gold. He compared the water spilled by the crown and by a lump of gold of the same mass.

                            The oldest surviving telling is Vitruvius, De architectura, Book 9, written about two centuries later.

                            What we can trust

                            The idea is sound: equal masses of gold and silver fill different volumes.

                            The details are a story. For a 1000 g crown, the extra spill is too small to measure reliably.

                            1. Story · 2 min. 1000 g of gold fills 51.8 cm³; the same mass of silver fills 95.2 cm³. Safety: no equipment on this slide.
                            2. Doubt · 1 min. Nobody recorded what Archimedes did. Rorres (New York University) works out why the overflow method would be hard to carry out accurately.

                              Checkpoint · Question 1 · Objective 1.5.1

                                A stone has a mass of 58.4 g. Lowered into a cylinder, it raises the water from 25.0 mL to 47.2 mL. Find the stone's density. Explain each step.

                                0:30

                                Explain your idea to your partner, then switch.

                                1:00

                                Write your answer in your notebook. Use these sentence starters:

                                1. The stone's volume is…
                                2. Its density is…

                                Success criteria

                                Sample answer

                                The stone's volume is the rise in the water, 47.2 − 25.0 = 22.2 mL, which is 22.2 cm³. Its density is 58.4 g ÷ 22.2 cm³ = 2.63 g/cm³. I kept three significant figures because the mass and the volume both have three.

                                1. Think · 30 s.
                                2. Talk · 1 min. Listen for "the rise".
                                3. Write · 2 min. Sentence starters support ESL students.
                                4. Self-check · 30 s.
                                5. Compare · 1 min. Wrong paths: 1.24 g/cm³, which divides by the final reading, and 0.380, which inverts the ratio. Safety, if done with a real stone: slide it down a tilted cylinder.

                                Checkpoint · Question 2 · Objective 1.5.3

                                  A metal's density is measured as 9.0 g/cm³. Nickel is 8.90 and copper 8.96 g/cm³. Which metal is it? Explain what would decide it.

                                  Think silently. Plan what you will say.

                                  0:30

                                  Explain your idea to your partner, then switch.

                                  1:00

                                  Write your answer in your notebook. Use these sentence starters:

                                  1. The measurement has… significant figures, so…
                                  2. Both metals…
                                  3. To decide, I would need…
                                  Word bank
                                  reference valuesignificant figurespreciseuncertaintythird figure

                                  Success criteria

                                  Sample answer

                                  It cannot be decided yet. The measurement has two significant figures, so with our rule of ±1 in the last digit it could be anything from 8.9 to 9.1. Both nickel and copper fall in that range. To decide, I would need the density to three significant figures, because the two reference values first differ in the third figure.

                                  1. Think · 30 s.
                                  2. Talk · 1 min.
                                  3. Write · 2 min. Wrong path: "copper, because 8.96 rounds to 9.0", which forgets that nickel's 8.90 also lies within 0.1 of 9.0.
                                  4. Self-check · 30 s.

                                  Checkpoint · Question 3 · Objective 1.5.4

                                    Regular soda is about 1.04 g/mL and sugar-free about 1.00 g/mL. Explain why the regular can usually sinks, and why its label cannot promise it.

                                    Think silently. Plan what you will say.

                                    0:30

                                    Explain your idea to your partner, then switch.

                                    1:00

                                    Write your answer in your notebook. Use these sentence starters:

                                    1. The sugar adds…
                                    2. So the regular can is…
                                    3. A label cannot decide it because…
                                    Word bank
                                    densitydissolved sugaroverall densityaluminumgas

                                    Success criteria

                                    Sample answer

                                    Sugar is denser than water, so the dissolved sugar adds more mass than volume, and the regular soda is denser than water, about 1.04 g/mL. So the regular can is usually denser overall than water, and it sinks. A label cannot decide it because the can's overall density also counts the aluminum and the gas at the top, which vary from can to can and are not on the label.

                                    1. Think · 30 s.
                                    2. Talk · 1 min.
                                    3. Write · 2 min. Wrong path: "the regular can holds more soda", when both hold 355 mL.
                                    4. Self-check · 30 s.
                                    5. Compare · 1 min. Safety, if repeated live: unopened cans only, and dry any spills.

                                      Wrap-up · Exit ticket · C.3(A)

                                        Answer the driving question

                                        Why does one can of soda sink while the other floats? Use the densities as evidence.

                                        Word bank
                                        densitymassvolumedissolved sugaroverall densityevidence

                                        Claim

                                        One sentence that answers the question.

                                        1. The regular can sinks because…

                                        Sample claim

                                        The regular can sinks because its overall density is greater than water's, while the sugar-free can's is less.

                                        Evidence

                                        What the numbers show.

                                        1. The regular drink has…
                                        2. The sugar-free drink has…

                                        Sample evidence

                                        The regular drink has a mass of about 370 g in 355 mL, about 1.04 g/mL, with 36.8 g of sugar. The sugar-free drink has about 355 g in 355 mL, about 1.00 g/mL. The gas at the top of each can adds volume with almost no mass.

                                        Reasoning

                                        Explain with density.

                                        1. An object sinks when…
                                        2. The sugar…

                                        Sample reasoning

                                        An object sinks when it is denser than the liquid. Sugar is denser than water, so the dissolved sugar adds more mass than volume, which tips the regular can over water's density.

                                        Self-check: mark each part your answer meets, then compare with the sample.

                                        1. Claim · 2 min. One sentence that compares densities, not weights.
                                        2. Evidence · 3 min. Both masses and both densities.
                                        3. Reasoning · 3 min. Push for "the can is close to the line, so the aluminum and gas matter too".
                                        4. Self-check · 2 min. Score 0 to 2 per part.
