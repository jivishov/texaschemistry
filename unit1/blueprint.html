Practices, Measurement and Matter: unit blueprint

One set of objectives drives four surfaces: a student reading, a teacher slide deck, missions and a case file. Every check item carries an objective ID, so alignment to the practice standards C.1, C.2, C.3 and C.4 can be traced item by item.

Grade 10 on-level, Honors optional19 TAC §112.43(c), adopted 2020About 14 class periodsExact TEKS wording

1. The package

Twenty-one pages: six readings, six slide decks, six mission sets, one case file, this blueprint and the unit index. Each surface has one job, and none of them tests content that the reading does not teach.

ReadStudent reading in book or continuous mode, with a simpler-language toggle, worked examples, stop-and-check items and interactives.
TeachTeacher deck of 19 to 21 slides. Each slide shows its reading section, and the notes give timings, safety and the wrong answers to expect.
PracticeMissions. Classroom data and real measurements for each idea, with feedback on every wrong answer.
ApplyCase file. One decision about a fictional gold bar that needs the whole unit.

Teacher flow, one lesson

  1. Open the deck. The driving question and three predictions come first.
  2. Teach through the deck. Students follow the matching reading section or read it as homework.
  3. Students work the worked examples and stop-and-check items, then the missions.
  4. The CER exit ticket and the self-rating close the lesson. Students write in their notebooks.

Design rules

  • Every calculated number comes from one shared measurement calculator, which carries significant figures, exact factors and rounding, so the same value appears on every surface.
  • Rounding is half away from zero, stated once in Lesson 1.4 and used everywhere.
  • One objective ID per item. Every wrong choice and every common wrong number gets feedback that names the faulty step.
  • Writing happens in notebooks. Pages offer choices, numbers and reveals, never text boxes.
  • Every rule is shown on a reading from a drawn instrument, never as a bare list.

Where the measurement content comes from. "Significant figures", "density", "accuracy", "precision" and "dimensional analysis" are not words in the TEKS. They are how this course meets C.1(E) ("collect quantitative data using the International System of Units (SI)"), C.1(D) (balances, graduated cylinders, burettes), C.2(B) ("sources of error, and limitations") and C.2(C) ("mathematical calculations to assess quantitative relationships in data"). Each objective below is tagged with the practice it serves.

Matter goes a little beyond the TEKS. The 2021 Chemistry TEKS have no content standard for classifying matter or for physical and chemical change. The 2010 standards had one, §112.35(c)(4): "The student knows the characteristics of matter and can analyze the relationships between chemical and physical changes and properties", with (A) "differentiate between physical and chemical changes and properties" and (D) "classify matter as pure substances or mixtures through investigation of their properties". TEA's 2017-2021 Chemistry TEKS side-by-side compares the two versions. Unit 2 uses "element" and "compound" from its first lesson and Unit 3 uses "physical property" and "density", so Lesson 1.2 teaches this vocabulary briefly, in one lesson, through C.1(F), C.1(G) and C.2(A).

Boundaries. Atoms appear only as building blocks; what is inside them is C.6, Unit 2. Elements are named with their symbols, but the periodic table and its trends are C.5, Unit 3. Formulas are only ones a student can read as a count of atoms (H2O, CO2, NaCl); the rules are C.7, Unit 4. The mole is listed as an SI base unit, with "Unit 5" beside it. Chemical change is described in words and particle pictures, never as an equation (C.9, Unit 6). The kelvin appears once, as K = °C + 273.15; gas behavior is C.10, Unit 7. There is no molarity (C.11, Unit 8).

Evidence and model

The aquarium is a classroom scenario, not advice on animal care. Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
"The water is cloudy because bacteria are growing" is an observation.Nobody has seen the bacteria. The cloudiness is the observation; the bacteria are an inference that needs a test.
A scientific theory becomes a law once it has enough evidence.A law describes a pattern and a theory explains it. More evidence never turns one into the other.
A test can prove a hypothesis is true.The jar test supports the hypothesis. A later test could always find a case it does not explain.
A familiar chemical needs no safety equipment.Ethanol is highly flammable and causes serious eye irritation, and its Safety Data Sheet calls for eye protection.

5. Lesson 1.2 · Matter and its properties

Driving question. Seawater left in a dish gives back its salt, and a nail left in it rusts. How are these two changes different?

Objectives

Evidence and model

Two dishes of seawater, one with an iron nail, are the evidence: after eight days the first is dry with a salt crust, and the nail in the second is rusted. Seawater is about 3.5% dissolved salts by weight (NOAA). The model is a set of 3D particle boxes for elements, compounds and mixtures, with atoms as building blocks only; what is inside them is Unit 2's. Chemical change is told in words and particle pictures, never as an equation, and no single clue (bubbles, a color) proves one, since boiling also makes bubbles. Distillation is a diagram, not a class activity.

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
Seawater is a pure substance because it looks the same all the way through.It holds water and several salts in amounts that vary. It is a homogeneous mixture.
The salt left when seawater dries is a new substance.The salt was dissolved all along. Evaporation is a physical change.
Bubbles always mean a chemical change.Boiling water makes bubbles of water vapor, which is still water.
A particle model shows atoms at their real size and color.Atoms are far too small to see, a sample holds vastly more of them, and the colors are a code.

6. Lesson 1.3 · SI units and dimensional analysis

Driving question. On September 23, 1999, Mars Climate Orbiter reached Mars and was never heard from again. How could numbers given in the wrong unit send a spacecraft too close to Mars?

Objectives

IDStudents willTEKSSlides
1.3.1Name the SI base units for length, mass, time and temperature, build derived units for volume and density, and use the prefixes from nano- to kilo-.C.1(E)5 to 7
1.3.2Write very large and very small measurements in scientific notation, and read the coefficient and exponent.C.2(C)8
1.3.3Convert a measurement with conversion factors written as fractions, in one step or several, including squared and cubed units and a rate with two units.C.2(C)9 to 11
1.3.4Use the investigation board's evidence to explain how a mix-up of units led to the loss of Mars Climate Orbiter, and what check would have caught it.C.4(A)12, 13

Evidence and model

NASA's Mishap Investigation Board Phase I report (November 10, 1999) is the evidence. A ground software file gave thruster impulse in pound-force seconds where the interface specification required newton-seconds, so the velocity changes used by navigation were low by a factor of 4.45. The first pass was planned at 226 km; estimates made afterwards put it at about 57 km, below the 80 km the board gives as the lowest survivable altitude. The pages say the spacecraft was destroyed in the atmosphere or left Mars on a solar orbit, as the report does, and name no company or person. Units follow the BIPM SI Brochure (9th edition), including the 2019 redefinition in one sentence, and exact factors follow NIST SP 811. Worked examples move from watched to partly done to independent.

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
The gram is the SI base unit of mass.The base unit is the kilogram, the only base unit with a prefix in its name.
Multiplying by a conversion factor changes the amount.A conversion factor equals 1, so it changes the unit but not the amount.
1 m³ is 100 cm³.The centimeter cubes fill a cubic decimeter: a cubed unit needs the factor cubed, so 1 m³ = 10⁶ cm³.
A negative exponent means a negative number.It means a number smaller than 1, such as 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ m.
A unit is only a label.25 lbf·s and 25 N·s are different amounts, as the orbiter showed.

7. Lesson 1.4 · Measurement and significant figures

Driving question. Water drained from a burette reads 24.62 mL; in a graduated cylinder it reads 24.6 mL and in a beaker 25 mL. Why does the same water get three readings with different numbers of digits?

Objectives

IDStudents willTEKSSlides
1.4.1Read an analog scale to one estimated digit past its finest mark, read a meniscus at eye level, and record a digital display as shown, using a balance, a graduated cylinder and a burette.C.1(D)5 to 8
1.4.2Count the significant figures in a measurement, including zeros, scientific notation and exact numbers.C.1(E)9, 10
1.4.3Round a calculated result by the rule for its operation: decimal places for adding and subtracting, significant figures for multiplying and dividing, and only at the last step.C.2(C)11 to 14
1.4.4Explain a measurement's uncertainty from the resolution of the instrument that made it, and choose the instrument a task needs.C.2(B)5, 6

Evidence and model

The three instruments are drawn in 3D with their real graduations, and every rule is shown on a reading taken from them. Significant figures are the record of a reading's resolution: every marked digit plus one estimated digit. Rounding is half away from zero, stated once here and used in every later unit's calculator. The burette's scatter follows the class A tolerance of ±0.05 mL (ASTM E287); the cylinder's and beaker's are illustrative, and the reading says so. A digital display is recorded as shown, and the balance scene shows taring.

Misconceptions this lesson targets

MisconceptionEvidence that corrects it
More digits on a calculator mean a more precise result.A result is no more precise than the measurements it came from.
Zeros are never significant.Zeros between digits and at the end of a decimal were measured. Only leading zeros never count.
A count, such as 3 washers, limits the significant figures of a result.A count is an exact number with unlimited significant figures.
A beaker and a burette give readings with the same uncertainty.The burette's finer marks give a reading with much less uncertainty.
Round each step as you go.Rounding at every step drifts; the worked examples carry every digit and round once at the end.

Evidence and model

The two cans are described from Purdue and University of Pennsylvania demonstration sheets, without brand names, and the scene shows the typical outcome: a can's overall density includes the aluminum and the gas above the drink, so the result is close to the line. The reason is shown on the liquids: USDA FoodData Central gives the sugar per can, and a 10% sucrose solution is 1.0381 g/cm³ at 20 °C, so the sugar adds mass with little added volume. The history box tells the Archimedes crown story as a story: it survives only in Vitruvius's De architectura, Book 9, written about two centuries later.

10. Case file: Checking a gold bar

Students act as analysts on a fictional assay team in Texas that checks metal bars before a vault accepts them for storage. A bar arrives with a certificate saying it is gold. Every test must leave the bar unharmed. The report answers one question: can the bar be accepted as gold, or does it need a further test?

Briefing

The memo explains the task and lists the six files with their TEKS, beside a 3D scene of the bar on a balance. A fact box gives the real context: Texas has its own depository for precious metals, the Texas Bullion Depository, run by the Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts; it opened in 2018 and is now in Leander. The Comptroller's 2018 release says, "Metals admitted to the depository will undergo three types of non-destructive testing, including a density scan to ensure purity." Nothing else of the depository's procedure is described, and the case never suggests it received a false bar. One troy ounce is exactly 31.1034768 g (NIST), and the CRC Handbook gives gold and tungsten the same density to three figures, 19.3 g/cm³. The team, the bar, its certificate and every reading are fictional, and the page says so.

Each file has three steps: a calculation or observation, the Unit 1 idea it uses, then a decision about a claim. Every number is computed by the shared measurement calculator, and every answer window leaves out each named slip.

File 1 · The question and the plan · C.1(A) C.1(C)

Students sort statements about the bar into observations and inferences, and choose tests that leave it unharmed (weighing, measuring, water displacement) over cutting or scratching it. A safety note covers handling a heavy bar.

File 2 · The certificate in SI units · C.1(E) C.2(C)

The certificate gives 32.150 troy ounces and 4.60 × 1.95 × 0.352 inches. Task: 999.98 g with the exact troy ounce, and 51.7 cm³ from the dimensions in centimeters. Decide why the exact factors do not limit the significant figures.

File 3 · Two ways to find the volume · C.1(D) C.2(B)

A millimeter ruler gives 11.68 cm × 4.95 cm × 0.89 cm, so 51 cm³ (the thickness has two significant figures), with a percent uncertainty of 1.4%. A 1000 mL cylinder goes from 500 mL to 552 mL, so 52 mL, with 3.8% from two readings each off by 1 mL. Decide which volume to trust more: the ruler's, for a box-shaped bar.

File 4 · The density · C.2(C) C.3(A)

Balance P reads 999.9 g. Task: 19 g/cm³ from each volume. A chart draws each result as a band as wide as its uncertainty against brass (8.53), silver (10.5), lead (11.3), gold and tungsten (19.3 g/cm³). Decide: the bar is not brass, silver or lead; it could be gold, or gold with tungsten inside.

File 5 · Which balance to trust · C.2(B) C.2(D)

Two balances weigh a certified 1000.0 g standard five times each. Balance P's mean is 1000.0 g (0.0% error); balance Q's readings agree just as closely, with a mean of 1001.5 g, 0.15% high. Decide: Q is precise but not accurate, a systematic error that calibration removes, so the bar's mass is read on P.

File 6 · What density cannot show · C.3(A) C.4(A)

A bar with a core filling 20% of its volume, the rest gold. Task: a lead core gives 17.7 g/cm³, outside both bands; a tungsten core gives 19.3 g/cm³, the same as pure gold. Decide what the density measurement can rule out and what it cannot.

Recommendation · claim, evidence, reasoning · C.3(A), C.3(C), C.4(B)

Students choose a claim (hold the bar for a further test that can tell its inside from its surface; accept it because its density matches gold's; accept it because its mass matches the certificate; or reject it because the two volumes disagree), select the evidence files, and mark what the files cannot show. The vault manager asks what a wrong decision costs either way: accepting a false bar stores far less gold than the certificate claims, and holding a real bar only delays it. Students then write the reasoning in their notebooks using sentence starters and a word bank. The check expects Files 4 and 6 among the evidence, and two limits: whether tungsten is hidden inside, and whether a small lead core (under about 2% of the volume) is inside. It rejects the bar's mass and "mostly lead" as limits, with feedback naming the file that settles each. After a successful check, a sample report appears for comparison.

Grading the notebook. A strong report converts the certificate, compares the two volumes with their uncertainties, rules out the lighter metals with the density bands, trusts balance P because it matches the standard, and names the limit of density: it cannot tell gold from gold around a tungsten core. A report that accepts the bar because 19 g/cm³ "matches gold" has not used File 6. The further test is left unnamed on purpose; no standards or primary technical source was found that describes one for this use.

Case-file boundary. The assay team, the bar, its certificate and all readings are fictional; the depository, its 2018 testing sentence, the troy ounce and the reference densities are real and cited. The displacement starts at 500 mL so the 11.7 cm bar is fully under water in a 1000 mL cylinder. Nothing in the case is done in class with a real gold bar; the safety notes cover lowering a heavy object into glassware on a string, in a tray.

12. Using the results

What the pages record

  • Each mission set shows the cases solved and the first-try accuracy, so a first answer can be told apart from eventual completion.
  • Numeric answers are accepted within about ±0.5%, never less than half a unit in the last place; the reference values stay fixed. Each common wrong number (a factor upside down, a squared unit converted once, rounding too early, the final level used as a volume, percent error divided by the measured value) gets its own feedback, and a test checks that none of them falls inside the tolerance.
  • Progress is saved in the student's own browser only. Nothing is sent to a server, so the pages are not a gradebook.

What they do not show

  • A correct number does not prove a correct setup. Ask for the factor chain written in the notebook, with units that cancel.
  • A correct significant-figure count does not prove a correct reading. Check readings taken on real glassware.
  • Judge explanations from notebooks and discussion: the exit tickets, the checkpoints and the case file report.
  • A completed self-rating is a reflection, not proof of mastery.

Each deck runs checkpoints as Think, Talk, Write, Self-check and Compare, with 30 second thinking and 60 second partner timers. The three predictions from slide 3 are revisited near the end of each deck, and the CER exit ticket returns to the driving question.

14. Data and accuracy notes

  • One shared calculator. A new kit file, the measurement calculator, carries significant figures through sums, differences, products and quotients, treats defined factors and counts as exact, converts units, and computes means, ranges, standard deviations and percent errors. It rounds half away from zero. Every number on every Unit 1 surface comes from it, and a test checks each lesson's values and each mission's answer window.
  • Exact factors. 1 in = 2.54 cm, 1 troy ounce = 31.1034768 g, 1 mL = 1 cm³ and the SI prefixes are exact (NIST SP 811; NIST Office of Weights and Measures; BIPM SI Brochure). The orbiter's 4.45 is the report's rounded 1 lbf = 4.448 221 615 260 5 N.
  • Reference densities. All from one table, CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics, near 20 °C: gold and tungsten 19.3, lead 11.3, silver 10.5, brass (alloy C26000) 8.53, aluminum 2.70 g/cm³. Water is 0.9982 g/mL at 20 °C.
  • Instrument tolerances. The class A burette's ±0.05 mL follows ASTM E287. The cylinder's and beaker's scatter in Lesson 1.4 is illustrative and labeled so.
  • Models and scale. Particle boxes show atoms as colored spheres; the caption says real particles are far smaller and far more numerous and the colors are a code. The glassware, balances, rulers and cans are drawn to scale with calculated readings and labeled as simulations.
  • Laboratory data. The aquarium log and jar test, the four balance groups and every case-file reading are fictional and say so.
  • History. Mars Climate Orbiter launched December 11, 1998 and was lost September 23, 1999; the board's Phase I report is dated November 10, 1999. The kilogram has been defined by the Planck constant since 2019. The Archimedes crown story is known only from Vitruvius, about two centuries after Archimedes. The Texas Bullion Depository's first release is dated June 6, 2018; its Leander building was completed in 2019.
